0. Motivation - from one-dimensional models to a continuum

This course has been quietly modelling tissues as one-dimensional objects. Module 2 treated a bone as an Euler-Bernoulli beam - a line with a bending stiffness. Module 4 treated cartilage as a stack of springs and dashpots squeezing fluid through a one-dimensional column. Module 6 treated a tendon as a nonlinear spring, a single force-length curve. Each reduction was deliberate and powerful, and each threw away the same thing: that a tissue is a three-dimensional body, deforming in every direction at once, with a stress that is not a single number but a state at each point.

A one-dimensional model cannot answer the questions that decide whether a tissue fails. When a bone is loaded off-axis, where is the stress highest across its cross-section? When a tendon is stretched along its fibres, how much does it bulge sideways, and why does that matter for its stiffness? When cartilage is pressed, how does the load partition between the solid matrix and the pressurised fluid, point by point? These are questions about fields - stress and strain that vary through the body's volume - and to answer them we need the language those fields are written in: continuum mechanics.

The plan runs from geometry to law to computation. We first make deformation precise - the displacement field, the deformation gradient, and the strain tensor that measures local stretching (Section 1). We then define stress as a tensor and impose equilibrium (Section 2), and connect the two with a constitutive law - linear elasticity and its strain energy (Section 3). With the physics in place, we turn to computation: the bar and beam elements that are the atoms of finite-element analysis (Section 4), and how they assemble into a global system solved for the stress field (Section 5). The last third makes the laws realistic for tissue: anisotropy and fibre reinforcement (Section 6), hyperelasticity at finite strain (Section 7), and the time-dependent viscoelastic and poroelastic behaviours (Section 8) that make bone a composite, tendon a fibre-reinforced solid, and cartilage a biphasic material (Section 9). Three labs and a problem set close it. The reward is that every idealisation of the earlier modules becomes a special case of one framework - and its limits become visible. On the course's modelling ladder (Level 0 scalar estimates through Level 10 multiscale mechanobiological adaptation) this module is Level 9, continuum and finite-element-style tissue models. Everything before it that touched a tissue sat lower: Module 2's beam and Module 6's tendon spring are Level 0 to 1 reductions of what is built here, and Module 4's biphasic column is this module's Section 8 in one dimension. Section 9 makes that relation exact by exhibiting each as a limit. What this module does not reach is Level 10: the material constants are fixed, and tissue that remodels its own $E$, $\nu$ and $\mathbf a$ in response to the stresses computed here is the adaptation loop of Module 2's mechanostat, left open in Section 9's table of idealisations.

1. Deformation: the displacement field, gradient, and strain

Continuum mechanics begins not with force but with geometry: a precise account of how a body changes shape. Label each material point by its position $\mathbf X$ in the undeformed (reference) body. When the body deforms, that point moves to a new position $\mathbf x$. The map $\mathbf x=\boldsymbol\chi(\mathbf X)$ is the deformation, and its local behaviour - how a tiny material fibre is stretched and rotated - is captured by a single object.

Definition 1.1 (displacement field and deformation gradient). The displacement of a material point is $\mathbf u(\mathbf X)=\mathbf x-\mathbf X$, the vector from where the point was to where it is. The deformation gradient is the Jacobian of the deformation map, $$\mathbf F=\frac{\partial\mathbf x}{\partial\mathbf X}=\mathbf I+\frac{\partial\mathbf u}{\partial\mathbf X},$$ a second-order tensor (in two dimensions a $2\times2$ matrix). It maps an infinitesimal material fibre $d\mathbf X$ in the reference body to its image $d\mathbf x=\mathbf F\,d\mathbf X$ in the deformed body, encoding the local stretch and rotation (Fig. 2). Its determinant $J=\det\mathbf F$ is the local volume ratio (deformed volume over reference volume); $J=1$ is an isochoric (volume-preserving) deformation.

The deformation gradient contains rotation as well as stretch, and rotation is not deformation - a rigid spin of the whole body strains nothing. To measure only the stretching, we form a quantity that is blind to rotation. The Green-Lagrange strain tensor does this exactly.

Definition 1.2 (strain). The Green-Lagrange strain is $$\mathbf E=\tfrac12\big(\mathbf F^{\!\top}\mathbf F-\mathbf I\big),$$ which vanishes for any rigid-body motion (where $\mathbf F$ is a pure rotation, $\mathbf F^{\!\top}\mathbf F=\mathbf I$) and measures the change in squared length of material fibres. When displacements and their gradients are small - the regime of bone and of small tissue strains - the quadratic term is negligible and $\mathbf E$ reduces to the infinitesimal (small) strain tensor $$\boldsymbol\varepsilon=\tfrac12\big(\nabla\mathbf u+\nabla\mathbf u^{\!\top}\big),\qquad \varepsilon_{ij}=\tfrac12\Big(\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial X_j}+\frac{\partial u_j}{\partial X_i}\Big),$$ whose diagonal entries $\varepsilon_{11},\varepsilon_{22},\varepsilon_{33}$ are the fractional stretches along each axis (the familiar $\Delta L/L$) and whose off-diagonal entries are the shear strains.
Proposition 1.1 (small strain is the linearisation of Green-Lagrange strain). For small displacement gradients $\nabla\mathbf u$, the Green-Lagrange strain equals the infinitesimal strain to first order: $$\boxed{\;\mathbf E=\boldsymbol\varepsilon+O\big(\lVert\nabla\mathbf u\rVert^2\big),\qquad \boldsymbol\varepsilon=\tfrac12\big(\nabla\mathbf u+\nabla\mathbf u^{\!\top}\big).\;}$$
Write $\mathbf F=\mathbf I+\mathbf H$ with $\mathbf H=\nabla\mathbf u$ the displacement gradient. Then $$\mathbf F^{\!\top}\mathbf F=(\mathbf I+\mathbf H)^{\!\top}(\mathbf I+\mathbf H)=\mathbf I+\mathbf H+\mathbf H^{\!\top}+\mathbf H^{\!\top}\mathbf H,$$ so $\mathbf E=\tfrac12(\mathbf F^{\!\top}\mathbf F-\mathbf I)=\tfrac12(\mathbf H+\mathbf H^{\!\top})+\tfrac12\mathbf H^{\!\top}\mathbf H$. The first term is exactly $\boldsymbol\varepsilon$; the second is quadratic in $\mathbf H=\nabla\mathbf u$ and is dropped when $\lVert\nabla\mathbf u\rVert\ll1$. Hence $\mathbf E=\boldsymbol\varepsilon+O(\lVert\nabla\mathbf u\rVert^2)$. The linear theory of Section 3 uses $\boldsymbol\varepsilon$; the finite-strain theory of Section 7 keeps the full $\mathbf E$. ∎

A concrete case anchors the tensor. Under a uniaxial stretch of ratio $\lambda$ along $X_1$ with lateral contraction ratio governed by a material constant $\nu$, Poisson's ratio, defined properly in Section 3 and used here only as the name for the ratio of transverse to axial strain, the deformation is $x_1=\lambda X_1$, $x_2=(1-\nu(\lambda-1))X_2$, so $\mathbf F=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda,\,1-\nu(\lambda-1))$ and, for small $\lambda-1$, the axial small strain is $\varepsilon_{11}=\lambda-1$ and the transverse strain is $\varepsilon_{22}=-\nu\varepsilon_{11}$ - the sideways bulge of a stretched tendon quantified. This is the geometry the next section loads.

2. Stress: traction and the Cauchy stress tensor

Deformation is geometry; stress is force. Cut a loaded body across an internal surface and the two sides push on each other. The force per unit area transmitted across that cut is the traction, and its value depends on the orientation of the cut - a fact that forces stress, like strain, to be a tensor rather than a number.

Definition 2.1 (traction). At a point, imagine a small planar cut with unit normal $\mathbf n$. The material on the positive-$\mathbf n$ side exerts on the other side a force per unit area, the traction $\mathbf t(\mathbf n)$. Write $\mathbf t^{(j)}=\mathbf t(\mathbf e_j)$ for the traction on the cut whose normal is the $j$-th coordinate axis, and collect the components into the array $\sigma_{ij}=t^{(j)}_i$: the $i$-th force component per unit area on the face whose normal points along $j$. The diagonal entries are normal stresses (tension and compression), the off-diagonal entries shear stresses. Nothing so far says these nine numbers determine the traction on any other cut; that is the content of the next result.
Proposition 2.1 (Cauchy's theorem). The traction depends linearly on the cut normal, so the nine components $\sigma_{ij}$ of Definition 2.1 form a second-order tensor $\boldsymbol\sigma$, the Cauchy stress, and $$\boxed{\;\mathbf t(\mathbf n)=\boldsymbol\sigma\,\mathbf n,\qquad t_i=\sigma_{ij}n_j\;}$$ for every unit normal $\mathbf n$.
Take the smallest setting that shows the mechanism: a tetrahedron with three faces normal to the coordinate axes and one oblique face of area $A$ with outward normal $\mathbf n$, all four meeting at the point, with the tetrahedron's longest edge $h$. The three axis faces are the projections of the oblique one, so the face normal to $\mathbf e_j$ has area $An_j$, and the enclosed volume is $\tfrac13Ah'$ for some height $h'\le h$. Newton's second law for the tetrahedron reads $$\mathbf t(\mathbf n)\,A-\sum_j\mathbf t^{(j)}An_j+\mathbf b\,V=\rho V\ddot{\mathbf x},$$ the minus sign because the outward normals of the axis faces point along $-\mathbf e_j$. Every surface term scales as $A\sim h^2$ and every volume term as $V\sim h^3$. Divide by $A$ and let $h\to0$: the body-force and inertia terms carry a surviving factor $V/A\sim h\to0$ and drop out, whatever the acceleration, leaving $\mathbf t(\mathbf n)=\sum_j\mathbf t^{(j)}n_j$. In components that is $t_i=\sigma_{ij}n_j$, which is linear in $\mathbf n$. Linearity is what makes $\sigma_{ij}$ a tensor rather than nine unrelated numbers: it transforms under a change of axes as the matrix of a linear map must, because it is the matrix of the map $\mathbf n\mapsto\mathbf t$. ∎
Proposition 2.2 (the stress tensor is symmetric). Balance of angular momentum forces $$\boxed{\;\boldsymbol\sigma=\boldsymbol\sigma^{\!\top},\qquad \sigma_{ij}=\sigma_{ji},\;}$$ so of its nine components only six are independent - three normal and three shear.
Take a material cube of side $h$ centred at the point and sum moments about its centre. A shear stress $\sigma_{ij}$ ($i\neq j$) acts on the two faces normal to $j$ with opposite sign, forming a couple of moment $\sim\sigma_{ij}h^2\cdot h=\sigma_{ij}h^3$ about the axis perpendicular to the $i$-$j$ plane; the complementary shear $\sigma_{ji}$ forms an opposing couple $\sim\sigma_{ji}h^3$. The body's rotational inertia scales as $\rho h^5$ and its angular acceleration is finite, so the net moment must vanish faster than $h^3$: dividing the moment balance by $h^3$ and letting $h\to0$ leaves $\sigma_{ij}-\sigma_{ji}=0$. Hence $\boldsymbol\sigma$ is symmetric. ∎

Stress must also balance force. For a body in static equilibrium under a body force per unit volume $\mathbf b$ (gravity, say), take a small cube: the two faces normal to direction $j$ carry tractions differing by a first-order Taylor step, so their net force per unit volume in direction $i$ is $[\sigma_{ij}(x_j+dx_j)-\sigma_{ij}(x_j)]/dx_j\to\partial\sigma_{ij}/\partial x_j$. Summing over the three face-pairs and adding the body force $b_i$, force balance requires the equilibrium equation

Equilibrium. $$\nabla\!\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma+\mathbf b=\mathbf 0,\qquad \frac{\partial\sigma_{ij}}{\partial x_j}+b_i=0,$$

three scalar equations (one per direction) that the stress field must satisfy at every interior point, with the surface traction $\boldsymbol\sigma\mathbf n$ matching the applied load on the boundary. Equilibrium, the strain-displacement relation of Section 1, and the constitutive law of Section 3 together close the system: three ingredients - kinematics, balance, and material law - determine the fields. Two scalars extracted from $\boldsymbol\sigma$ recur in tissue failure, and both are unchanged by a rotation of the axes, so they are properties of the stress state and not of the coordinates chosen to write it. The principal stresses $\sigma_1\ge\sigma_2\ge\sigma_3$ are the eigenvalues of $\boldsymbol\sigma$, the extreme normal stresses over all cut orientations (the eigenvectors give the cuts that carry no shear), and $\sigma_1$ decides brittle fracture in bone, which parts on the plane of greatest tension. The von Mises stress is built from the deviatoric part $\mathbf s=\boldsymbol\sigma-\tfrac13(\operatorname{tr}\boldsymbol\sigma)\mathbf I$, the shape-changing part left after the pressure is removed, $$\sigma_{\rm vM}=\sqrt{\tfrac32\,\mathbf s:\mathbf s}=\sqrt{\tfrac12\big[(\sigma_1-\sigma_2)^2+(\sigma_2-\sigma_3)^2+(\sigma_3-\sigma_1)^2\big]},$$ normalised so that a bar in uniaxial tension $\sigma$ has $\sigma_{\rm vM}=\sigma$. It governs ductile yield, which is a shearing process: a state of pure pressure has $\mathbf s=\mathbf 0$ and $\sigma_{\rm vM}=0$ and cannot yield a metal however large the pressure, which is why deep-sea organisms are not crushed by hydrostatic load but are torn by a shear of a hundredth the magnitude.

3. Linear elasticity and the strain-energy function

Kinematics gave strain, balance gave stress; a constitutive law ties them together, encoding what the material is. The simplest - accurate for bone and for small strains of any tissue - is linear elasticity: stress proportional to strain, through a fourth-order stiffness tensor.

Definition 3.1 (generalised Hooke's law; isotropic elasticity). Linear elasticity sets $\sigma_{ij}=C_{ijkl}\,\varepsilon_{kl}$, with $\mathbf C$ the stiffness tensor. For an isotropic material - no preferred direction, the case for bone matrix at the tissue scale - $\mathbf C$ reduces to two constants, and $$\boxed{\;\boldsymbol\sigma=\lambda\,(\operatorname{tr}\boldsymbol\varepsilon)\,\mathbf I+2\mu\,\boldsymbol\varepsilon,\;}$$ with $\lambda,\mu$ the Lamé constants ($\mu$ the shear modulus; this $\lambda$ is the Lamé constant, distinct from the stretch ratio $\lambda$ of Section 1). Equivalently the response is set by Young's modulus $E$ and Poisson's ratio $\nu$: for uniaxial stress $\sigma$, the axial strain is $\varepsilon=\sigma/E$ and the transverse strain is $-\nu\varepsilon$, with $\mu=\dfrac{E}{2(1+\nu)}$ and $\lambda=\dfrac{E\nu}{(1+\nu)(1-2\nu)}$.
Proposition 3.1 (stored energy and its positivity). A linear-elastic material stores a strain-energy density $$\boxed{\;W=\tfrac12\,\boldsymbol\sigma:\boldsymbol\varepsilon=\tfrac12\,\varepsilon_{ij}C_{ijkl}\varepsilon_{kl},\;}$$ the work per unit volume done in straining it, and stress is its gradient, $\sigma_{ij}=\partial W/\partial\varepsilon_{ij}$. Stability of the material requires $W\gt0$ for every non-zero strain, which for the isotropic law holds iff $\mu\gt0$ and the bulk modulus $K=\lambda+\tfrac23\mu\gt0$ (equivalently $E\gt0$ and $-1\lt\nu\lt\tfrac12$).
Load the element quasi-statically along a straight path $\boldsymbol\varepsilon(s)=s\,\bar{\boldsymbol\varepsilon}$, $s:0\to1$. The stress is $\boldsymbol\sigma(s)=s\,\mathbf C:\bar{\boldsymbol\varepsilon}$, and the work per volume is $W=\int_0^1\boldsymbol\sigma:d\boldsymbol\varepsilon=\int_0^1 s\,(\mathbf C:\bar{\boldsymbol\varepsilon}):\bar{\boldsymbol\varepsilon}\,ds=\tfrac12\bar{\boldsymbol\varepsilon}:\mathbf C:\bar{\boldsymbol\varepsilon}=\tfrac12\boldsymbol\sigma:\boldsymbol\varepsilon$, and differentiating $W=\tfrac12\varepsilon_{ij}C_{ijkl}\varepsilon_{kl}$ (with $\mathbf C$ symmetric) gives $\partial W/\partial\varepsilon_{ij}=C_{ijkl}\varepsilon_{kl}=\sigma_{ij}$. Splitting any strain into a volumetric part $\tfrac13(\operatorname{tr}\boldsymbol\varepsilon)\mathbf I$ and a deviatoric (shape-changing) part $\mathbf e$, the isotropic energy separates as $W=\tfrac12 K(\operatorname{tr}\boldsymbol\varepsilon)^2+\mu\,\mathbf e:\mathbf e$; both terms are non-negative for all strains iff $K\gt0$ and $\mu\gt0$, and each vanishes only at zero strain, giving positive-definiteness. ∎

The two constants span an enormous range across the tissues of this course, which is why their mechanical roles differ so sharply.

But three of these four tissues are poorly served by the isotropic, linear, time-independent law: tendon is strongly anisotropic (stiff along fibres, soft across), tendon and cartilage are nonlinear and time-dependent, and cartilage is biphasic. The rest of the module builds those laws - but first, the linear law is enough to make the continuum computable, which is the business of the finite element.

4. The bar and beam elements: finite-element intuition

The continuum equations of the last three sections - strain from displacement, stress from strain, equilibrium of stress - are partial differential equations, solvable in closed form only for simple geometries. The finite-element method makes them computable for any shape by a single idea: chop the body into small pieces (elements), assume a simple displacement variation within each, and require equilibrium of the pieces. The atom of the method is the bar element, and it is nothing more than Hooke's law for a rod.

Definition 4.1 (the axial bar element). A straight rod of length $L$, cross-section $A$, and modulus $E$ carries axial displacements $u_1,u_2$ at its two end nodes, with axial forces $f_1,f_2$. Assuming the displacement varies linearly along the rod, the strain is the constant $\varepsilon=(u_2-u_1)/L$, the stress $\sigma=E\varepsilon$, and the internal force $N=\sigma A=\dfrac{EA}{L}(u_2-u_1)$. Force balance at the nodes gives the element stiffness relation $$\boxed{\;\begin{pmatrix}f_1\\ f_2\end{pmatrix}=\frac{EA}{L}\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\-1&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}u_1\\ u_2\end{pmatrix}=\mathbf k_e\,\mathbf u_e,\;}$$ with $k=EA/L$ the axial stiffness (the spring constant of Modules 2 and 6, here derived from the continuum).
Proposition 4.1 (the element matrix is the Hessian of its strain energy). The bar element's stiffness matrix $\mathbf k_e$ is symmetric positive-semidefinite, and equals the second derivative of the element strain energy $W_e=\tfrac12\,\dfrac{EA}{L}(u_2-u_1)^2$ with respect to the nodal displacements; its single zero eigenvalue is the rigid translation $u_1=u_2$.
The stored energy is the strain-energy density $\tfrac12 E\varepsilon^2$ times the volume $AL$: $W_e=\tfrac12 E\big(\tfrac{u_2-u_1}{L}\big)^2 AL=\tfrac12\tfrac{EA}{L}(u_2-u_1)^2$. Its gradient gives the nodal forces, $f_1=\partial W_e/\partial u_1=-\tfrac{EA}{L}(u_2-u_1)$, $f_2=\partial W_e/\partial u_2=+\tfrac{EA}{L}(u_2-u_1)$, which is the boxed relation, and its Hessian $\partial^2 W_e/\partial u_i\partial u_j=\tfrac{EA}{L}\big(\begin{smallmatrix}1&-1\\-1&1\end{smallmatrix}\big)=\mathbf k_e$. This matrix is symmetric with eigenvalues $0$ (eigenvector $(1,1)$, a rigid shift that strains nothing) and $2EA/L$ (eigenvector $(1,-1)$, pure stretch), hence positive-semidefinite. The zero eigenvalue is why a structure needs a support: without a fixed node, the assembled global stiffness $\mathbf K$ (Section 5) is singular and the displacement is undetermined up to a rigid motion. ∎

Bending needs one more element. A beam element resists transverse deflection, and because bending couples deflection to rotation (Module 2's Euler-Bernoulli theory), each node carries two degrees of freedom - a transverse displacement $w$ and a slope $\theta=dw/dx$ - so the element stiffness is a $4\times4$ matrix built from the bending stiffness $EI$ ($I$ the cross-section's second moment of area, Module 2). It follows from the same energy argument as the bar of Fig. 5.

Proposition 4.2 (the beam element matrix). Let the transverse displacement of a beam element of length $L$ and bending stiffness $EI$ be interpolated from its four nodal degrees of freedom $\mathbf u_e=(w_1,\theta_1,w_2,\theta_2)$ by the cubic Hermite shape functions $N_i(x)$, the unique cubics with $N_i$ taking the value one for its own degree of freedom and zero for the other three. Then the element stiffness is the Hessian of its bending energy, $$\boxed{\;\mathbf k_{\rm beam}=\frac{EI}{L^3}\begin{pmatrix}12&6L&-12&6L\\6L&4L^2&-6L&2L^2\\-12&-6L&12&-6L\\6L&2L^2&-6L&4L^2\end{pmatrix},\qquad (\mathbf k_{\rm beam})_{ij}=\int_0^L EI\,N_i''N_j''\,dx,\;}$$ symmetric positive-semidefinite with a two-dimensional null space: rigid translation and rigid rotation.
Module 2's Euler-Bernoulli theory gives the bending energy of a beam as $W_e=\tfrac12\int_0^L EI\,(w'')^2dx$, the curvature $w''$ playing the role the axial strain played in Proposition 4.1. Writing $w(x)=\sum_i N_i(x)\,u_{e,i}$ makes $w''=\sum_iN_i''u_{e,i}$, so $$W_e=\tfrac12\sum_{i,j}u_{e,i}\Big(\int_0^LEI\,N_i''N_j''\,dx\Big)u_{e,j}=\tfrac12\,\mathbf u_e^{\!\top}\mathbf k_e\mathbf u_e,$$ and the Hessian $\partial^2W_e/\partial u_{e,i}\partial u_{e,j}$ is the bracket, which is symmetric by inspection. With $s=x/L$ the four Hermite cubics are $N_1=1-3s^2+2s^3$, $N_2=L(s-2s^2+s^3)$, $N_3=3s^2-2s^3$, $N_4=L(-s^2+s^3)$; each takes the value one for its own nodal quantity and zero for the other three, which is what makes $\mathbf u_e$ the nodal displacements and slopes. Take the first column as the worked case. $N_1''=(-6+12s)/L^2$, so $$(\mathbf k_{\rm beam})_{11}=\int_0^L\frac{EI}{L^4}(12s-6)^2dx=\frac{EI}{L^3}\int_0^1(12s-6)^2ds=\frac{EI}{L^3}\Big[\frac{(12s-6)^3}{36}\Big]_0^1=\frac{12EI}{L^3},$$ and with $N_2''=(-4+6s)/L$, $$(\mathbf k_{\rm beam})_{12}=\int_0^L\frac{EI}{L^3}(12s-6)(6s-4)\,dx=\frac{EI}{L^2}\int_0^1(72s^2-84s+24)\,ds=\frac{EI}{L^2}(24-42+24)=\frac{6EI}{L^2}.$$ The remaining fourteen entries follow by the same integral and give the boxed matrix. Positive-semidefiniteness is inherited from the energy, which is an integral of $EI(w'')^2\ge0$; the null space is the set of nodal vectors with $w''\equiv0$, that is the straight-line motions $w=a+bx$, a translation and a rotation, so $\mathbf k_{\rm beam}$ has rank two and a beam, like a bar, needs supports before its system can be solved. ∎

The entries are the nodal forces and moments a unit nodal displacement or rotation calls up, so a bone modelled as a chain of beam elements reproduces its deflection under any load; K10 checks that one element already gives Module 2's cantilever formula exactly. The bar carries axial force, the beam carries bending; a general frame element superposes the two. With one element understood, the method is defined by how elements combine.

5. Assembling and solving a toy finite-element model

A body is meshed into many elements sharing nodes. The rule that combines them is physical: the total strain energy is the sum of the elements' energies, so the global stiffness is the sum of the element stiffnesses, each added into the rows and columns of the nodes it touches.

Proposition 5.1 (assembly and solution). For a mesh of elements $e$ with stiffness matrices $\mathbf k_e$ acting on their nodal displacements, the global equilibrium is $$\boxed{\;\mathbf K\,\mathbf u=\mathbf f,\qquad \mathbf K=\sum_e \mathbf L_e^{\!\top}\mathbf k_e\mathbf L_e,\;}$$ where $\mathbf L_e$ selects an element's nodes from the global list. $\mathbf K$ is symmetric positive-semidefinite; imposing enough displacement boundary conditions to remove rigid-body motion makes the reduced system positive-definite and uniquely solvable for $\mathbf u$, after which each element's stress follows from its nodal displacements.
The total strain energy is $W=\sum_e W_e=\sum_e\tfrac12\mathbf u_e^{\!\top}\mathbf k_e\mathbf u_e=\tfrac12\mathbf u^{\!\top}\big(\sum_e\mathbf L_e^{\!\top}\mathbf k_e\mathbf L_e\big)\mathbf u=\tfrac12\mathbf u^{\!\top}\mathbf K\mathbf u$, using $\mathbf u_e=\mathbf L_e\mathbf u$. Equilibrium is stationarity of the total potential energy $\Pi=W-\mathbf f^{\!\top}\mathbf u$: $\partial\Pi/\partial\mathbf u=\mathbf K\mathbf u-\mathbf f=\mathbf 0$, the boxed system. $\mathbf K$ inherits symmetry and positive-semidefiniteness from the $\mathbf k_e$ (a sum of such matrices), with rigid-body motions its null space (Proposition 4.1); fixing those degrees of freedom leaves a positive-definite reduced $\mathbf K$, which is invertible, so $\mathbf u$ is unique. The element strain, then stress $\sigma=E\varepsilon$, is read back from the solved nodal displacements. ∎

The whole method is short enough to read. The code below meshes a tapered bar into bar elements, assembles $\mathbf K$, applies a fixed end and an end load, solves $\mathbf K\mathbf u=\mathbf f$, and recovers the element stresses.

import numpy as np

E, L, P, A0, nel = 17e9, 0.10, 2000.0, 2e-4, 8
xn = np.linspace(0, L, nel + 1)
Ael = A0*(1 - 0.5*(xn[:-1] + xn[1:])/2/L)     # taper A0 -> A0/2
K = np.zeros((nel + 1, nel + 1))
for e in range(nel):                           # assemble
    k = E*Ael[e]/(xn[e + 1] - xn[e])
    K[e:e + 2, e:e + 2] += k*np.array([[1, -1], [-1, 1]])
f = np.zeros(nel + 1)
f[-1] = P                                      # end load
free = np.arange(1, nel + 1)                    # node 0 fixed
u = np.zeros(nel + 1)
u[free] = np.linalg.solve(K[np.ix_(free, free)], f[free])
sig = np.array([E*(u[e + 1] - u[e])/(xn[e + 1] - xn[e])
                for e in range(nel)])
print("tip displacement = %.4f mm" % (u[-1]*1e3))
print("stress %.1f -> %.1f MPa" % (sig[0]/1e6, sig[-1]/1e6))

The output is exact where it can be and convergent where it cannot. The tip displacement is $0.082\ \mathrm{mm}$ and the element stresses rise from $10\ \mathrm{MPa}$ at the wide end to $19\ \mathrm{MPa}$ at the narrow end, straddling the true $P/A(x)$ hyperbola that climbs from $10$ to $20\ \mathrm{MPa}$ (Fig. 6). Each element carries a single constant stress - the price of the linear-displacement assumption - so the solution is a staircase; refining the mesh shrinks the steps and the staircase converges to the exact curve. This is the whole of finite-element analysis in miniature: a boundary-value problem no formula solves becomes a matrix system a computer does, with an accuracy set by the mesh. The remaining sections make the material law inside each element realistic for tissue.

6. Anisotropy and fibre reinforcement

The isotropic law of Section 3 assumes the material responds the same in every direction. That is fine for bone matrix but badly wrong for tendon, ligament, and muscle, which are built of fibres running one way. A tendon pulled along its collagen is a stiff cable; pulled across, it is soft putty. Capturing this needs a law with a built-in direction.

Definition 6.1 (transversely isotropic, fibre-reinforced material). Let $\mathbf a$ be the unit vector of the fibre direction in the reference body. A material with one such preferred direction is transversely isotropic - isotropic in the plane perpendicular to $\mathbf a$ - and its stiffness needs five constants instead of two. The simplest useful form is a fibre-reinforced strain energy, an isotropic matrix plus a term that stiffens only along the fibres, $$\boxed{\;W=W_{\rm iso}(\boldsymbol\varepsilon)+W_{\rm fib}(\varepsilon_a),\qquad \varepsilon_a=\mathbf a\cdot\boldsymbol\varepsilon\,\mathbf a,\;}$$ where $\varepsilon_a$ is the strain resolved along the fibre. The matrix carries load in every direction; the fibre term adds stiffness only when the tissue is stretched along $\mathbf a$.
Proposition 6.1 (fibre reinforcement is uniaxial). The extra stress from the fibre term acts purely along the fibre direction: $$\boxed{\;\boldsymbol\sigma_{\rm fib}=\frac{\partial W_{\rm fib}}{\partial\varepsilon_a}\,\mathbf a\otimes\mathbf a,\;}$$ ($\mathbf a\otimes\mathbf a$ the outer product, $(\mathbf a\otimes\mathbf a)_{ij}=a_ia_j$), so a fibre-reinforced material is stiff for loads along $\mathbf a$ and reduces to its (soft) isotropic matrix for loads perpendicular to $\mathbf a$.
The fibre strain is $\varepsilon_a=\mathbf a\cdot\boldsymbol\varepsilon\,\mathbf a=a_i\varepsilon_{ij}a_j$, so $\partial\varepsilon_a/\partial\varepsilon_{ij}=a_ia_j=(\mathbf a\otimes\mathbf a)_{ij}$. The fibre stress is the gradient of the fibre energy (Proposition 3.1 generalised), $(\boldsymbol\sigma_{\rm fib})_{ij}=\partial W_{\rm fib}/\partial\varepsilon_{ij}=W_{\rm fib}'(\varepsilon_a)\,\partial\varepsilon_a/\partial\varepsilon_{ij}=W_{\rm fib}'(\varepsilon_a)\,a_ia_j$, i.e. $\boldsymbol\sigma_{\rm fib}=W_{\rm fib}'(\varepsilon_a)\,\mathbf a\otimes\mathbf a$. This tensor has its only non-zero action along $\mathbf a$: for a load with normal $\mathbf n\perp\mathbf a$, $\boldsymbol\sigma_{\rm fib}\mathbf n=W_{\rm fib}'(\varepsilon_a)(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf n)\mathbf a=\mathbf 0$, so across the fibres only the isotropic matrix responds. ∎

Figure 7 makes the consequence quantitative. Resolving a uniaxial strain onto the fibres and the resulting fibre stress back onto the load direction projects twice, so the directional stiffness is $E(\theta)=E_m+E_f\cos^4\theta$ (derived in K6): about $1.5\ \mathrm{GPa}$ along the fibres and $0.12\ \mathrm{GPa}$ across them, a ratio of $12.5$, with the stiffness halving by $35^\circ$ - the anisotropy that lets a tendon be a taut cable in one direction and fold freely in another. The same $\mathbf a\otimes\mathbf a$ structure describes ligament, the annulus of an intervertebral disc, and the helical fibres of muscle and heart wall; changing $\mathbf a$ (or adding a second fibre family) tailors the law to each tissue. But tendon is also grossly nonlinear - its stiffness rises with stretch - and a strain measured as $\Delta L/L$ breaks down once the stretch is large. Both need the finite-strain framework of the next section.

7. Hyperelasticity: finite strain and strain-energy density

Linear elasticity assumes strains are small enough that $\boldsymbol\varepsilon=\tfrac12(\nabla\mathbf u+\nabla\mathbf u^{\!\top})$ and that stress is proportional to it. Soft tissues violate both: a tendon stretches several per cent, skin and muscle tens of per cent, and their stress-stretch curves bend. Hyperelasticity keeps the full deformation gradient $\mathbf F$ (Section 1) and derives stress from a stored-energy function, so large deformations and nonlinearity are built in from the start.

Definition 7.1 (hyperelastic material). A material is hyperelastic if its stress derives from a strain-energy density $W(\mathbf F)$ per unit reference volume, a function of the deformation gradient alone. The work-conjugate stress is its gradient - the (first Piola-Kirchhoff) nominal stress $\mathbf P=\partial W/\partial\mathbf F$, or equivalently the Cauchy stress $\boldsymbol\sigma=J^{-1}\mathbf P\mathbf F^{\!\top}$. The archetype is the neo-Hookean solid, $$W=\tfrac{\mu}{2}\big(\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf F^{\!\top}\mathbf F)-3\big)-\mu\ln J+\tfrac{\lambda}{2}(\ln J)^2,$$ whose two constants $\mu,\lambda$ are the same Lamé constants as the linear law (the Lamé $\lambda$, not the stretch ratio $\lambda$ of the stress-stretch figures), now governing arbitrarily large strain.
Proposition 7.1 (hyperelasticity reduces to linear elasticity at small strain). Expanding a hyperelastic law about the undeformed state $\mathbf F=\mathbf I$ recovers linear elasticity to first order: for the neo-Hookean solid, $$\boxed{\;\boldsymbol\sigma=\lambda(\operatorname{tr}\boldsymbol\varepsilon)\mathbf I+2\mu\boldsymbol\varepsilon+O(\lVert\nabla\mathbf u\rVert^2),\;}$$ so the linear constants are the small-strain limit of the hyperelastic ones, and the two theories agree exactly where strains are small.
Write $\mathbf F=\mathbf I+\mathbf H$, $\mathbf H=\nabla\mathbf u$ small. Then $\mathbf F^{\!\top}\mathbf F=\mathbf I+2\boldsymbol\varepsilon+O(\mathbf H^2)$ with $\boldsymbol\varepsilon=\tfrac12(\mathbf H+\mathbf H^{\!\top})$, and $J=\det\mathbf F=1+\operatorname{tr}\mathbf H+O(\mathbf H^2)$, so $\ln J=\operatorname{tr}\boldsymbol\varepsilon+O(\mathbf H^2)$. The nominal stress of the neo-Hookean energy is $\mathbf P=\mu(\mathbf F-\mathbf F^{-\top})+\lambda\ln J\,\mathbf F^{-\top}$; substituting $\mathbf F=\mathbf I+\mathbf H$ and $\mathbf F^{-\top}=\mathbf I-\mathbf H^{\!\top}+O(\mathbf H^2)$ gives $\mathbf P=\mu(\mathbf H+\mathbf H^{\!\top})+\lambda(\operatorname{tr}\boldsymbol\varepsilon)\mathbf I+O(\mathbf H^2)=2\mu\boldsymbol\varepsilon+\lambda(\operatorname{tr}\boldsymbol\varepsilon)\mathbf I+O(\mathbf H^2)$. To this order $\boldsymbol\sigma=\mathbf P$, the isotropic linear law of Definition 3.1. ∎

Figure 8 contrasts the laws over a $10\%$ stretch. The linear and neo-Hookean curves rise together and stay close - a modest nonlinearity. The fibre-reinforced curve is different in kind: it is nearly flat at first (the toe region, where crimped collagen straightens at almost no stress - exactly the J-shaped law Module 6 derived) and then climbs steeply once the fibres are taut, crossing above the linear line. Modelling that toe needs a fibre strain energy that grows exponentially with fibre stretch (a term like $W_{\rm fib}\propto e^{k\varepsilon_a}-1$ with $k$ a fibre stiffening rate (distinct from the permeability $k$ of Section 8), combining the fibre reinforcement of Section 6 with the finite strain of this section). The toe is not a curiosity: it is why tendons are compliant shock-absorbers at low load and stiff force-transmitters at high load, the dual role Module 6 built a whole spring model around. With anisotropy and nonlinearity in hand, one behaviour remains - time dependence - which is where the fluid in tissue enters.

8. Viscoelasticity and poroelasticity

An elastic material, linear or hyperelastic, responds to load instantly and stores all the work as recoverable energy. Real tissue does neither: hold a tendon at a fixed stretch and its stress slowly falls; press cartilage and it keeps sinking for minutes. This time dependence has two physical origins, and the continuum captures both.

Definition 8.1 (linear viscoelasticity). A viscoelastic material's stress depends on the whole history of strain, not its current value. For small strains this is the hereditary integral $$\boxed{\;\sigma(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{t} G(t-s)\,\frac{d\varepsilon}{ds}\,ds,\;}$$ with $G(t)$ the relaxation modulus - the stress remaining a time $t$ after a unit step of strain. $G$ falls from an instantaneous value $G(0)=G_0$ to an equilibrium $G(\infty)=G_\infty$; the standard-linear-solid model (a spring in parallel with a spring-and-dashpot, Module 6) gives $G(t)=G_\infty+(G_0-G_\infty)e^{-t/\tau}$ with relaxation time $\tau$.

The second origin is fluid. Cartilage and, on longer scales, bone are biphasic: a porous solid matrix saturated with interstitial fluid. Load is shared between a stress in the solid and a pressure in the fluid, and the fluid can only move by seeping through the matrix (Darcy's law), which takes time.

Definition 8.2 (biphasic / poroelastic material). In a biphasic material the total stress splits into a fluid pressure and a solid (effective) stress, $$\boxed{\;\boldsymbol\sigma=-p\,\mathbf I+\boldsymbol\sigma_{\rm s},\;}$$ where $p$ is the interstitial fluid pressure and $\boldsymbol\sigma_{\rm s}$ the stress carried by the solid matrix. Combining this split with Darcy flow and mass conservation yields a consolidation (diffusion) equation for the matrix displacement $u_z$ (written $u_z$ here to keep it distinct from the displacement field $\mathbf u$ of Section 1 and the nodal displacements of Section 4), $\partial u_z/\partial t=c_v\,\partial^2u_z/\partial z^2$, with consolidation coefficient $c_v=H_Ak_p$ ($H_A$ the aggregate modulus, $k_p$ the permeability) - the very equation Module 4 derived for cartilage. Because the pore pressure is proportional to the compaction gradient, $p$ satisfies the same diffusion equation with complementary boundary conditions (drained where the matrix is free, sealed where it is fixed), so a solver written for either field answers for both; Lab C solves for $p$.
Proposition 8.1 (a draining tissue can only get softer). Suppose the relaxation modulus is a sum of decaying exponentials with non-negative weights above a floor, $$G(t)=G_\infty+\sum_{m}g_m\,e^{-t/\tau_m},\qquad g_m\ge0,\quad\tau_m\gt0,\quad G_\infty\ge0,$$ a Prony series (the standard linear solid of Definition 8.1 is the one-term case). Then $$\boxed{\;G_0\ge G(t)\ge G_\infty\ge0,\qquad G'(t)\le0\ \text{ for all }t\ge0,\;}$$ with $G_0=G(0)=G_\infty+\sum_mg_m$. A biphasic tissue has this form, so it is stiffer the faster it is loaded: no drainage process can make it stiffen with time.
Differentiate term by term: $G'(t)=-\sum_m(g_m/\tau_m)e^{-t/\tau_m}$. Every $g_m\ge0$, every $\tau_m\gt0$ and every exponential is positive, so each term of the sum is non-positive and $G'(t)\le0$ for all $t\ge0$. A function with a non-positive derivative is non-increasing, so $G(0)\ge G(t)\ge\lim_{t\to\infty}G(t)=G_\infty$, and $G_\infty\ge0$ because a negative equilibrium modulus would violate the energy positivity of Proposition 3.1. That is the boxed chain, and the sign of $G'$ never depended on how many terms the series has.

The biphasic tissue supplies the weights rather than assuming them. Solving the consolidation equation of Definition 8.2 for a layer of thickness $L$, drained at one face and sealed at the other, by separation of variables gives modes $\cos((2n+1)\pi z/2L)$ with decay times $\tau_n=4L^2/((2n+1)^2\pi^2c_v)$, and the layer-averaged pore pressure after a step load is $$F(T)=\sum_{n\ge0}\frac{8}{(2n+1)^2\pi^2}\,e^{-(2n+1)^2\pi^2T/4},\qquad T=\frac{c_vt}{L^2}.$$ Every weight $8/((2n+1)^2\pi^2)$ is positive, so seepage is a Prony series with the hypothesis of this proposition already satisfied, and the load can only pass one way, from fluid to matrix. Lab C computes that series and its finite-difference solution and finds them agreeing to $0.6\%$. The physical reading is the one Figure 9's caption gives: at $t=0$ the fluid has had no time to move and, being nearly incompressible, carries a large share of the load, so the tissue answers with its full $G_0$; as $t$ grows the pressure gradient drives seepage, the fluid sheds its share, and what is left is the matrix alone at $G_\infty$. Two mechanisms, viscoelastic and biphasic, one continuum signature. ∎

Figure 9 shows both faces of time dependence: the relaxing stress of a viscoelastic tendon and the draining load-partition of biphasic cartilage. They are not merely analogous - a biphasic tissue is viscoelastic, its relaxation time $\tau\sim L^2/c_v$ set by how long fluid takes to seep a distance $L$ (Module 4's diffusion timescale). This is why cartilage feels rubbery under a quick tap and yielding under a sustained press, and why impact loading - too fast for the fluid to escape - is carried safely by pressure while a slow crushing load falls on the matrix and damages it. The continuum with a relaxation modulus, or a biphasic split, captures the whole range.

10. Computational labs

Lab A - finite-element convergence

Physical question. How fast does a finite-element answer approach the exact one as the mesh is refined? Model. the tapered-bar solver of Section 5. Parameters. $E=17\ \mathrm{GPa}$, $L=0.1\ \mathrm{m}$, area $A_0\to A_0/2$, load $P=2\ \mathrm{kN}$. Equations. exact tip displacement $u_L=\dfrac{2PL}{EA_0}\ln 2$; error $=|u_L^{\rm FE}-u_L|/u_L$.

import numpy as np

E, L, P, A0 = 17e9, 0.10, 2000.0, 2e-4
u_exact = 2*P*L/(E*A0)*np.log(2)


def tip(nel):
    xn = np.linspace(0, L, nel + 1)
    Ael = A0*(1 - 0.5*(xn[:-1] + xn[1:])/2/L)
    K = np.zeros((nel + 1, nel + 1))
    for e in range(nel):
        k = E*Ael[e]/(xn[e + 1] - xn[e])
        K[e:e + 2, e:e + 2] += k*np.array([[1, -1], [-1, 1]])
    fr = np.arange(1, nel + 1)
    u = np.zeros(nel + 1)
    u[fr] = np.linalg.solve(K[np.ix_(fr, fr)], np.eye(nel)[-1]*P)
    return u[-1]


for n in (2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64):
    print("N=%2d  error = %.2e" % (n, abs(tip(n) - u_exact)/u_exact))

Interpretation. The error falls from $1.1\times10^{-2}$ at 2 elements to $1.1\times10^{-5}$ at 64 - a factor of $\sim$4 per mesh doubling, the slope-$-2$ (second-order) convergence of linear bar elements. Sensitivity. A steeper taper raises the constant but not the slope. Extension. Replace the bar with the beam element of Section 4 and confirm its convergence on a cantilever tip deflection.

Lab B - when may the linear law be used?

Physical question. Up to what strain is the small-strain linear law within $5\%$ of the true nonlinear response? Model. neo-Hookean vs fibre-reinforced (Section 7). Parameters. $\mu$, and a fibre law $\propto e^{k\varepsilon}-1$ with $k=48$. Equations. error $=|\sigma_{\rm nl}-\sigma_{\rm lin}|/\sigma_{\rm lin}$ vs strain.

import numpy as np

eps = np.linspace(1e-4, 0.5, 4000)
lam = 1 + eps
neo = lam**2 - 1/lam                   # neo-Hookean (units of mu)
dep_neo = np.abs(neo - 3*eps)/(3*eps)  # vs its linear tangent 3*mu*eps
k = 48.0
fib = np.exp(k*eps) - 1                 # fibre exponential toe
dep_fib = np.abs(fib - k*eps)/(k*eps)   # vs tangent k*eps
for name, d in (("neo-Hookean", dep_neo), ("fibre", dep_fib)):
    over = np.where(d > 0.05)[0]
    lim = eps[over[0]] if len(over) else eps[-1]
    print("%-11s linear law within 5%% up to eps = %.3f" % (name, lim))

Interpretation. The neo-Hookean linear law stays within $5\%$ out to $\approx47\%$ strain, but the fibre law - all exponential toe - breaks the same tolerance by $\approx0.2\%$ strain: a $\sim$200-fold difference in validity. Sensitivity. The fibre range scales as $1/k$; a sharper toe is linear-valid over an even narrower band. Extension. Show analytically that an $e^{k\varepsilon}$ law departs from its tangent by $\approx k\varepsilon/2$, so the $5\%$ limit is $\varepsilon\approx0.1/k$.

Lab C - load sharing between fluid and matrix under a step load

Physical question. When a cartilage layer is pressed and held, how fast does the load pass from the pressurised fluid to the solid matrix? Model. the consolidation diffusion of Section 8 (Module 4), one dimension, drained at the loaded surface and sealed at the bone. Parameters. the only parameter is the dimensionless time $T=c_vt/L^2$; the run below covers $T:0\to0.5$ using $c_v=10^{-3}\ \mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}$ and $L=1\ \mathrm{m}$, an assumed pair chosen so that one explicit step is $0.25\ \mathrm s$. Equations. a step load is carried entirely by the fluid at $t=0^+$, so $p(z,0)=\sigma_0$; thereafter $\partial p/\partial t=c_v\,\partial^2p/\partial z^2$, the same consolidation equation the matrix displacement obeys (Definition 8.2), solved by explicit finite differences. The layer-averaged fluid share is $F(T)=\langle p\rangle/\sigma_0$ and the matrix share is $U(T)=1-F(T)$.

import numpy as np

nz, cv, L = 40, 1e-3, 1.0
dz = L/nz
dt = 0.4*dz**2/cv                    # stable explicit step
p = np.ones(nz + 1)                  # step load: fluid carries all of it
p[0] = 0.0                           # drained top surface
T50 = None
for it in range(2000):
    lap = np.zeros_like(p)
    lap[1:-1] = (p[2:] - 2*p[1:-1] + p[:-2])/dz**2
    lap[-1] = (2*p[-2] - 2*p[-1])/dz**2   # sealed bottom
    p = p + dt*cv*lap
    p[0] = 0.0
    T = cv*(it + 1)*dt/L**2          # dimensionless consolidation time
    if T50 is None and p.mean() < 0.5:
        T50 = T
    if it in (0, 1999):
        print("T = %.4f: fluid share = %.4f, matrix share = %.4f"
              % (T, p.mean(), 1 - p.mean()))
print("half-consolidation at T = %.3f" % T50)
ser = sum(8/((2*n + 1)**2*np.pi**2)*np.exp(-((2*n + 1)*np.pi/2)**2*T)
          for n in range(80))
print("analytic series at T = %.2f gives %.4f" % (T, ser))
# -> T = 0.0003: fluid share = 0.9659, matrix share = 0.0341
# -> T = 0.5000: fluid share = 0.2347, matrix share = 0.7653
# -> half-consolidation at T = 0.195
# -> analytic series at T = 0.50 gives 0.2360

Interpretation. The applied load starts almost entirely on the fluid, $F=0.966$ at the first step, and ends mostly on the matrix, $F=0.235$ and $U=0.765$ at $T=0.5$; the analytic Terzaghi series $F(T)=\sum_{n\ge0}\frac{8}{(2n+1)^2\pi^2}e^{-(2n+1)^2\pi^2T/4}$ gives $0.2360$ at the same $T$, so the 40-cell mesh is accurate to $0.6\%$. Half the load has transferred by $T_{1/2}=0.195$ (the series itself gives $0.1967$, the tabulated Terzaghi value $0.197$). The transfer is monotone, which is the same one-way behaviour Proposition 8.1 proves for the step-strain relaxation modulus, and for the same reason: every term of the series decays and none of them grows. Sensitivity. Because $T$ is the only parameter, the physical half-time is $t_{1/2}=0.195\,L^2/c_v$: a layer twice as thick, or a matrix half as permeable, takes four times or twice as long. K8 confirms the thickness scaling by running the solver at three thicknesses and then puts cartilage's own $c_v$ into it. Extension. Re-run with a sealed top and a drained bottom and confirm $T_{1/2}$ is unchanged, since the drainage path length, not its direction, sets the clock.

11. Problems, diagnostics, and limitations

Jump to: Conceptual · Derivational · Computational · Diagnostics · Repayment

Thirty problems - ten conceptual, ten derivational, ten computational - plus five diagnostics. Each carries a Probes note and a collapsible worked solution. Every computational solution either carries its own runnable code with the output quoted beneath it, names the lab block in Section 10 that produces its numbers, or evaluates a closed-form sweep with every value of the sweep written out.

Conceptual ↑ contents

C1 - why a tensor. Why can a single number not describe the stress at a point, forcing a tensor? Probes: traction depends on the cut; Section 2.
Solution
The traction - force per area transmitted across an internal cut - changes with the orientation of the cut: a plane parallel to a pull carries shear, a plane perpendicular carries the full tension. A number cannot encode a quantity that depends on direction; the linear map $\mathbf t=\boldsymbol\sigma\mathbf n$ from cut-normal to traction is exactly a second-order tensor. Strain is a tensor for the mirror reason - stretch depends on direction.
C2 - six, not nine. The stress tensor has nine components but only six are independent. Why? Probes: angular-momentum balance; Section 2.
Solution
Balance of angular momentum on a shrinking element forces $\sigma_{ij}=\sigma_{ji}$ (Proposition 2.2): the shear on one pair of faces must equal its complement, or the element would spin up without bound. A symmetric $3\times3$ matrix has six independent entries - three normal and three shear stresses - which is why the elastic law needs six stress-strain relations, not nine.
C3 - rotation is not strain. Why is the deformation gradient F a poor strain measure, and what fixes it? Probes: rigid rotation; Section 1.
Solution
F contains rotation as well as stretch: spinning the body rigidly changes F (to a rotation matrix) but strains nothing. A strain measure must vanish for every rigid motion. The Green-Lagrange strain $\mathbf E=\tfrac12(\mathbf F^{\!\top}\mathbf F-\mathbf I)$ does: for a pure rotation $\mathbf F^{\!\top}\mathbf F=\mathbf I$ so $\mathbf E=0$. It measures the change in squared fibre length, the rotation-free part of F.
C4 - why a structure needs a support. The bar element's stiffness matrix is singular. What does its zero eigenvalue mean, and what is the consequence? Probes: rigid translation; Section 4.
Solution
The zero eigenvalue has eigenvector $(1,1)$ - both nodes moving together, a rigid translation that stretches nothing and stores no energy. A matrix with a zero eigenvalue is singular, so $\mathbf K\mathbf u=\mathbf f$ has no unique solution: the displacement is undetermined up to a rigid body motion. Imposing enough supports to remove rigid modes makes the reduced $\mathbf K$ invertible - the mathematical reason every structure must be tied down.
C5 - isotropic bone, anisotropic tendon. Why model bone with one modulus but tendon with a direction? Probes: microstructure; Section 6.
Solution
Bone's mineralised matrix, at the tissue scale, has no strongly preferred direction, so an isotropic two-constant law is a good average. Tendon is built of collagen fibres nearly all aligned along its axis, so it is an order of magnitude stiffer along the fibres than across - a transversely isotropic material whose fibre term (Proposition 6.1) adds stiffness only along $\mathbf a$. The law must reflect the microstructure it averages.
C6 - the toe. Why is a tendon compliant at low load and stiff at high load? Probes: crimped collagen; Section 7.
Solution
At rest the collagen fibres are crimped (wavy); pulling first straightens the crimp at almost no resistance - the compliant toe region - and only once the fibres are taut does further stretch load them directly, giving a steep rise. A fibre strain energy growing exponentially with fibre stretch reproduces this J-shaped curve; the toe is why tendon absorbs low-energy motion softly yet transmits high force stiffly (Module 6).
C7 - when linear fails. Why does linear elasticity break at large strain while hyperelasticity does not? Probes: finite strain; Section 7.
Solution
Linear elasticity assumes strain is the small quantity $\tfrac12(\nabla\mathbf u+\nabla\mathbf u^{\!\top})$ and stress is proportional to it - both true only for small displacement gradients. Hyperelasticity keeps the full deformation gradient F and derives stress from an energy $W(\mathbf F)$, so it is valid at any stretch and can be nonlinear. It contains linear elasticity as its small-strain limit (Proposition 7.1), extending rather than replacing it.
C8 - stiff fast, soft slow. Why does cartilage resist a quick load but yield to a slow one? Probes: fluid pressurisation; Section 8.
Solution
Cartilage is biphasic: a fast load gives the interstitial fluid no time to escape, so it pressurises and carries most of the load, and the tissue resists with its high instantaneous stiffness $G_0$. Under a sustained load the fluid slowly seeps out, transferring the load to the soft solid matrix, so the tissue creeps to its low equilibrium stiffness $G_\infty$ (Proposition 8.1). The same tissue is stiff or soft depending on the loading rate.
C9 - biphasic is viscoelastic. Why does a purely elastic solid soaked in fluid behave viscoelastically? Probes: seepage as relaxation; Section 8.
Solution
Even with an elastic matrix and an inviscid rearrangement, the time the fluid takes to seep through the pores introduces a memory: stress at a given strain depends on how much fluid has drained, hence on history. The relaxation time is the diffusion time $\tau\sim L^2/c_v$. So poroelasticity produces a relaxation modulus $G(t)$ indistinguishable in form from a viscoelastic one - a second physical route to the same continuum signature.
C10 - the parent theory. In what sense is the continuum the parent of the beam, spring, and biphasic-column models? Probes: each as a limit; Section 9.
Solution
Each earlier model is the continuum specialised: the Euler-Bernoulli beam is the 3-D elasticity of a slender body under a plane-sections assumption; the tendon spring is the fibre-reinforced law reduced to one dimension; the cartilage spring-dashpot column is the biphasic consolidation equation in one dimension. They are projections, not different physics, so the continuum both justifies them and says exactly what each threw away - the unifying view of Section 9.

Derivational ↑ contents

D1 - the deformation gradient. From the deformation map, derive that a material fibre transforms as $d\mathbf x=\mathbf F\,d\mathbf X$. Probes: the Jacobian; Section 1.
Solution
Let $\mathbf x=\boldsymbol\chi(\mathbf X)$. A nearby reference point $\mathbf X+d\mathbf X$ maps to $\boldsymbol\chi(\mathbf X+d\mathbf X)=\boldsymbol\chi(\mathbf X)+\dfrac{\partial\boldsymbol\chi}{\partial\mathbf X}d\mathbf X+O(\lvert d\mathbf X\rvert^2)$, so the deformed fibre is $d\mathbf x=\mathbf x(\mathbf X+d\mathbf X)-\mathbf x(\mathbf X)=\mathbf F\,d\mathbf X$ with $\mathbf F=\partial\mathbf x/\partial\mathbf X$ the Jacobian. Writing $\mathbf x=\mathbf X+\mathbf u$ gives $\mathbf F=\mathbf I+\partial\mathbf u/\partial\mathbf X$.
D2 - small strain from finite strain. Show the Green-Lagrange strain reduces to the infinitesimal strain for small gradients. Probes: linearisation; Section 1.
Solution
With $\mathbf F=\mathbf I+\mathbf H$, $\mathbf H=\nabla\mathbf u$: $\mathbf F^{\!\top}\mathbf F=\mathbf I+\mathbf H+\mathbf H^{\!\top}+\mathbf H^{\!\top}\mathbf H$, so $\mathbf E=\tfrac12(\mathbf F^{\!\top}\mathbf F-\mathbf I)=\tfrac12(\mathbf H+\mathbf H^{\!\top})+\tfrac12\mathbf H^{\!\top}\mathbf H$. The first term is $\boldsymbol\varepsilon$; the second is quadratic and dropped for $\lVert\mathbf H\rVert\ll1$, giving $\mathbf E=\boldsymbol\varepsilon+O(\lVert\nabla\mathbf u\rVert^2)$ (Proposition 1.1). It also vanishes for a pure rotation, where $\mathbf F^{\!\top}\mathbf F=\mathbf I$.
D3 - which power of $h$ kills which term. Redo the tetrahedron argument of Proposition 2.1 keeping the body force and the inertia term explicitly, and say which power of $h$ kills each. Probes: the stress tetrahedron, and why Cauchy's theorem is not a statement about statics; Section 2.
Solution
Keep every term. For a tetrahedron of longest edge $h$ the oblique face has area $A$, the axis faces have areas $An_j$ (they are its projections), and the enclosed volume is $V=\tfrac13Ah'$ with $h'\le h$. Newton's second law, not force balance, reads $\mathbf t(\mathbf n)A-\sum_j\mathbf t^{(j)}An_j+\mathbf bV=\rho V\ddot{\mathbf x}$. Divide throughout by $A$. The two surface terms are $O(1)$ in $h$ - they are tractions, and dividing an area-integrated quantity by that area leaves the traction itself. The body force retains $V/A=\tfrac13h'$ and the inertia retains $\rho V/A=\tfrac13\rho h'$, so both carry exactly one surviving power of $h$, and it is that single power that kills them as $h\to0$. Two consequences worth stating. Because the acceleration enters only through the $h^1$ factor, the argument holds however large $\ddot{\mathbf x}$ is: Cauchy's theorem is not a statement about statics, and the stress tensor exists in a body being struck as much as in one standing still. And because a body force enters the same way, no distributed load - gravity, an electromagnetic body force - can break the linearity either. What survives is $\mathbf t(\mathbf n)=\sum_j\mathbf t^{(j)}n_j$, that is $t_i=\sigma_{ij}n_j$, which is Proposition 2.1.
D4 - stress symmetry. Derive $\sigma_{ij}=\sigma_{ji}$ from moment balance. Probes: couples on a cube; Section 2.
Solution
On a cube of side $h$, the shear $\sigma_{ij}$ on the faces normal to $j$ forms a couple $\sim\sigma_{ij}h^3$ about the perpendicular axis; the complementary shear $\sigma_{ji}$ forms an opposing couple $\sim\sigma_{ji}h^3$. The rotational inertia scales as $\rho h^5$ with finite angular acceleration, so the net moment must vanish faster than $h^3$; dividing by $h^3$ and letting $h\to0$ gives $\sigma_{ij}-\sigma_{ji}=0$ (Proposition 2.2).
D5 - the isotropic law. Write the isotropic stress-strain law and relate the Lamé constants to $E,\nu$. Probes: two constants; Section 3.
Solution
Isotropy leaves two constants: $\boldsymbol\sigma=\lambda(\operatorname{tr}\boldsymbol\varepsilon)\mathbf I+2\mu\boldsymbol\varepsilon$. For uniaxial stress $\sigma_{11}=\sigma$, others zero, the axial strain is $\varepsilon_{11}=\sigma/E$ and transverse $\varepsilon_{22}=-\nu\sigma/E$; substituting into the law and matching gives $\mu=\dfrac{E}{2(1+\nu)}$ (the shear modulus) and $\lambda=\dfrac{E\nu}{(1+\nu)(1-2\nu)}$. The shear modulus governs shape change, the bulk modulus $K=\lambda+\tfrac23\mu$ volume change.
D6 - strain energy. Derive $W=\tfrac12\boldsymbol\sigma:\boldsymbol\varepsilon$ and $\sigma_{ij}=\partial W/\partial\varepsilon_{ij}$. Probes: work along a load path; Section 3.
Solution
Load proportionally, $\boldsymbol\varepsilon(s)=s\bar{\boldsymbol\varepsilon}$, $\boldsymbol\sigma(s)=s\mathbf C:\bar{\boldsymbol\varepsilon}$. The work per volume is $W=\int_0^1\boldsymbol\sigma:d\boldsymbol\varepsilon=\int_0^1 s\,ds\,(\mathbf C:\bar{\boldsymbol\varepsilon}):\bar{\boldsymbol\varepsilon}=\tfrac12\bar{\boldsymbol\varepsilon}:\mathbf C:\bar{\boldsymbol\varepsilon}=\tfrac12\boldsymbol\sigma:\boldsymbol\varepsilon$. Differentiating $W=\tfrac12\varepsilon_{ij}C_{ijkl}\varepsilon_{kl}$ (with $\mathbf C$ symmetric) gives $\partial W/\partial\varepsilon_{ij}=C_{ijkl}\varepsilon_{kl}=\sigma_{ij}$ (Proposition 3.1).
D7 - the bar element. Derive the $2\times2$ bar stiffness matrix from the element strain energy. Probes: energy Hessian; Section 4.
Solution
Linear displacement gives constant strain $\varepsilon=(u_2-u_1)/L$; the energy is the density $\tfrac12E\varepsilon^2$ times volume $AL$: $W_e=\tfrac12\tfrac{EA}{L}(u_2-u_1)^2$. Nodal forces are its gradient, $f_1=\partial W_e/\partial u_1=-\tfrac{EA}{L}(u_2-u_1)$, $f_2=+\tfrac{EA}{L}(u_2-u_1)$; the Hessian is $\mathbf k_e=\tfrac{EA}{L}\big(\begin{smallmatrix}1&-1\\-1&1\end{smallmatrix}\big)$ (Proposition 4.1), symmetric with a zero eigenvalue for rigid translation.
D8 - assembly. Show the global stiffness is the sum of element stiffnesses, $\mathbf K=\sum_e\mathbf L_e^{\!\top}\mathbf k_e\mathbf L_e$. Probes: energy additivity; Section 5.
Solution
Total energy is the sum of element energies: $W=\sum_e\tfrac12\mathbf u_e^{\!\top}\mathbf k_e\mathbf u_e$. Writing each element's nodal displacements as a selection of the global vector, $\mathbf u_e=\mathbf L_e\mathbf u$, gives $W=\tfrac12\mathbf u^{\!\top}\big(\sum_e\mathbf L_e^{\!\top}\mathbf k_e\mathbf L_e\big)\mathbf u=\tfrac12\mathbf u^{\!\top}\mathbf K\mathbf u$. Equilibrium is stationarity of $\Pi=W-\mathbf f^{\!\top}\mathbf u$, giving $\mathbf K\mathbf u=\mathbf f$ (Proposition 5.1) - assembly is just adding energies.
D9 - fibre stress. Show the fibre reinforcement contributes stress only along the fibre, $\boldsymbol\sigma_{\rm fib}=W_{\rm fib}'\,\mathbf a\otimes\mathbf a$. Probes: chain rule on $\varepsilon_a$; Section 6.
Solution
The fibre strain is $\varepsilon_a=a_i\varepsilon_{ij}a_j$, so $\partial\varepsilon_a/\partial\varepsilon_{ij}=a_ia_j$. By the chain rule the fibre stress is $(\boldsymbol\sigma_{\rm fib})_{ij}=\partial W_{\rm fib}/\partial\varepsilon_{ij}=W_{\rm fib}'(\varepsilon_a)\,a_ia_j$, i.e. $\boldsymbol\sigma_{\rm fib}=W_{\rm fib}'(\varepsilon_a)\,\mathbf a\otimes\mathbf a$ (Proposition 6.1). For a normal $\mathbf n\perp\mathbf a$, $\boldsymbol\sigma_{\rm fib}\mathbf n\propto(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf n)\mathbf a=\mathbf 0$: no fibre stress across the fibres.
D10 - neo-Hookean to linear. Show the neo-Hookean stress reduces to the isotropic linear law at small strain. Probes: expand about F = I; Section 7.
Solution
The neo-Hookean nominal stress is $\mathbf P=\mu(\mathbf F-\mathbf F^{-\top})+\lambda\ln J\,\mathbf F^{-\top}$. With $\mathbf F=\mathbf I+\mathbf H$: $\mathbf F^{-\top}=\mathbf I-\mathbf H^{\!\top}+O(\mathbf H^2)$ and $\ln J=\operatorname{tr}\mathbf H+O(\mathbf H^2)$. Then $\mathbf P=\mu(\mathbf H+\mathbf H^{\!\top})+\lambda(\operatorname{tr}\boldsymbol\varepsilon)\mathbf I+O(\mathbf H^2)=2\mu\boldsymbol\varepsilon+\lambda(\operatorname{tr}\boldsymbol\varepsilon)\mathbf I$, the isotropic linear law (Proposition 7.1), with $\boldsymbol\sigma=\mathbf P$ to this order.

K2 - recover the modulus (inverse problem). A tapered bar of known geometry is pulled with $P=2\ \mathrm{kN}$ and its tip displacement is measured as $81.55\ \mu\mathrm m$. Probes: an inverse problem solved on the forward model, and error propagation as the thing that decides whether a measurement is worth making; Section 5.
Solution
The forward map $E\mapsto u_L^{\rm FE}(E)$ is the Section 5 solver, monotone decreasing and smooth, so the inverse is a one-dimensional root find: bracket $E$ between $1\ \mathrm{GPa}$ and $1\ \mathrm{TPa}$ and bisect on $u_L^{\rm FE}(E)-u_{\rm meas}=0$. With 64 elements this returns $E=17.00\ \mathrm{GPa}$, cortical bone, in a few dozen function evaluations. Doing it on the model rather than on the closed form is the point: the same three lines invert a bar whose taper, hole, or material grading has no closed form, which is the situation every real identification is in (Module 15's parameter estimation, now applied to a material property rather than a segment inertia). Error propagation is where the answer earns its use. Since the bar is linear elastic, $u_L\propto1/E$ exactly, so the relative errors map almost one to one: a $1\%$ error in the measured displacement returns a $0.99\%$ error in $E$, and $5\%$ returns $4.76\%$ - not $5\%$, because the map is a reciprocal, not a linear one. Numerically, $1\%$ of the reading is $0.816\ \mu\mathrm m$ out of $81.5\ \mu\mathrm m$, so a $1\%$ modulus estimate needs sub-micron displacement resolution. That is the whole design constraint on such a test, and it is invisible until the sensitivity is computed.
import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import brentq

L, P, A0, nel = 0.10, 2000.0, 2e-4, 64


def tip(E):
    xn = np.linspace(0, L, nel + 1)
    Ael = A0*(1 - 0.5*(xn[:-1] + xn[1:])/2/L)
    K = np.zeros((nel + 1, nel + 1))
    for e in range(nel):
        k = E*Ael[e]/(xn[e + 1] - xn[e])
        K[e:e + 2, e:e + 2] += k*np.array([[1, -1], [-1, 1]])
    fr = np.arange(1, nel + 1)
    u = np.zeros(nel + 1)
    u[fr] = np.linalg.solve(K[np.ix_(fr, fr)], np.eye(nel)[-1]*P)
    return u[-1]

K3 - the localisation sweep (sensitivity). Put a soft and a stiff segment in series and sweep the modulus ratio, reporting the fraction of the total deformation the soft segment takes. Probes: strain localisation across a ratio sweep; Section 5.
Solution
In series the force is common, so the stress $\sigma=P/A$ is equal but the strain $\varepsilon=\sigma/E$ is inversely proportional to modulus. For two equal segments the soft one takes a fraction $r/(1+r)$ of the total displacement, $r=E_{\rm stiff}/E_{\rm soft}$: sweeping the ratio gives $50\%$ at $r=1$, $91\%$ at $r=10$, $99\%$ at $r=100$, and $99.9\%$ at $r=1000$. The curve across the sweep, not one ratio, is the result.
K4 - the stress-concentration factor (sweep). A bar steps from area $A$ to $A/2$ through a fillet; sweep the fillet sharpness and compute the peak-stress factor the one-dimensional average misses. Probes: geometric stress raisers and the concentration factor; Section 2.
Solution
Equilibrium fixes the average: where the area halves the mean stress doubles, $\sigma_{\rm thin}=2\sigma_{\rm thick}$, and a one-dimensional bar model stops there. The true stress at the shoulder is $K_t$ times that thin-section nominal, and $K_t$ depends on how sharply the section changes. This module cannot derive $K_t$ - it needs a two-dimensional solution of the equilibrium equations of Section 2 around the fillet, which is a mesh, not a formula - so take the standard handbook fit $K_t\approx1+\tfrac12\sqrt{h/r}$ as an assumed relation (Appendix), with $h$ the thin-section thickness and $r$ the fillet radius. Sweeping $r/h$ gives $K_t=1.71$ for a generous fillet ($r/h=0.5$), $2.00$ at $r/h=0.25$, and $4.54$ for a sharp one ($r/h=0.02$), a factor $2.7$ across the sweep from geometry alone. So the true peak reaches about $4.5$ times the thin-section nominal, or nine times the stress in the thick section, where the one-dimensional model reports a factor of two and nothing else. The sweep, not either endpoint, is the result: it is the excess $K_t-1$ that scales as $r^{-1/2}$, so halving a fillet radius from $r/h=0.5$ to $0.25$ raises the excess by $41\%$ ($0.707\to1.000$) and the peak stress itself by $17\%$, and no radius is small enough to be safe. That peak sits at the re-entrant corner and is where cracks initiate, which is why bone stress analysis meshes the geometry instead of averaging over it, and why a surgical screw hole or a sharp osteotomy is a fracture risk out of all proportion to the material it removes.
K5 - the validity band (sensitivity). Find the strain at which the linear law departs $5\%$ from the neo-Hookean and from the fibre law. Probes: small-strain licence; Lab B.
Solution
The neo-Hookean stress stays within $5\%$ of its linear tangent out to $\approx47\%$ strain, but the exponential fibre law - tangent $k\varepsilon$, exact $e^{k\varepsilon}-1$ with $k=48$ - departs $5\%$ by $\varepsilon\approx0.1/k\approx0.2\%$. K6 - the anisotropy sweep. Probes: the double projection hidden in $\mathbf a\otimes\mathbf a$, and why a tendon loaded slightly off-axis is not slightly weaker; Section 6.
Solution
Prescribe a uniaxial strain $\boldsymbol\varepsilon=\varepsilon\,\mathbf n\otimes\mathbf n$ along the load direction $\mathbf n$, at an angle $\theta$ to the fibres $\mathbf a$. The fibre resolves it once: $\varepsilon_a=\mathbf a\cdot\boldsymbol\varepsilon\,\mathbf a=\varepsilon(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf n)^2=\varepsilon\cos^2\theta$. Proposition 6.1 turns that into $\boldsymbol\sigma_{\rm fib}=W_{\rm fib}'(\varepsilon_a)\,\mathbf a\otimes\mathbf a$, and reading the stress back along the load direction resolves it a second time: $\mathbf n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma_{\rm fib}\mathbf n=W_{\rm fib}'(\varepsilon_a)\cos^2\theta$. For a linear fibre law $W_{\rm fib}'=E_f\varepsilon_a$ the two projections compound, and with the isotropic matrix carrying $E_m$ in every direction, $$\boxed{\;E(\theta)=E_m+E_f\cos^4\theta.\;}$$ The fourth power, not the second, is the answer to the question, and it is what Fig. 7 plots. K7 - how much does a neo-Hookean solid actually stiffen? (simulation + regime comparison). Compute the uniaxial Cauchy stress and its tangent for an incompressible neo-Hookean solid over $\lambda=1$ to $1.5$, express the tangent as a percentage above its small-strain value, and use the answer to explain Lab B's finding that the linear law survives to $47\%$ strain. Probes: a nonlinear law that is barely nonlinear, and why the second-order term of a stress-stretch curve can vanish; Section 7, Lab B.
Solution
For the incompressible neo-Hookean solid the uniaxial Cauchy (true) stress is $\sigma(\lambda)=\mu(\lambda^2-1/\lambda)$, the nominal stress being $\sigma/\lambda=\mu(\lambda-1/\lambda^2)$, and the tangent is $d\sigma/d\lambda=\mu(2\lambda+1/\lambda^2)$, equal to $3\mu$ at $\lambda=1$ - the small-strain slope, matching $E=2\mu(1+\nu)=3\mu$ at $\nu=\tfrac12$. Computing it: the tangent is $0.0\%$, $0.2\%$, $0.9\%$, $3.1\%$ and $14.8\%$ above $3\mu$ at stretches $1.02$, $1.05$, $1.10$, $1.20$ and $1.50$. So the archetypal nonlinear law barely stiffens over the range a tendon ever sees; the nonlinearity is real but it is a fifteen per cent effect at a fifty per cent stretch. The reason is visible in the series. Expanding about $\lambda=1+e$, $$\frac{\sigma}{\mu}=(1+e)^2-\frac{1}{1+e}=3e+e^3-e^4+O(e^5),$$ and the quadratic term cancels exactly: the $+e^2$ from $\lambda^2$ is killed by the $-e^2$ from $-1/\lambda$. A curve whose first correction to linearity is cubic stays near its tangent for a long way, which is exactly Lab B's $47\%$: the relative departure is about $e^2/3$, so five per cent needs $e\approx0.39$, and the higher terms push the true crossing to $0.47$. Contrast the fibre law $e^{k\varepsilon}-1$, whose quadratic term is $k^2\varepsilon^2/2$ and does not cancel: it departs by $k\varepsilon/2$, five per cent at $\varepsilon\approx0.1/k$, which for $k=48$ is $0.2\%$. import numpy as np


def t_half(L, cv=1e-3, nz=40):
    """Time for the layer-averaged pore pressure to fall to one half."""
    dz = L/nz
    dt = 0.4*dz**2/cv
    p = np.ones(nz + 1)
    p[0] = 0.0
    for it in range(2000000):
        lap = np.zeros_like(p)
        lap[1:-1] = (p[2:] - 2*p[1:-1] + p[:-2])/dz**2
        lap[-1] = (2*p[-2] - 2*p[-1])/dz**2
        p = p + dt*cv*lap
        p[0] = 0.0
        if p.mean() < 0.5:
            return (it + 1)*dt
    return np.nan

K9 - design a composite bar (optimisation). Take the tapered bar of Section 5 meshed into eight equal-length elements. Four of them may be made of a stiff material ($E_{\rm stiff}=20\ \mathrm{GPa}$) and the other four of a compliant one ($E_{\rm soft}=5\ \mathrm{GPa}$); both are assumed values, and the budget is four elements, not four units of mass. Minimise the tip displacement over all $\binom84=70$ placements, and say what ranks them. Probes: compliance minimisation as a discrete search, and the difference between the objective and the sensitivity that ranks the choices; Section 5.
Solution
In series the elements carry a common force, so the tip displacement is the sum of the element extensions, $u_L=\sum_eP\ell_e/(E_eA_e)$, and this is the objective. Upgrading element $e$ from soft to stiff reduces it by $\Delta_e=P\ell_e/A_e\,(1/E_{\rm soft}-1/E_{\rm stiff})$. Note what ranks the placements: not the objective's own term $P\ell_e/(E_eA_e)$, which already contains the modulus one is choosing, but the sensitivity $\Delta_e$, which depends on the geometry alone through $\ell_e/A_e$. Since the elements are of equal length and the bar tapers, $\ell_e/A_e$ is largest at the thin end, so the four thinnest elements are the optimum, and because the $\Delta_e$ are independent the greedy choice is the global one - no search is needed, though running one confirms it. The exhaustive search over all seventy placements returns $(4,5,6,7)$, the thin half, at $0.1555\ \mathrm{mm}$; the worst is $(0,1,2,3)$, the thick half, at $0.1908\ \mathrm{mm}$; the all-soft bar gives $0.2771\ \mathrm{mm}$. Choosing where the same amount of stiff material goes is worth $18.5\%$ of the tip displacement, roughly a third of what the material itself buys. The principle - reinforce where the strain energy density is highest - is the one that puts cortical bone in a thin shell far from the neutral axis (Module 2) and carbon fibre on the tension face of a spar.

K10 - what one beam element does and does not know (regime comparison). Assemble a single beam element for a tip-loaded cantilever and compare its tip deflection with Euler-Bernoulli's $PL^3/3EI$. Then estimate the shear deflection that a Timoshenko beam would add, and sweep the slenderness $L/h$ to find where the omission stops being negligible. Say which of the two disagreements the finite element can detect by itself. Probes: exactness of a shape function, and the difference between discretisation error and modelling error; Section 4.
Solution
Fixing node 1 and solving the reduced $2\times2$ system of Proposition 4.2 for a tip load gives $w_2=PL^3/3EI$ exactly - the two agree to machine precision, difference $0\mathrm e{+}00$ - because the exact deflection of a tip-loaded cantilever is a cubic and the Hermite shape functions span the cubics, so the element makes no discretisation error at all. This is the opposite of Lab A's tapered bar, whose linear shape function cannot represent a varying strain and needs a mesh. Now the second comparison, and the point of the problem: the element cannot detect the modelling error, because it inherits it. Both the element and the formula come from the Euler-Bernoulli energy $\tfrac12\int EI(w'')^2dx$, which assumes plane sections stay normal to the axis, so both omit shear deformation and both are wrong by the same amount. Quantifying that omission needs a third model. A Timoshenko beam carries a constant shear strain $\gamma=P/(\kappa GA)$ along a tip-loaded cantilever, adding $\delta_s=\gamma L=PL/(\kappa GA)$, so the fractional correction is $\delta_s/\delta_b=3EI/(\kappa GAL^2)$; for a rectangular section of depth $h$ this is $\tfrac{E}{4\kappa G}(h/L)^2$, with the assumed shear coefficient $\kappa=5/6$ and $\nu=0.3$ giving $E/G=2(1+\nu)=2.6$. Sweeping: the shear term adds $0.20\%$ at $L/h=20$, $0.78\%$ at $10$, $3.12\%$ at $5$, $8.67\%$ at $3$ and $19.50\%$ at $2$. A femur at $L/h\approx10$ is safely slender and Module 2's beam theory is good to a per cent; a vertebral body at $L/h\approx1$ is not a beam at all and needs a three-dimensional mesh. The lesson is the general one about verification and validation: refining a mesh drives the discretisation error to zero and leaves the modelling error untouched, so a perfectly converged answer to the wrong equations is still wrong.

Five diagnostics ↑ contents

  1. Volume change. What does $J=\det\mathbf F=1$ mean physically?
    Solution
    The deformation is isochoric - volume-preserving; the deformed volume equals the reference volume, the near-incompressible limit of soft tissue.
  2. Counting constants. How many independent elastic constants has an isotropic material, and how many a transversely isotropic one?
    Solution
    Two for isotropic ($E,\nu$ or $\lambda,\mu$); five for transversely isotropic (one fibre direction).
  3. Instantaneous vs equilibrium. Which is larger, a biphasic tissue's instantaneous or equilibrium modulus?
    Solution
    The instantaneous modulus $G_0$ - the pressurised fluid stiffens the tissue before it can drain (Proposition 8.1).
  4. Element order. Why does one beam element give the exact tip deflection of a point-loaded cantilever, but many bar elements are needed for a tapered bar?
    Solution
    The exact deflection of a tip-loaded cantilever is a cubic, and the Hermite shape functions span the cubics, so one element represents it with no error; the bar's linear shape function cannot represent the varying strain of a taper, so it only converges with refinement. Exactness here is exactness against Euler-Bernoulli, not against a real beam: the element inherits that theory's neglect of shear and cannot detect it (K10).
  5. Where cracks start. Why does the 1-D bar miss the true failure site at a notch?
    Solution
    It gives only the average stress; the real peak at a re-entrant corner (the stress concentration) needs a 2-D or 3-D mesh to resolve, and that peak is where cracks initiate.
