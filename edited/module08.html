0. Motivation - why walking is cheap, but not free

Walking looks ordinary because we do it thousands of times a day. Mechanically it is strange. The body repeatedly tips forward, catches itself on the next foot, and redirects its center of mass into a new arc. If the catch is too late, the same movement becomes a fall. If the catch is perfectly timed, the movement becomes efficient locomotion.

This module turns that intuition into equations. The body is not a motorized cart that rolls horizontally at constant height. It vaults over the stance leg, exchanges kinetic and gravitational potential energy, collides into the next step, and uses muscular work to replace the energy that cannot be recovered. Walking is cheap because some energy is exchanged pendularly; it is not free because the step-to-step transition dissipates energy and must be paid for by ankle, knee, hip, trunk, and neural work.

The arc of this module. We define the gait cycle (section 1), derive the inverted-pendulum COM arc (section 2), connect GRF and COP to motion (section 3), build compass gait and transition costs (sections 4-5), estimate joint torques and powers (section 6), explain speed choice and arm swing (sections 7-8), and use the same physics-chemistry-biology stack to understand falls in older adults (section 10).

1. Gait-cycle notation and events

A gait cycle is one stride: the interval from an event on one foot to the next occurrence of the same event on that foot. A step is the interval from one foot contact to the opposite foot contact. A stride length $L_s$ is the forward distance covered in one stride; a step length $L$ is half of that distance for symmetric walking, so $L_s=2L$. The cadence $c$ is steps per unit time.

Proposition 1.1 (speed bookkeeping). For symmetric walking with step length $L$ and cadence $c$, the mean forward speed is $$v=cL.$$ If cadence is reported in steps per minute, $v=c_{\rm min}L/60$.
During one step the body advances $L$. If $c$ steps occur per second, distance per second is $cL$. The stride version is the same statement because one stride advances $2L$ and occurs at frequency $c/2$. ∎
EventMechanical meaningWhy it matters

MidstanceThe COM passes over the stance foot.Potential energy peaks; horizontal speed is near its local minimum.

SwingThe foot leaves the ground and advances.Foot clearance and timing determine trip risk.

Double support means both feet are in contact; single support means only one foot is in contact. Walking has double support. Running does not: it replaces double support with aerial flight. That difference is why this module is pendular and Module 9 becomes spring-mass.

2. The inverted-pendulum COM arc

In single support, the stance leg is well approximated as a rigid link of length $\ell$ from the contact point to the COM, an idealisation section 12 returns to. Let $\theta$ be the leg angle from vertical, positive forward. The COM coordinates relative to the stance foot are

$$x=\ell\sin\theta,\qquad y=\ell\cos\theta.$$

When $\theta=0$, the COM is highest. At the beginning or end of the step, $|\theta|$ is larger and the COM is lower. If the leg angle sweeps from $-\alpha$ to $+\alpha$, the vertical rise from step edge to midstance is exact.

Proposition 2.1 (pendular vault height). The COM rise during stance is $$\Delta h=\ell(1-\cos\alpha).$$
At midstance, $y_{\rm mid}=\ell$. At either edge, $y_{\rm edge}=\ell\cos\alpha$. Their difference is $\ell-\ell\cos\alpha$. ∎

If no work is done, the mechanical energy $E=\frac12 Mv^2+Mgy$ is nearly conserved across the middle of stance. The body slows as it rises and speeds up as it falls. This is the pendular exchange that makes walking economical, and, exactly as in Module 7, the natural rate of the leg-length pendulum is $\omega_0=\sqrt{g/\ell}$. One caution about $\ell$, because Module 7 used the same letter for a slightly different distance and got a slightly different rate. There $\ell$ was the COM height above the ankle in quiet standing, $0.9$ m, and the body was a rigid bar of moment of inertia $I\approx66\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}$ about the ankle, giving $\omega_0=\sqrt{Mg\ell/I}=3.1\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$. Here the pivot is the ground contact point, about $0.05$ m lower, and the walker is a point mass, so $\omega_0=\sqrt{g/\ell}$. The reference value used throughout this module is $\ell=0.95$ m, giving $\omega_0=3.21\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$. The two rates differ by $4\,\%$, and they differ for two reasons at once: the pivot moved, and the body is idealised differently.

Proposition 2.2 (pendular speed limit and the Froude number). Because the stance foot can only push, the ground reaction is compressive, and at midstance the vertical balance forces the speed to obey $v^2\le g\ell$. The dimensionless group $$\mathrm{Fr}=\frac{v^2}{g\ell}$$ therefore cannot exceed $1$ in pure vaulting; walking gives way to running well before that, near $\mathrm{Fr}\approx\tfrac12$.
At midstance the COM sweeps horizontally on a circular arc of radius $\ell$ about the stance foot, so it carries a centripetal acceleration $v^2/\ell$ directed downward, toward the foot. Newton's second law in the vertical direction is $$Mg-N=M\frac{v^2}{\ell},$$ with $N\ge0$ the vertical ground reaction — the foot can push up but cannot pull the body down. Solving, $N=M\!\left(g-\dfrac{v^2}{\ell}\right)$. The contact constraint $N\ge0$ then requires $v^2\le g\ell$, i.e. $\mathrm{Fr}\le1$. At $\mathrm{Fr}=1$ the ground reaction vanishes at midstance and the vault turns ballistic, so any faster gait must leave the ground — that is running (Module 9). Humans switch earlier, near $\mathrm{Fr}\approx\tfrac12$: with the module's reference $\ell=0.95$ m this is $v=\sqrt{0.5\,g\ell}=2.16\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, where the growing push-off and swing costs overtake the pendular saving. ∎
Dynamic similarity. This is a corollary of Prop 2.2, not a new claim. Because $\mathrm{Fr}=v^2/(g\ell)$ is the only dimensionless speed in the vaulting model, geometrically similar legged bodies walk alike at equal $\mathrm{Fr}$, not at equal absolute speed — a mouse, a human, and (were it pendular) a sauropod share one gait chart in Froude units. This is why $\mathrm{Fr}$, not $v$, predicts the walk–run transition across species and body sizes.

3. Ground reaction force and COP trajectory in walking

In Module 7, the center of pressure (COP) was the point inside the foot where the vertical ground reaction could be taken to act. In walking, the same point becomes a moving record of the stance foot's loading. At heel strike the COP starts near the posterior heel, travels forward along the foot during stance, and leaves near the forefoot or toes.

Let $\mathbf r_{\rm com}$ be the COM position, $M$ total body mass, and $\mathbf F_{\rm grf}$ the ground reaction force. Newton's second law gives

Proposition 3.1 (GRF is COM acceleration plus weight). During stance, if air resistance is negligible, $$\mathbf F_{\rm grf}=M\ddot{\mathbf r}_{\rm com}+Mg\mathbf e_y,$$ where $\mathbf e_y$ points upward.
The external forces on the whole body are ground reaction and weight: $\mathbf F_{\rm grf}-Mg\mathbf e_y=M\ddot{\mathbf r}_{\rm com}$. Rearranging gives the result. ∎

The COP tells where that resultant acts; the GRF tells which acceleration it produces. A force plate measures both, but the equations explain why the two are not redundant: location sets moment arms, while vector magnitude sets COM acceleration.

4. Compass gait model

The simplest walking model that has alternating feet is the compass gait model. It has two rigid legs of length $\ell$ joined at a hip mass. During single support, one leg is pinned at the stance foot and the other swings forward. At heel strike, the leading foot becomes the new stance foot and the old stance leg becomes the trailing leg. That relabeling is not bookkeeping trivia; it is where collision loss enters.

Let $\theta$ be the stance-leg angle from vertical and $\phi$ the swing-leg angle from vertical. In the simplest point-mass version, the single-support equation for the hip angle is the inverted-pendulum equation

$$\ddot\theta=\frac{g}{\ell}\sin\theta+\frac{u}{M\ell^2},$$

where $u$ is a generalized actuation term. The gravity term carries the $+\sin\theta$ sign of Module 7's unstable inverted pendulum: standing fought this toppling, walking lets it run, catching the fall on the swing leg. A passive walker sets $u=0$ and walks only if slope and collision geometry supply a stable limit cycle (section 9).

The relabeling at heel strike is where mechanical energy actually leaves the model, and it is an impulsive collision we can solve exactly.

Proposition 4.1 (heel-strike impact map). For the point-mass compass walker with inter-leg angle $2\alpha$ at contact, the instantaneous heel-strike collision scales the angular velocity by $\cos 2\alpha$: $$\dot\theta^{+}=\dot\theta^{-}\cos 2\alpha,$$ so a fraction $\sin^2 2\alpha$ of the kinetic energy is dissipated at every step.
Heel strike is instantaneous and perfectly inelastic: the leading foot sticks and becomes the new pivot while the trailing foot releases. The impulsive ground reaction acts at the new contact point, so it has no moment about that point, and the angular momentum of the hip mass about the new contact is conserved across the collision. Just before impact the mass moves at speed $v^-=\ell\dot\theta^-$ perpendicular to the trailing (old stance) leg. The new stance leg makes angle $2\alpha$ with the old one, so the direction perpendicular to the new leg is rotated by $2\alpha$ from $v^-$, and the velocity component that produces rotation about the new foot is $v^-\cos 2\alpha$. Hence the angular momentum about the new contact is $$H^-=M\ell\,(v^-\cos 2\alpha)=M\ell^2\dot\theta^-\cos 2\alpha \quad\text{before},\qquad H^+=M\ell^2\dot\theta^+\quad\text{after},$$ and conservation $H^-=H^+$ gives $\dot\theta^+=\dot\theta^-\cos 2\alpha$. The kinetic-energy ratio is $(\dot\theta^+/\dot\theta^-)^2=\cos^2 2\alpha$, so the lost fraction is $1-\cos^2 2\alpha=\sin^2 2\alpha$ — precisely the redirection loss computed energetically in section 5. ∎

5. Step-to-step transition cost

The inverted-pendulum arc saves energy within a step, but consecutive arcs point in different directions. At the end of one step the COM velocity is directed along the old arc; at the beginning of the next, the velocity must align with the new arc. The redirection costs work.

Proposition 5.1 (redirection loss and its step-length scaling). Redirecting the COM velocity of magnitude $v$ by the inter-leg angle $2\alpha$ through the inelastic heel strike dissipates $$E_{\rm loss}=\tfrac12 Mv^2\sin^2(2\alpha)\;\approx\;\tfrac12 Mv^2\Big(\frac{L}{\ell}\Big)^2\quad(\text{small }\alpha),$$ so the cost per step grows as $v^2L^2$ and the cost per unit distance as $v^2L$: long, fast steps are expensive.
By the heel-strike impact map (section 4, Prop 4.1), the collision scales the speed by $\cos 2\alpha$, so the kinetic energy drops from $\tfrac12 Mv^2$ to $\tfrac12 Mv^2\cos^2 2\alpha$ and the dissipated energy is $$E_{\rm loss}=\tfrac12 Mv^2\big(1-\cos^2 2\alpha\big)=\tfrac12 Mv^2\sin^2 2\alpha.$$ For a step of length $L$ the leg sweeps from $-\alpha$ to $+\alpha$ with $\sin\alpha=(L/2)/\ell$, so for small angles $2\alpha\approx L/\ell$ and $\sin^2 2\alpha\approx (L/\ell)^2$, giving the stated scaling. Dividing by the step length $L$ shows the cost of transport from transitions rises as $v^2L$. ∎

The redirection must be paid for somehow, but how it is paid changes the bill. The key result is that spreading the redirection over more, gentler impulses is cheaper than one hard collision.

Proposition 5.2 (why pre-emptive push-off is cheap). Let the COM velocity of magnitude $v$ be redirected through a total angle $\beta=2\alpha$ at the step-to-step transition. Two accountings must be kept apart. (a) Split collisions. If the redirection is done by $n$ equal inelastic collisions, the total dissipation is $n\cdot\tfrac12 Mv^2\sin^2(\beta/n)\approx\tfrac12 Mv^2\beta^2/n$: the loss falls only as $1/n$. (b) Push-off plus one collision. If a trailing-leg push-off supplies all but the last of the $n$ redirections as positive muscle work, and only the final heel strike dissipates, then in steady state the walker supplies exactly what that one collision destroys, $$\boxed{\;E_{\rm step}\;=\;\tfrac12 Mv^2\sin^2\!\frac{\beta}{n}\;\approx\;\frac{1}{n^{2}}\cdot\tfrac12 Mv^2\beta^2\;}$$ so the loss falls as $1/n^{2}$, not $1/n$. For the half-and-half split $n=2$ — the simplest walker, with an impulsive push-off directed along the trailing leg — the exact per-step cost is $\tfrac12 Mv^2\tan^2\alpha$, whose ratio to the collision-only cost is $$\frac{\tan^2\alpha}{\sin^2 2\alpha}=\frac{1}{4\cos^{4}\alpha}.$$ That tends to $\tfrac14$ as $\alpha\to0$, which is the classic one-quarter, but it equals $0.330$ at this module's reference half-angle $\alpha=21.1^\circ$.
For (a), one inelastic redirection through an angle $\psi$ destroys the velocity component normal to the new arc, $v\sin\psi$, and therefore $\tfrac12 Mv^2\sin^2\psi$ of kinetic energy (Prop 5.1). Doing the whole angle $\beta$ in $n$ equal collisions costs $n\cdot\tfrac12 Mv^2\sin^2(\beta/n)\approx\tfrac12 Mv^2\beta^2/n$: $\sin^2$ is quadratic in a small angle, and there are $n$ of them, so exactly one factor of $n$ survives. For (b) the bookkeeping is different, because a push-off is not a collision. It is an impulse directed along the trailing leg, perpendicular to the COM velocity it acts on, so it adds energy instead of removing it. Over a periodic step the mechanical energy must return to its starting value, so the positive work the push-off supplies equals what the single remaining heel strike destroys, $\tfrac12 Mv^2\sin^2(\beta/n)$. That is one term, not $n$ of them, so the squared angle keeps its full $1/n^{2}$. The $n=2$ case can be done exactly, and it is worth doing, because the exact answer is not the textbook one. Let the pre-transition speed be $v^-$, perpendicular to the trailing leg. A push-off impulse along that leg turns the velocity through an angle $\varphi$ and raises its magnitude to $v^-/\cos\varphi$; the heel strike then turns it through the remaining $2\alpha-\varphi$ and multiplies it by $\cos(2\alpha-\varphi)$. A periodic gait needs the speed to return to $v^-$, which forces $\cos(2\alpha-\varphi)=\cos\varphi$, that is $\varphi=\alpha$. Steady state chooses the half-and-half split; it is not an assumption. The surviving dissipation is then $\tfrac12 M(v^-/\cos\alpha)^2\sin^2\alpha=\tfrac12 M(v^-)^2\tan^2\alpha$, whose ratio to the collision-only $\tfrac12 M(v^-)^2\sin^2 2\alpha$ is $\tan^2\alpha/\sin^2 2\alpha=1/(4\cos^{4}\alpha)$. At this module's own reference step ($2\alpha=42.2^\circ$, $v=1.3\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, $M=70$ kg) the collision-only cost is $26.7$ J per step and the split cost is $8.8$ J, a ratio of $0.330$. The real saving is a factor of $3.03$, not the textbook $4$, because $42^\circ$ is not a small angle. ∎

This is why ankle power in late stance (section 6) is not merely propulsion: it is collision management, timed just before the opposite heel strikes so the transition costs as little as the geometry allows.

6. Joint torques and joint powers

Inverse dynamics estimates the net joint torques required to produce measured motion under measured external forces — cashing the promise made in Module 3 that measured segment inertias would later drive a gait torque estimate.

Proposition 6.1 (segmental inverse dynamics). For a planar body segment of moment of inertia $I$ and angular acceleration $\ddot\theta$, acted on by a known external moment $M_{\rm ext}$ (gravity, the ground reaction, and the already-solved distal joint), the net proximal joint torque is $$\boxed{\;\tau=I\ddot\theta-M_{\rm ext}\;}.$$ Applied foot-first up the chain, it yields the ankle, knee, and hip torques in turn.
Euler's rotational equation for the segment is $I\ddot\theta=\sum M=\tau+M_{\rm ext}$, the sum of the proximal joint torque $\tau$ and every other moment $M_{\rm ext}$; solving gives $\tau=I\ddot\theta-M_{\rm ext}$. Because each segment's distal end carries the joint reaction of the segment below it, the recursion starts at the free (foot) end, where the only external load is the measured ground reaction of section 3, and each solved joint supplies the $M_{\rm ext}$ for the next segment up. ∎

Solving foot-first up the chain gives the ankle, knee, and hip moments. The power at joint $j$ is then a signed rate of work.

Joint power. If $\tau_j$ is the net internal joint torque and $\dot\theta_j$ is the joint angular velocity, $$P_j=\tau_j\dot\theta_j.$$ Positive $P_j$ means the joint is doing net mechanical work on the body; negative $P_j$ means the joint is absorbing work.

Walking has a recognizable power signature: the ankle produces a large positive burst in late stance, the knee absorbs energy after heel strike and during swing control, and the hip alternates between powering the trunk and controlling limb motion.

Worked number: the peak hip reaction in walking

Module 3 left this module a specific debt. Its equation (4.1) put the hip joint reaction in static single-leg stance at $R=W_s(1+b/a)$, where $W_s=\tfrac56 W$ is the body weight less the swinging leg, $b\approx0.10$ m is the horizontal distance from the femoral head to the supported centre of mass, and $a\approx0.05$ m is the hip abductor moment arm. With $W=Mg=686.7$ N that is $R=2.5\,W=1717$ N standing still. Module 3 then stated that gait raises the peak to $3$ to $5\,W$ and pointed here for the reason. Here is the reason, and here is how much of that band a planar walking model can honestly reach.

The frontal-plane balance itself is unchanged by walking; what changes is the load it balances. By Prop 3.1 the vertical ground reaction is $F_{{\rm grf},y}=M(g+\ddot y_{\rm com})$, so the supported weight $W_s$ in Module 3's equation is multiplied by the instantaneous vertical ground reaction expressed in body weights, $F_{{\rm grf},y}/W$. This module supplies two values of that ratio, and neither is asserted.

The midstance trough is derived. Prop 2.2 already contains it: $N=M(g-v^2/\ell)$, so $F_{{\rm grf},y}/W=1-v^2/(g\ell)=1-\mathrm{Fr}$. At the reference gait $\mathrm{Fr}=0.181$, so the ratio is $0.819$, that is $562$ N. Vaulting unloads the stance leg at midstance, and it does so by exactly the Froude number.

The transition peak follows from the redirection impulse. At the step-to-step transition the COM's vertical velocity reverses from $-v\sin\alpha$ to $+v\sin\alpha$, so the two feet together must deliver a vertical impulse $2Mv\sin\alpha$ over and above body weight. For the reference gait ($\alpha=21.1^\circ$, $T=1.053$ s, and a double-support fraction $2\times62-100=24\,\%$ of the stride split into two periods of $0.126$ s), that impulse is $65.6\ \mathrm{N\,s}$ and the mean excess force is $519$ N, so the two feet together carry $1.76\,W$ through double support. The two ratios are consistent with periodicity: weighting each by its duration gives $0.76\times0.819+0.24\times1.756=1.044$, and a periodic stride must average exactly $1$, so the two independent routes close to $4.4\,\%$ — about what using the midstance value as a stand-in for the whole single-support phase should cost.

How that $1.76\,W$ divides between the trailing and the leading limb is a measurement question, forwarded to Module 15. Assume the leading limb's peak share is $1.10$ to $1.20\,W$, the range force plates report.

Peak hip joint reaction in level walking. Scale Module 3's (4.1) by the peak vertical ground reaction, and use Module 3's honest resultant of $2.6$ to $2.8\,W$ (which accounts for the abductors' $30^\circ$ line of pull) rather than its vertical-only $2.5\,W$: $$R_{\rm peak}\;\approx\;(1.10\text{ to }1.20)\times(2.6\text{ to }2.8)\,W\;=\;2.9\text{ to }3.4\,W.$$ The debt is paid, and paid at its true size. The estimate lands at the bottom of the $3$ to $5\,W$ band Module 3 quoted for walking and running together, and it overlaps the $2.3$ to $2.9\,W$ that Module 3 reports from in-vivo telemetry in slow level walking. The upper half of that band belongs to running and stair descent, which this planar walking model does not cover. What the calculation shows is where the load comes from: not from walking being violent — the peak ground reaction is only $10$ to $20\,\%$ above body weight — but from the abductors working at half the lever arm of the load they balance.

Two links in that chain are assumptions, and are labelled as such: the lever ratio $b/a=2$ is Module 3's anthropometry, and the peak ground-reaction share $1.10$ to $1.20\,W$ is a measured range this module does not derive. Everything else — the $0.819\,W$ midstance trough, the $65.6\ \mathrm{N\,s}$ redirection impulse, the $4.4\,\%$ periodicity check — follows from Props 2.2, 3.1 and 5.1.

7. Speed, cadence, stride length, and cost

A walker can increase speed by increasing cadence, increasing step length, or both. Neither is free. Long steps increase the redirection angle $\alpha$ and hence the collision cost of section 5 ($\propto v^2\sin^2 2\alpha$). Very high cadence increases swing work, muscle activation frequency, and the joint work of section 6. Preferred walking speed emerges from this tradeoff, and the cost of transport it minimises is the observable of the second lab in section 11.

The cost of a step has two competing parts, and writing both down at once is what forces an optimum. Fix a reference gait first, because the rest of this module and every computational problem will use it: a walker of mass $M=70$ kg with contact-to-COM pendulum length $\ell=0.95$ m, walking at $v=1.3\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$.

A minimal proxy for the mechanical cost of one step is

$$C(v,L)=\underbrace{\tfrac12 Mv^2\sin^2\!\left(2\arcsin\frac{L}{2\ell}\right)}_{\text{transition}}\;+\;\underbrace{\kappa M\left(\frac{v}{L}\right)^2}_{\text{swing}},$$

where the first term is exactly the step-to-step transition loss of Prop 5.1, rewritten in terms of step length through $\sin\alpha=L/2\ell$, and the second charges the walker for swinging the leg at cadence $c=v/L$, a driven pendulum whose work rises as the square of its frequency. There is exactly one free constant, the swing-cost coefficient $\kappa$, which has units of area. It is the one number in this section that is assumed rather than derived: take $\kappa=0.09\ \mathrm{m^2}$, so that $\kappa M=6.3\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}$ reads as an effective swing inertia. A speed-independent baseline may be added to $C$; it shifts every value equally and moves no optimum, so it is dropped from here on.

Minimising $C$ over $L$ at the reference gait puts the optimum at $L^{\ast}=0.684$ m, where the transition term is $26.7$ J per step and the swing term $22.7$ J, a total of $49.4$ J. The step half-angle there is $\alpha=\arcsin(L^{\ast}/2\ell)=21.1^\circ$, the cadence is $c^{\ast}=v/L^{\ast}=1.90$ steps s$^{-1}$ or $114$ steps per minute, and the stride period is $T=2L^{\ast}/v=1.053$ s. Those numbers are the reference gait, Lab 2 of section 11 is the code that produces them, and section 12 quotes no others.

This proxy is not a metabolic model and should not be read as one. Its purpose is to make the optimum unavoidable: one cannot minimise transition cost and swing cost at the same step length, because the first rises with $L$ and the second falls with it. The optimum is also flat, and slightly asymmetric — a $20\,\%$ longer step costs $4.6\,\%$ more, a $20\,\%$ shorter step costs $8.2\,\%$ more. That is why a walker can drift a long way from $L^{\ast}$ without feeling a penalty, and why shortening the step, which a tired or cautious walker does, is punished faster than lengthening it.

8. Arm swing and whole-body angular momentum

Arm swing is often described as passive, but passive does not mean irrelevant. The legs exchange angular momentum with the trunk on every step, and if the arms did not swing the trunk would have to absorb it. That claim deserves a torque rather than an adjective, and this section produces one: about $10$ N m of trunk yaw torque with the arms held still, against $6.8$ N m with them swinging. Arm swing is a low-cost moving counterweight, and the cost it saves can be counted.

Proposition 8.1 (angular-momentum bookkeeping). About the whole-body COM, the angular momentum satisfies $$\dot{\mathbf H}_G=\sum \mathbf M_G^{\rm ext}.$$ If external moments about the COM are small over part of the stride, arm and leg angular momenta must approximately cancel within the body.
This is Euler's angular-momentum balance for the whole body about its COM. With small net external moment, $\dot{\mathbf H}_G\approx0$, so internal segment angular momenta trade against each other. ∎

That bookkeeping has a size, and it is worth computing instead of gesturing at. About the body's vertical axis, a segment of mass $m$ at mediolateral offset $y$ moving fore-aft at $v_x$ relative to the trunk carries $H_z=-m\,y\,v_x$. The two legs are antiphase and on opposite sides of the midline, so their contributions carry the same sign and add: with a leg mass $m_{\rm leg}=0.161M=11.3$ kg at an offset $d_{\rm leg}=0.09$ m moving at $1.3\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, $|H_{\rm legs}|=2.64\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2\,s^{-1}}$. Each arm swings opposite its ipsilateral leg, so the arms subtract: with $m_{\rm arm}=0.050M=3.5$ kg at $d_{\rm arm}=0.20$ m moving at $0.6\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, $|H_{\rm arms}|=0.84\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2\,s^{-1}}$, cancelling $32\,\%$ of the legs' yaw momentum. The three segment masses and the two offsets are assumed, from standard segment fractions; nothing else here is.

The residual $1.80\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2\,s^{-1}}$ must reverse sign twice per stride, so by Prop 8.1 the trunk and hip muscles must supply a mean yaw torque of $2|H_z|/(T/2)$ over each half stride of $0.527$ s: $10.0$ N m with the arms held still, against $6.8$ N m with them swinging. That is the number behind “tying the arms down changes the gait”. The arms are light — five percent of body mass each against sixteen for a leg — but they act at more than twice the moment arm, and that is the whole trade. K8 rebuilds the estimate and tests how hard it leans on the two assumed offsets.

9. Passive dynamic walking and stability

A passive dynamic walker can walk downhill with no motors. It is not violating energy conservation. Gravity supplies the energy lost at each collision, and the walker settles into a stable limit cycle: one stride maps the state back to itself.

Let $\mathbf z_n=(\theta_n,\dot\theta_n)$ be the state just after heel strike on step $n$. One stride defines a map

$$\mathbf z_{n+1}=\mathcal P(\mathbf z_n;\gamma),$$

where $\gamma$ is the slope angle. A periodic gait is a fixed point $\mathbf z^*=\mathcal P(\mathbf z^*;\gamma)$, and its local stability is determined by the eigenvalues of the Jacobian $D\mathcal P(\mathbf z^*)$. If their magnitudes are less than one, nearby steps converge back to the gait — the walker recovers from a small disturbance with no control at all. We do not need the full map to find which slope sustains the gait, though: an energy balance fixes it.

Proposition 9.1 (passive limit-cycle energy balance). A periodic compass gait on a slope $\gamma$ requires the gravitational energy released per step to equal the heel-strike loss, $$MgL\sin\gamma=\tfrac12 Mv^2\sin^2 2\alpha,$$ which for small angles selects a mid-stance speed $v^2\approx g\ell\,\dfrac{\sin\gamma}{\alpha}$: faster on steeper slopes, and impossible on the level.
Over one step the configuration returns to itself, so the mechanical energy is unchanged step to step. Only two things change it: gravity does positive work $MgL\sin\gamma$ as the COM descends the slope by $L\sin\gamma$ over a step of length $L$, and the heel strike dissipates $E_{\rm loss}=\tfrac12 Mv^2\sin^2 2\alpha$ (section 5, Prop 5.1). Periodicity demands they balance, $MgL\sin\gamma=\tfrac12 Mv^2\sin^2 2\alpha$. Substituting $L\approx2\ell\alpha$ and $\sin 2\alpha\approx2\alpha$ gives $Mg\,(2\ell\alpha)\sin\gamma=\tfrac12 Mv^2(2\alpha)^2$, hence $v^2\approx g\ell\sin\gamma/\alpha$. For the module's reference $\ell=0.95$ m, a step half-angle $\alpha=0.30$ rad and a gentle slope $\gamma=0.05$ rad ($2.87^\circ$), this gives $v=1.25\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, a realistic walking speed from gravity alone. The two small-angle substitutions just made are doing real work at $2\alpha=34.4^\circ$: K9 solves the same energy balance without them and gets $1.31\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, $5.2\,\%$ higher. On the level ($\gamma=0$) the only solution is $v=0$: gravity supplies nothing, so the losses cannot be replaced and steady walking must inject exactly $\tfrac12 Mv^2\sin^2 2\alpha$ per step by active push-off. ∎
Passive walking lesson. A gait can be mechanically self-stabilizing over a finite basin (the Jacobian eigenvalues sit inside the unit circle), and on a slope gravity even pays the running cost — but level-ground human walking still needs muscles: without slope or actuation, the heel-strike loss of Prop 5.1 drains the energy and the walker halts.

10. When walking fails: older-adult falls and post-fall decline

A fall is not simply "bad balance." For older adults, the causal stack is best read from the bottom upward: chemistry/materials set the tissue and signaling substrate; biology turns that substrate into sensing, force, reaction-time, and control capacity; physics is the visible gait event when the available control margin is no longer enough for the perturbation. Physics is therefore not separate from aging biology and chemistry, but it is still the layer that decides the actual motion, impact energy, and contact path. After the fall, biology and chemistry decide whether recovery restores function or whether pain, inflammation, fear, and unloading drive decline.

Chemistry/materials layer: the aging substrate

Chemistry helps explain both why the fall becomes more likely and why it becomes more injurious. Low vitamin D and disturbed calcium-parathyroid hormone balance can reduce muscle performance and bone mineralization. Chronic low-grade inflammation promotes catabolism, worsens muscle quality, and biases remodeling away from robust tissue maintenance. Diabetes-related glycation and microvascular injury can damage peripheral nerves, degrading sensation before the fall. Sedatives, anticholinergics, antihypertensives, alcohol, dehydration, and electrolyte disturbances alter receptor signaling, alertness, blood pressure regulation, and reaction time. At impact, the same chemistry/material state sets the failure threshold: bone mineral density, trabecular architecture, collagen cross-link quality, and remodeling balance determine whether a hip, wrist, vertebra, or skull survives the mechanical energy delivered by the fall.

Biology layer: the weakened control system

The pre-fall biology is the loss of reserve in the control loop. Sarcopenia reduces muscle cross-sectional area, especially fast force-producing capacity, so the corrective step and hip strategy develop too little impulse too late. Motor-unit remodeling makes activation coarser; tendon and muscle stiffness changes reduce power transfer; vestibular, visual, and proprioceptive signals become noisier; peripheral neuropathy makes the foot-ground state less certain; and executive load matters because walking while turning, talking, or scanning a curb is a dual-task control problem. The result is not merely "weakness." It is a slower, noisier, lower-authority feedback controller operating with less mechanical margin.

Physics layer: the visible fall event

The physics question is whether the extrapolated COM can be brought back inside a recoverable base of support before the body rotates or translates too far. Older adults often walk with shorter steps and lower speed as a rational stability strategy, but several variables shrink the recovery margin at once: reduced toe clearance increases trip probability; slower reaction time delays corrective stepping; weaker hip abductors and ankle plantarflexors reduce the impulse that can redirect the COM; and impaired sensation delays detection of where the foot and ground are. Each of those shrinks a margin that Prop 10.1 makes explicit and Lab 4 measures: at the reference perturbation below, the recoverable margin is $0.155$ m, and a $0.20$ s reaction delay alone spends $0.090$ m of it.

Proposition 10.1 (fall-margin inequality). Model the body between foot contacts as the linear inverted pendulum of Module 7, with $\omega_0=\sqrt{g/\ell}$. A single step to a new contact point $x_p$ arrests a forward perturbation if and only if that contact is placed at or beyond the extrapolated centre of mass and is within reach: $$\boxed{\;x_{\rm com}+\frac{\dot x_{\rm com}}{\omega_0}\;\le\;x_p\;\le\;x_s^{\max}\;}$$ where $x_s^{\max}$ is the farthest contact the walker can actually reach. A perturbation is unrecoverable in one step exactly when the extrapolated centre of mass runs past the feasible step reach.
Take the new contact as the origin, forward positive, so $x_{\rm com}(0)\lt0$ at the instant the foot lands. Module 7's linearised inverted pendulum gives $\ddot x_{\rm com}=\omega_0^2\,x_{\rm com}$, whose general solution is $x_{\rm com}(t)=Ae^{\omega_0 t}+Be^{-\omega_0 t}$ with $A=\tfrac12\big(x_{\rm com}(0)+\dot x_{\rm com}(0)/\omega_0\big)$ and $B=\tfrac12\big(x_{\rm com}(0)-\dot x_{\rm com}(0)/\omega_0\big)$. With the COM behind the new foot and moving forward, $B\lt0$; the second term decays and never carries the COM forward, so the sign of $A$ decides everything. Both exponentials are positive for all $t$, so $x_{\rm com}(t)\lt0$ for every $t\gt0$ — the body never passes forward of the new contact — if and only if $A\le0$, that is $x_{\rm com}(0)+\dot x_{\rm com}(0)/\omega_0\le0$. ($A=0$ is the marginal capture, the body arriving over the foot with exactly zero speed; $A\lt0$ means it rocks back short of vertical, which the next step deals with.) Restoring the original origin, the contact must satisfy $x_p\ge x_{\rm com}+\dot x_{\rm com}/\omega_0$. The quantity $\xi=x_{\rm com}+\dot x_{\rm com}/\omega_0$ is Module 7's extrapolated centre of mass, and its Prop 4.2 shows that $\xi$ obeys the first-order law $\dot\xi=\omega_0(\xi-x_{\rm cop})$: put the pressure point beyond $\xi$ and $\xi$ falls back, put it short and $\xi$ runs away. A step can only put the pressure point where the foot can reach, so a one-step recovery exists if and only if $\xi\le x_s^{\max}$. ∎

Aging attacks both sides of that single inequality, and the inequality says which attack costs more. It raises the left side through reaction delay: during a delay $\Delta$ the COM drifts a further $\dot x_{\rm com}\Delta$ before the foot moves, so the requirement becomes $x_{\rm com}+\dot x_{\rm com}\Delta+\dot x_{\rm com}/\omega_0\le x_s^{\max}$. It lowers the right side through hip range, ankle strength, and the confidence to commit to a long step. Take the reference perturbation of Lab 4 — $x_{\rm com}=0.03$ m, $\dot x_{\rm com}=0.45\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, $\Delta=0.20$ s, $\ell=0.95$ m so $\omega_0=3.21\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$. The capture point sits at $0.030+0.090+0.140=0.260$ m, and against a reachable step of $0.415$ m the margin is $0.155$ m. K10 computes which of the three variables spends that margin fastest, and the answer is not the one clinical intuition picks.

Biology after the fall: why health often declines afterward

The post-fall decline is not psychological weakness. It is a coupled biological cascade. Pain reduces loading; unloading accelerates sarcopenia and bone loss; fear of falling narrows activity; less activity worsens cardiovascular reserve, insulin sensitivity, balance calibration, and muscle power; hospitalization adds sleep disruption, inflammation, infection risk, and rapid deconditioning. The next fall then becomes more likely. A single mechanical event can become a systems-biology transition to a lower functional state.

Lab 2: transition-cost optimization

12. Problems, diagnostics, and limitations

Conceptual problems

C1. Identify which part of a walking step is controlled falling and which part is catching. Probes: mechanism before terminology.
Solution
The fall is the descent after midstance toward the next contact; the catch is the leading-foot collision and loading response that redirects the COM. The split is not merely descriptive: the falling half is nearly conservative and costs almost nothing, while the catching half is where Prop 5.1's $26.7$ J per step leaves the system. Walking is cheap in the half most people would call dangerous.
C2. Explain why walking has double support but running has flight. Probes: gait classification.
Solution
Walking keeps at least one foot on the ground and has a double-support exchange. Running has an aerial interval, so its mechanics are spring-mass rather than inverted-pendulum dominated.
C3. Why is midstance not the most energetically expensive part of walking? Probes: exchange versus loss.
Solution
In ideal pendular motion, kinetic and potential energy exchange at midstance with little net loss. The expensive part is redirecting velocity between steps. The reference gait makes the contrast numerical: the vault exchanges $Mg\Delta h=43.8$ J each way (Lab 1), while the transition destroys $26.7$ J (Prop 5.1). Exchange is larger than loss, and only the loss has to be paid for.
C4. Why does the COP move from heel to toe in stance? Probes: foot loading.
Solution
As load transfers from heel strike through midfoot to forefoot push-off, the pressure-weighted average of contact pressure moves forward.
C5. Explain why long steps can be costly even if cadence is low. Probes: transition angle.
Solution
Longer steps increase the angle between successive inverted-pendulum arcs, increasing collision/redirection loss.
C6. Why does ankle push-off reduce heel-strike loss? Probes: timing of work.
Solution
It redirects the COM before the leading-leg collision, leaving a smaller incompatible velocity component for heel strike to dissipate. Because the loss goes as the square of the angle destroyed, halving the angle quarters the residual, and the per-step cost of the reference gait falls from $26.7$ J to $8.8$ J (Prop 5.2). Timing is the whole mechanism: the same impulse delivered after heel strike would restore the energy, not prevent its loss.
C7. What does positive joint power mean? Probes: sign convention.
Solution
Positive $P=\tau\dot\theta$ means the joint's net torque adds mechanical energy; negative power means it absorbs energy.
C8. Why can arm swing reduce walking cost? Probes: angular momentum.
Solution
The arms swing opposite the legs, reducing trunk angular momentum that muscles would otherwise need to control.
C9. Why is passive dynamic walking not free on level ground? Probes: energy source.
Solution
The passive walker relies on downhill gravitational work. On level ground, muscles must replace heel-strike losses. On the slope of section 9 ($\gamma=0.05$ rad) the descent supplies $MgL\sin\gamma=19.3$ J per step, and the collision destroys exactly that; set $\gamma=0$ and the supply is zero, so Prop 9.1's balance has only $v=0$ to offer.
C10. Why do older-adult falls require physics, chemistry, and biology together? Probes: stack reasoning.
Solution
Physics explains loss of stability and impact; chemistry/materials explain fracture and tissue tolerance; biology explains sarcopenia, fear, inflammation, and deconditioning after the event.

Derivational problems

D1. Derive $v=cL$ for symmetric walking. Probes: gait bookkeeping.
Solution
Each step advances $L$ and there are $c$ steps per second, so speed is distance/time $v=cL$.
D2. Derive $\Delta h=\ell(1-\cos\alpha)$ for an inverted-pendulum stance arc. Probes: COM geometry.
Solution
The COM height is $\ell\cos\theta$; compare edge $\theta=\alpha$ to midstance $\theta=0$.
D3. Derive the Froude number by nondimensionalizing $v^2$ with $g\ell$. Probes: dynamic similarity.
Solution
$g\ell$ has units $\mathrm{m^2s^{-2}}$, the same as $v^2$, so $v^2/(g\ell)$ is the natural dimensionless speed.
D4. Starting from Newton's law, derive $\mathbf F_{\rm grf}=M\ddot{\mathbf r}_{\rm com}+Mg\mathbf e_y$. Probes: whole-body force balance.
Solution
Sum external forces: $\mathbf F_{\rm grf}-Mg\mathbf e_y=M\ddot{\mathbf r}_{\rm com}$, then rearrange.
D5. Derive the compass-gait relabeling condition at heel strike. Probes: event maps.
Solution
The old swing foot becomes the new stance pivot, so pre-impact swing-leg angle becomes post-impact stance-leg angle with sign convention changed; velocities map through the impact constraint.
D6. Derive the redirection loss $\frac12 Mv^2\sin^2(2\alpha)$. Probes: collision geometry.
Solution
The component perpendicular to the new admissible tangent is $v\sin(2\alpha)$; an inelastic collision removes its kinetic energy.
D7. Derive $P_j=\tau_j\dot\theta_j$ from work rate. Probes: rotational power.
Solution
Rotational work is $dW=\tau_j d\theta_j$. Divide by $dt$ to get $P_j=\tau_j\dot\theta_j$.
D8. Derive a first-order condition for the optimal step length in the cost proxy of section 7, $C(L)=\tfrac12 Mv^2\sin^2(2\arcsin(L/2\ell))+\kappa M(v/L)^2$. Probes: optimum, not substitution.
Solution
Write $\alpha=\arcsin(L/2\ell)$, so $d\alpha/dL=1/(2\ell\cos\alpha)$. Then $$\frac{dC}{dL}=\tfrac12 Mv^2\cdot 2\sin 2\alpha\cos 2\alpha\cdot\frac{2}{2\ell\cos\alpha}-\frac{2\kappa Mv^2}{L^3},$$ and setting $dC/dL=0$ gives the first-order condition $$\frac{\sin 2\alpha\,\cos 2\alpha}{\ell\cos\alpha}=\frac{2\kappa}{L^{3}}.$$ Two features are worth reading off before any number is put in. First, $Mv^2$ divides out of both sides, so $L^{\ast}$ does not depend on speed at all in this proxy — a strong and testable prediction, and the reason cadence rather than step length carries most of a speed change. Second, the left side rises from zero with $L$ while the right side falls as $L^{-3}$, so exactly one interior root exists for $\kappa\gt0$, and it is a minimum. At $\ell=0.95$ m and $\kappa=0.09\ \mathrm{m^2}$ that root is $L^{\ast}=0.684$ m.
D9. Derive the capture inequality $x_{\rm com}+\dot x_{\rm com}/\omega_0\le x_s^{\max}$ from the linear inverted pendulum, rather than quoting it. Probes: fall margin.
Solution
Solve $\ddot x=\omega_0^2 x$ about the new contact: $x(t)=Ae^{\omega_0t}+Be^{-\omega_0t}$ with $A=\tfrac12(x_0+\dot x_0/\omega_0)$. The decaying mode can never carry the COM forward past the foot, so boundedness is decided entirely by the sign of $A$, and the body stays behind the contact if and only if $A\le0$. That single inequality is the divergent-component condition, and $\xi=x+\dot x/\omega_0$ is Module 7's extrapolated centre of mass. A step can only place the contact within reach, so the recovery exists exactly when $\xi\le x_s^{\max}$. Prop 10.1 of section 10 writes the argument out in full; the point of doing it here is that the “divergent component” is not a definition to be recited but the coefficient of the growing exponential in a two-line solution.
D10. Show why passive downhill walking needs slope work to equal collision loss over a cycle. Probes: limit-cycle energy.
Solution
On a periodic gait, total mechanical energy repeats each step. Therefore gravitational work from descent plus actuator work must equal impact and frictional losses. Passive means actuator work is zero, so slope work pays the losses.

Computational problems

K1. Integrate the rigid-vault stance arc for $\ell=0.95$ m at a midstance speed of $1.3\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, using energy conservation $v(\theta)^2=v_{\rm mid}^2+2g\ell(1-\cos\theta)$ and the radial force balance to get the vertical ground reaction $F_y(\theta)=M\cos\theta\,\big(g\cos\theta-v(\theta)^2/\ell\big)$. Plot $F_y/W$ across stance for $\alpha=12^\circ,16^\circ,20^\circ$, then compare its shape with the double-humped force-plate record Lab 3 assumes. What is the model wrong about, and by how much? Probes: a model's most visible failure, not its successes.
Solution

The rigid vault predicts a single hump with its maximum at midstance and its minima at the step edges — the exact inverse of what is measured. At all three angles $F_y$ peaks at $\theta=0$ with $F_y/W=1-\mathrm{Fr}=0.819$, and falls to $0.737$, $0.675$ and $0.599\,W$ at the edges for $\alpha=12^\circ,16^\circ,20^\circ$ (and to $0.576\,W$ at the reference $\alpha=21.1^\circ$). Real walking peaks near $1.10\,W$ at $28\,\%$ and $72\,\%$ of stance and troughs at $0.75\,W$ at midstance. The model gets the midstance value nearly right ($0.819$ against $0.75$), gets the peaks qualitatively backwards, and never exceeds body weight at all.

The reason sits in the two factors of $F_y$. Energy conservation makes the COM fastest at the step edges, so the centripetal term $v^2/\ell$ is largest exactly there and unloads the leg most; and the $\cos\theta$ projection then removes more of an already smaller radial force. A rigid leg has no way to push harder at the edges. The measured peaks come from the two things the rigid vault deletes: the leading limb's collision at heel strike and the trailing limb's push-off. So the vault's vertical-force error is not a detail to patch. It is the step-to-step transition of section 5, showing up as the part of the force record the model cannot draw.

Sweeping $v_{\rm mid}$ upward at the reference angle, the edge value falls but stays positive: $+0.576\,W$ at $1.3$, $+0.344\,W$ at $2.0$, $+0.278\,W$ at the $2.16\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ walk–run speed, and $+0.119\,W$ at $2.5\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. Prop 2.2 derives its $\mathrm{Fr}\le1$ ceiling at midstance, but along this arc the edges approach zero first, so a real vaulting walker would lose ground contact at the step edges before it does at midstance.

K2. Sweep walking speed from $0.6$ to $2.0\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ and compute $\mathrm{Fr}$ for leg lengths $0.8$, $0.95$ and $1.1$ m. Then invert the relationship: a gait laboratory measures a walk–run transition speed of $2.1\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ for one subject and $1.9\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ for another, and reports no anthropometry. Recover each subject's effective pendulum length, say what assumption you had to make, and quantify how far the answer moves if that assumption is wrong by $\pm0.1$ in $\mathrm{Fr}$. Probes: dynamic similarity read backwards, as a measurement.
Solution

Forward: at $v=1.3\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, $\mathrm{Fr}=0.215,\,0.181,\,0.157$ for $\ell=0.8,\,0.95,\,1.1$ m, and the speeds at which each reaches $\mathrm{Fr}=0.5$ are $1.98$, $2.16$ and $2.32\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. The short-legged walker sits at a higher Froude number for the same absolute speed and so reaches the walk–run range sooner, which is why children run at speeds adults walk.

Backward: assuming the transition happens at a fixed $\mathrm{Fr}^{\ast}=0.5$, $\ell=v_{\rm tr}^2/(\mathrm{Fr}^{\ast}g)$, giving $\ell=0.899$ m for the $2.1\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ subject and $0.736$ m for the $1.9\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ one. The assumption is that $\mathrm{Fr}^{\ast}$ is a constant of the gait and not of the person — which is exactly what dynamic similarity claims and exactly what such a measurement was supposed to test, so the inversion is only as good as that circularity allows. Sensitivity: since $\ell\propto1/\mathrm{Fr}^{\ast}$, an error of $\pm0.1$ in $\mathrm{Fr}^{\ast}$ moves $\ell$ by $-16.7\,\%$ and $+25.0\,\%$, so the recovered length for the first subject spans $0.749$ to $1.124$ m. That band is wider than the difference between the two subjects, and that is the real lesson: Froude scaling predicts how gaits map onto one another, but it is a poor instrument for recovering a length from a single speed.

K3. Optimise step length in the cost proxy of section 7 at $v=1.3\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, then sweep $\kappa$ over $0.05$ to $0.15\ \mathrm{m^2}$ and report how far $L^{\ast}$ moves. Is the predicted step length a robust output of the model or an artefact of the one assumed constant? Probes: optimisation, then the sensitivity of the optimum to its assumption.
Solution
Evaluate the proxy on a grid of feasible $L$ at fixed $v$, or solve $dC/dL=0$ numerically (D8). With $M=70$ kg, $\ell=0.95$ m, $\kappa=0.09\ \mathrm{m^2}$ and $v=1.3\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, the grid minimum is $L^{\ast}=0.684$ m, where the transition term is $26.7$ J and the swing term $22.7$ J; the cadence is $c^{\ast}=v/L^{\ast}=1.90$ steps per second, or $114$ steps per minute. Too-long steps raise transition loss as $\sin^2 2\alpha$; too-short steps raise swing cost as $1/L^2$. The optimum is flat and asymmetric: $L$ raised by $20\,\%$ costs $4.6\,\%$ more, lowered by $20\,\%$ costs $8.2\,\%$ more, which is why a walker can change step length noticeably without feeling the penalty. Sweeping the assumed constant, $L^{\ast}=0.577$ m at $\kappa=0.05\ \mathrm{m^2}$ and $0.807$ m at $\kappa=0.15\ \mathrm{m^2}$: a threefold change in the assumption moves the prediction by $40\,\%$. The first-order condition of D8 explains the number — for small $\alpha$ it reduces to $L^{4}\approx2\kappa\ell^{2}$, so $L^{\ast}\propto\kappa^{1/4}$. That fourth root is what makes the model useful and also what limits the claim: the predicted step length is weakly, not insensitively, tied to the one assumed constant, so the agreement with the $0.7$ m adults actually use is partly calibration and should be reported as such. The cost at the optimum is not robust at all, since the swing term scales directly with $\kappa$. One numerical caution before running the grid: the transition term is $\sin^2 2\alpha$, which turns over at $\alpha=45^\circ$ and falls back to zero as $L\to2\ell=1.9$ m, so a grid extended past $L\approx1.34$ m finds a second, spurious minimum at its own right-hand edge. Restrict the search to $L\lt2\ell\sin45^\circ$.
K4. Sweep the fraction $f\in[0,1)$ of the step-to-step redirection performed by the trailing-leg push-off, with the leading heel strike destroying what is left. Using the exact simplest-walker geometry of Prop 5.2 (a push-off along the trailing leg raises the speed to $v/\cos(2\alpha f)$ before the collision removes $\sin((1-f)2\alpha)$ of it), compute the energy the walker must supply per step for the reference gait ($M=70$ kg, $v=1.3\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, $2\alpha=42.2^\circ$). Check the $1/n^2$ law against the exact result and say by how much the small-angle “one quarter” overstates the saving. Probes: the difference between a scaling law and its small-angle limit.
Solution

In steady state the supplied energy equals the dissipated energy, so the per-step cost is $\tfrac12 Mv^2\sin^2\!\big((1-f)2\alpha\big)/\cos^2(2\alpha f)$. With $\tfrac12 Mv^2=59.15$ J the collision-only baseline ($f=0$) is $26.73$ J. Sweeping $f$ gives $16.88$ J at $f=0.25$, $8.83$ J at $f=0.5$, and $2.74$ J at $f=0.75$. The cost falls monotonically toward zero, so the mechanical model alone says “push off as much as possible”. What stops a real walker near $f=0.5$ is that the impulse must be produced by the ankle plantarflexors inside the $0.126$ s of double support, and Lab 3's peak of $214\ \mathrm W$ is already close to what that muscle group can deliver.

In Prop 5.2's $n=1/(1-f)$ form, the exact ratios to the baseline are $1.000,\,0.330,\,0.169,\,0.103$ for $n=1,2,3,4$, against the small-angle $1/n^2=1.000,\,0.250,\,0.111,\,0.063$. The exact $n=2$ ratio has the closed form $1/(4\cos^4\alpha)=0.330$: the textbook “one quarter” is the $\alpha\to0$ limit, and at a real $21.1^\circ$ half-angle it understates the surviving cost by $32\,\%$ — a saving of $3.03\times$, not $4\times$. The same warning applies to the naive angle argument: cutting the redirection angle to $0.75$ of its value does not cut the loss by $0.75^2=43.8\,\%$. Cutting it with a shorter step gives $\sin^2(0.75\cdot2\alpha)/\sin^2 2\alpha$, a $39.0\,\%$ reduction; cutting it with push-off ($f=0.25$) gives only $36.9\,\%$, because the push-off that removed the angle also sped the COM up.

K5. Using Lab 3's quasi-static ankle moment and plantarflexion rate, integrate the positive ankle work over stance. Then compare it with the step-to-step transition loss that section 5 computes for the same reference gait. What fraction of the transition does the ankle repay, and what has to supply the rest? Probes: closing the energy books on one step.
Solution
Compute $P(t)=\tau(t)\dot\theta(t)$ and integrate only $P^{+}$ by the trapezoid rule over stance. Lab 3 gives $\int P^{+}\,dt=22.5$ J of positive ankle work per step, peaking at $214\ \mathrm W$ ($3.06\ \mathrm{W\,kg^{-1}}$) at $79\,\%$ of stance. The collision-only transition loss for the same gait is $\tfrac12 Mv^2\sin^2 2\alpha=26.7$ J, so the ankle burst repays $84\,\%$ of it. The rest is hip work in early stance, plus the residual that Prop 5.2 leaves when push-off and collision do not split the redirection exactly evenly. The number to keep is the ratio, not either energy alone: one joint, acting for about a tenth of a second, covers most of the entire mechanical cost of walking.
K6. Take a joint angle $\theta(t)=0.35\sin(2\pi t)$ rad over one second, add independent Gaussian noise of standard deviation $\sigma_\theta=2$ mrad (a realistic marker-based estimate) drawn from np.random.default_rng(0), differentiate twice with np.gradient applied twice, and convert to a torque error with a shank-plus-foot moment of inertia $I=0.035\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}$ via Prop 6.1. Sweep the sample rate over $60$, $120$ and $240$ Hz. Which way does the error move, why is that the opposite of what more data usually buys, and how does the error compare with the signal it is meant to measure? Probes: why a faster camera makes inverse dynamics worse.
Solution

With the seed fixed at np.random.default_rng(0) the RMS torque error is $0.137$, $0.556$ and $2.468$ N m at $60$, $120$ and $240$ Hz — growing by about a factor of four for every doubling of the sample rate. The factor is not empirical. A double application of np.gradient forms $(\theta_{i+2}-2\theta_i+\theta_{i-2})/(4\Delta t^2)$, so uncorrelated noise of standard deviation $\sigma_\theta$ produces an angular-acceleration error of $\sigma_\theta\sqrt{6}/(4\Delta t^{2})$, which scales as $f_s^{2}$: the analytic values $I\sigma_\theta\sqrt6 f_s^2/4=0.154,\,0.617,\,2.469$ N m reproduce the sampled ones to within $13\,\%$ at every rate, and to $0.04\,\%$ at $240$ Hz, where the longest record leaves the least sampling scatter. Sampling faster adds no information about a $1$ Hz joint motion and multiplies the noise gain, so the naive pipeline gets monotonically worse with better hardware.

Comparing the error with the wrong quantity makes it look harmless. Against Lab 3's $95$ N m peak ankle moment even $2.5$ N m is under $3\,\%$. But the quantity this calculation actually estimates is the segment's own $I\ddot\theta$, whose true peak is $I(0.35)(2\pi)^2=0.484$ N m — so at $240$ Hz the noise is $5.1$ times the signal, and the “measurement” is noise with a units label. Raise the marker noise to $10$ mrad and the $240$ Hz error reaches $12.3$ N m, which is no longer negligible against anything. The fix is not a slower camera but a low-pass filter chosen from the signal bandwidth before differentiating, which is why every real inverse-dynamics pipeline filters first. Module 15 builds that pipeline and its uncertainty budget.

K7. Sweep cadence and step length combinations that produce the same speed, and find which combination minimises the cost proxy. Then repeat the sweep at $v=1.0$ and $1.6\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ and explain what the model predicts about how humans should change speed. Probes: redundancy in gait choice, and a falsifiable prediction from it.
Solution
Constrain $cL=v$ and evaluate the proxy over feasible $L$; the constraint removes one of the two variables, so the apparent redundancy in $(L,c)$ is not a free choice. At $v=1.3\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ the minimum lands at $L^{\ast}=0.684$ m and $c^{\ast}=1.90$ steps s$^{-1}$ ($114$ steps per minute). The redundancy is broken because the two costs scale oppositely in $L$: transition cost rises as $\sin^2(2\arcsin(L/2\ell))$ and swing cost falls as $1/L^2$. Now repeat at $v=1.0$ and $1.6\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$: $L^{\ast}=0.684$ m in both cases, unchanged to four figures. That is not a numerical accident. Both terms carry the same factor $Mv^2$, so $v$ divides out of $dC/dL=0$ entirely (D8), and the model predicts that preferred step length is set by leg length and the swing coefficient alone, with the whole speed change carried by cadence. It is a strong prediction and a falsifiable one: humans do increase step length somewhat with speed, so the proxy is right about the direction of the redundancy break and wrong about its rigidity — the missing ingredient is that swing cost is not purely $\propto c^2$ at high cadence.
K8. Build the yaw angular momentum of a walking body from segments instead of assuming a percentage. Treat each leg as a point mass $m_{\rm leg}=0.161M$ at a mediolateral offset $d_{\rm leg}=0.09$ m from the body's vertical axis, moving fore-aft at $\pm1.3\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ relative to the trunk, and each arm as $m_{\rm arm}=0.050M$ at $d_{\rm arm}=0.20$ m moving at $\mp0.6\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. Compute the peak vertical-axis angular momentum with and without arm swing, and the mean trunk yaw torque each requires over a half stride. What fraction of the legs' momentum do the arms actually cancel? Probes: deriving a cancellation fraction rather than asserting one.
Solution

About the vertical axis, a segment at mediolateral offset $y$ moving fore-aft at $v_x$ contributes $H_z=-m\,y\,v_x$. The two legs are antiphase and on opposite sides of the midline, so their contributions carry the same sign and add, while each arm swings opposite its ipsilateral leg, so the arms subtract: $$|H_{\rm legs}|=2m_{\rm leg}d_{\rm leg}u_{\rm leg}=2(11.27)(0.09)(1.3)=2.637,\qquad |H_{\rm arms}|=2m_{\rm arm}d_{\rm arm}u_{\rm arm}=2(3.50)(0.20)(0.60)=0.840,$$ both in $\mathrm{kg\,m^2\,s^{-1}}$. The arms therefore cancel $0.840/2.637=31.9\,\%$ of the legs' yaw momentum, leaving a residual of $1.797\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2\,s^{-1}}$.

That residual must reverse sign twice per stride, so over a half stride ($T/2=0.527$ s) the trunk must supply a mean yaw torque $2|H_z|/(T/2)$: $10.0$ N m with the arms held still, $6.8$ N m with them swinging. If the stabilisation cost is quadratic in torque, arm swing cuts it by $1-(6.8/10.0)^2=54\,\%$. The often-quoted “about thirty percent” cancellation is close to right, but it is now a result of segment masses and offsets rather than an input, and the model says why it comes out that way: the arms are light — five percent of body mass each against sixteen for a leg — but they work at more than twice the moment arm, and those two factors nearly cancel. The estimate leans hardest on $d_{\rm arm}$: it enters linearly, so a $25\,\%$ error there moves the cancellation fraction by the same $25\,\%$.

K9. Derive the passive walker's stride map from Prop 9.1 rather than assuming one. Let $E_n=\tfrac12 Mv_n^2$ be the kinetic energy just before heel strike $n$. Gravity adds $MgL\sin\gamma$ over a step and the collision multiplies the energy by $\cos^2 2\alpha$, so $E_{n+1}=aE_n+b$. Identify $a$ and $b$ for $\ell=0.95$ m, $\alpha=0.30$ rad, $\gamma=0.05$ rad and $M=70$ kg, find the fixed point, iterate from a walker released too slowly, and compare the exact fixed-point speed with Prop 9.1's small-angle estimate. Probes: a limit cycle as the fixed point of a map you derived.
Solution

With $L=2\ell\sin\alpha=0.5615$ m, $a=\cos^2(0.6)=0.6812$ and $b=MgL\sin\gamma=19.27$ J. The fixed point is $E^{\ast}=b/(1-a)=60.44$ J, so $v^{\ast}=\sqrt{2E^{\ast}/M}=1.314\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. Stability needs $|a|\lt1$, and $a=\cos^2 2\alpha$ is always less than one for any non-zero step angle: the passive gait is locally stable in energy for every slope and every step length, with perturbations decaying by a factor $0.681$ per step, an e-folding in $2.60$ steps. Released from $E_0=5$ J the walker reaches $57.9$ J after eight steps, within $4.3\,\%$ of the limit cycle. A walker set down too slowly speeds itself up, and that is what makes passive walkers robust enough to build out of wood.

Prop 9.1's small-angle formula $v=\sqrt{g\ell\sin\gamma/\alpha}$ gives $1.246\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, $5.2\,\%$ below the exact $1.314$. The whole discrepancy is the two small-angle steps in that proof ($L\approx2\ell\alpha$ and $\sin 2\alpha\approx2\alpha$) applied at $2\alpha=34.4^\circ$, which is not small. Note also what this one-dimensional map does not capture. It tracks energy only, so it cannot see the swing-leg state, and $|a|\lt1$ here does not show that the full two-state Jacobian $D\mathcal P$ of section 9 has both eigenvalues inside the unit circle. Passive walkers do fall over; energy stability is necessary, not sufficient.

K10. Sweep reaction delay, step reach and COM speed in the fall-margin inequality of Prop 10.1 and rank them by the elasticity of the margin, $\partial\ln m/\partial\ln(\cdot)$, at the Lab 4 reference point. Which variable spends the margin fastest, and how do the answers change if you differentiate the capture point instead of the margin? Probes: which quantity is being differentiated is half the problem.
Solution
Write the margin as $m=x_s^{\max}-\big(x_{\rm com}+\dot x_{\rm com}\Delta+\dot x_{\rm com}/\omega_0\big)$ and evaluate at the Lab 4 reference point ($x_{\rm com}=0.03$ m, $\dot x_{\rm com}=0.45\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, $\Delta=0.20$ s, $\omega_0=3.21\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$, $x_s^{\max}=0.415$ m), where the capture point is $0.260$ m and the margin $0.155$ m. The elasticities are $-1.48$ to COM speed, $-0.58$ to reaction delay and $+2.68$ to step reach. Two readings follow. First, speed beats delay by a factor of $2.6$, because speed enters the capture point twice — once through the reaction drift $\dot x_{\rm com}\Delta$ and once through the divergent term $\dot x_{\rm com}/\omega_0$ — while delay enters only once. Second, and against intuition, the largest single elasticity belongs to step reach: a ten percent loss of reach costs more margin than a ten percent increase in delay. That is why hip range and the confidence to take a long recovery step matter at least as much as reflex speed. Finally, differentiating the wrong quantity changes every answer: the elasticities of the capture point are $+0.88$ to speed, $+0.35$ to delay, and exactly $0$ to step reach, which does not appear in it at all. Naming which quantity is differentiated is half of this problem.

Diagnostics

  1. If someone says walking is free because energy is exchanged pendularly, ask where heel-strike loss went.
  2. If a gait figure has arrows but no foot, COP, COM, or event labels, it is not yet a gait figure.
  3. If inverse dynamics reports torque without angular velocity, it cannot report joint power.
  4. If an older-adult fall explanation names only "balance," it is missing tissue chemistry and recovery biology.
  5. If a passive walker is drawn on level ground without actuation, it must eventually stop.
