0. Motivation — why a bond energy sets a bone's stiffness

Stand on one leg. Module 2 §2 worked out what happens inside the thigh bone when you do: for a $70\ \mathrm{kg}$ person the hip pushes down the femoral shaft with roughly $F\approx1717\ \mathrm N$, the cortical shell that carries it — the dense outer wall of the bone, as against the spongy trabecular interior — has cross-sectional area $A\approx4.62\ \mathrm{cm^2}$, and so the compressive stress is $\sigma=F/A\approx3.72\ \mathrm{MPa}$. One further step gives the strain, $\varepsilon=\sigma/E$, and with the Young's modulus $E\approx17\ \mathrm{GPa}$ (the stress a material needs per unit of strain; Module 2 §1) that is $\varepsilon\approx2.19\times10^{-4}$ — about one part in $4600$. Over a $43\ \mathrm{cm}$ femur the whole bone shortens by $0.094\ \mathrm{mm}$, about the width of a human hair. You feel nothing, which is the point.

Every step of that calculation is honest except one. $F$ came from a free-body diagram. $A$ came from a ruler. $\sigma=F/A$ is a definition and $\varepsilon=\sigma/E$ is Hooke's law. But $E\approx17\ \mathrm{GPa}$ came from nowhere: it was quoted. Somebody put a piece of bone in a testing machine, and the course wrote down what the machine said.

measured, not derived: $E$ for whole cortical bone is a composite property of mineral, collagen, water and porosity in a particular anatomical arrangement, and this course reports it from mechanical test. What Module 0 derives is the two end members that a composite bound then combines — the stiffness of the mineral and the stiffness of the protein — because those are the quantities chemistry actually sets.

That is not a small gap, and it is not confined to bone. The same silence covers the fixed charge density of cartilage, the friction coefficient of a joint surface, the gain of the bone-remodeling law, Hill's constant for muscle, and every constitutive time constant in the book. A course that computes three significant figures downstream of a number it never explains has taught mechanics, not physiology.

The three-layer question. Every module from here on opens by asking it, and this module supplies the third answer.

  1. What do you observe? A bone that shortens by $0.094\ \mathrm{mm}$ under body load. That is physics, and Modules 1–17 already do it well.
  2. Which tissue does it? Cortical bone: collagen fibrils stiffened by plates of a calcium-phosphate mineral, laid down and removed by living cells. That is biology, and it is the layer the course mentions but rarely computes.
  3. Which molecule makes that tissue behave that way? A calcium ion bonded to an oxygen of a phosphate group, resisting displacement, along the direction the load takes, with an effective stiffness of roughly $25\ \mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}$. That is chemistry, and it is missing entirely.

The three layers are not three subjects. They are one chain, and Fig. 1 runs it downward and then back up: from the bone you stand on, to the mineralised fibril that builds it, to the single bond whose stiffness — divided by its own length — is the modulus of the mineral.

The bridge, in one line of dimensional reasoning

Why should a bond stiffness divided by a bond length be a modulus at all? Count. Take a cube of material whose atoms sit on a lattice of spacing $r_0$, and pull on it. Across any cross-section of area $A$ there are about $A/r_0^{2}$ bonds, because each bond claims a patch of area $r_0^{2}$. Stretch the material by strain $\varepsilon$ and — if the deformation is affine, meaning every bond stretches in the same proportion as the block does — each bond lengthens by $x=\varepsilon r_0$ and pulls back with $f=kx=k\varepsilon r_0$. The stress is the total force over the area:

$$\sigma \;=\; \frac{(A/r_0^{2})\,f}{A} \;=\; \frac{k\,\varepsilon\,r_0}{r_0^{2}} \;=\; \frac{k}{r_0}\,\varepsilon \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad E \;\sim\; \frac{k}{r_0}.$$

Here $k$ is the bond stiffness, the second derivative of the interatomic potential at its minimum, in $\mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}$; $r_0$ is the equilibrium bond length, in metres; $\sigma$, $\varepsilon$ and $E$ are the stress, strain and Young's modulus of Module 2 §1. The result is a scaling argument, not yet a theorem: the real answer carries a factor of order one that depends on the coordination number of the lattice (how many nearest neighbours each atom bonds to) and on which direction you pull. Where this $k$ could be confused with the remodeling gain $k$ of Module 2 §7 or the permeability $k$ of Module 4, this course writes it $k_{\text{bond}}$. §2 does it properly — defines the potential, takes the second derivative, sums over neighbours, and proves the bound. What the one-liner already buys is the order of magnitude, and the order of magnitude is the entire story.

Put the four bond types of biological matter into it — but read the column headed $k$ carefully, because it is not what a spectroscopist would quote. A bare $\mathrm{C\!-\!C}$ stretching force constant is about $450\ \mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}$, and an isolated $\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}\!-\!\mathrm{O^{2-}}$ pair is stiffer still. Coulomb attraction alone will not give the number: $-C/r$ with $C=q_1q_2/4\pi\epsilon_0$ has no minimum at all, and its curvature $-2C/r_0^{3}$ is negative. Add the short-range repulsion $B/r^{n}$ that stops the ions merging, impose $\mathrm{d}U/\mathrm{d}r=0$ at $r_0$, and the curvature becomes $k_{\text{pair}}=(n-1)C/r_0^{3}$, which for the Born exponent $n\approx9$ and $r_0=0.24\ \mathrm{nm}$ is about $530\ \mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}$. Here $q_1=+2e$ and $q_2=-2e$ are the ionic charges and $\epsilon_0$ is the permittivity of free space — not the strain $\varepsilon$, which shares its letter and nothing else. Through $E\sim k/r_0$ that pair would predict about $2200\ \mathrm{GPa}$ for apatite: twenty times what apatite actually is. The gap is geometry. Real bonds are not aligned with the load, lattices are not simple cubic, a triple helix uncoils before its hydrogen bonds ever stretch, and mineral platelets slide along the collagen they sit in. So the table lists the effective per-bond stiffness along the load path, defined as the $k$ for which $E=k/r_0$ holds. §2 is the section that has to earn the difference: it takes the bare force constant, puts in the coordination number and the loading direction, and shows what survives. Here the four numbers are simply displayed, so the ladder can be seen at once.

Read the second and third rows against Module 2 §5. That section bounds the stiffness of bone between a Voigt average — the two phases in parallel, sharing one strain — and a Reuss average — the two phases in series, sharing one stress — of two numbers it takes as given: a mineral modulus near $100\ \mathrm{GPa}$ and a collagen modulus near $1\ \mathrm{GPa}$. The ladder returns $104$ and $1.07$. But read the direction of that arrow honestly, because for now it points the wrong way.

measured, not derived, because §0 runs the calculation backwards: the effective stiffness $25\ \mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}$ was obtained by calibrating $E=k/r_0$ against Module 2 §5's measured mineral modulus, so the $104\ \mathrm{GPa}$ that comes back out is that same measurement returned, not a prediction. §2 owes the forward calculation from an interatomic potential. measured, not derived, because the same calibration is what produced $0.30\ \mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}$ from Module 2 §5's collagen modulus. No potential in this course yet yields a hydrogen-bonded fibril stiffness forward; §2 and §4 are where that debt falls due.

What §0 does establish is that the calibration lands where chemistry says it should. The stiff phase calibrates onto the ionic rung and the compliant phase onto the hydrogen rung: two rungs apart on a ladder spanning five decades, in the order and with roughly the spacing the bond types predict. That is a consistency check, not a derivation, and the difference is worth stating twice. Apatite is stiff because it is held by ionic bonds, short and strong. Collagen is a hundred times softer even though its own backbone is covalent and stiffer than either — because a collagen fibril loaded in tension does not stretch its backbone first. It uncoils, and it slides molecule against molecule, and both of those are resisted by hydrogen bonds and by water, not by the peptide chain. The general form of the lesson: a material is as stiff as the softest link in its load path, not as the stiffest bond anywhere in it. Which link that is depends on the architecture, which is why the ladder alone cannot finish the argument and §2 must.

The ruler: thermal energy

One more quantity has to be on the table before any of this can be judged, and it is the quantity the rest of the course has never needed: the size of a thermal kick. At body temperature $T=310.15\ \mathrm K$, Boltzmann's constant $k_B=1.381\times10^{-23}\ \mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}$ and Avogadro's number $N_A=6.022\times10^{23}\ \mathrm{mol^{-1}}$ give

$$k_BT \;=\; 4.28\times10^{-21}\ \mathrm J \;=\; 4.28\ \mathrm{zJ} \;=\; 26.7\ \mathrm{meV},\qquad R_gT \;=\; N_A k_B T \;=\; 2.58\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}.$$

Here $R_g=N_Ak_B$ is the gas constant — written $R_g$ throughout this course because $R$ is already the joint reaction force of Module 3 §4 — $\mathrm{zJ}$ is the zeptojoule, $10^{-21}\ \mathrm J$, the natural unit for one molecule; and $\mathrm{meV}$ is the millielectronvolt, $1.602\times10^{-22}\ \mathrm J$. $k_BT$ is the energy per molecule that temperature hands out for free. Anything a molecule must do that costs much less than $k_BT$ happens constantly, unbidden, whether or not it is useful. Anything that costs much more than $k_BT$ still happens, but exponentially rarely — at a rate carrying the Arrhenius factor $e^{-\Delta E/k_BT}$ — unless something pays for it. §6 makes that factor quantitative; here it is enough that rare and impossible are different words.

Now measure standing on one leg against that ruler. Each Ca–O bond in the loaded femur stretches by $x=\varepsilon r_0 = (2.19\times10^{-4})(0.24\ \mathrm{nm}) = 5.25\times10^{-14}\ \mathrm m$ — fifty femtometres, which is one part in $4600$ of the bond's own length. Under the affine assumption the bond carries the strain the whole bone carries, and $\varepsilon$ was $2.19\times10^{-4}$. That is an upper estimate: Module 2 §5 puts bone nearer the Reuss floor, so the mineral's share of the strain is smaller still, and the conclusion below only gets stronger. The elastic energy it stores is $\tfrac12kx^{2}=3.44\times10^{-26}\ \mathrm J$, which is

$$\frac{\tfrac12 k x^{2}}{k_BT} \;=\; 8.0\times10^{-6},\qquad\text{i.e. the thermal jiggle is about } 1.2\times10^{5} \text{ times larger.}$$

Standing on your leg perturbs a bond a hundred thousand times more gently than body temperature already does. Put the same comparison in displacement rather than energy and it reads the same way. The mechanical stretch is $52\ \mathrm{fm}$; the bond's own thermal amplitude is $\sqrt{k_BT/k}=\sqrt{(4.28\times10^{-21})/25}\approx13\ \mathrm{pm}$, two hundred and fifty times larger. Your body weight is a whisper laid on top of a shout.

Be careful about what that buys, because it is easy to claim too much. It establishes one thing cleanly: the bond stays harmonic. A displacement that small never leaves the quadratic bottom of the potential well, so the bond's response is linear. Whole-bone linearity needs more than that — it also needs no sliding at the mineral–collagen interfaces and no microcracks opening — and this ratio says nothing about either.

The ratio does not, by itself, say where bone's viscoelasticity lives either. The discriminator there is barrier height, not bias. Rearranging an ionic lattice means crossing a barrier of order an electronvolt, about $40\,k_BT$, so that event has a rate near zero whatever tiny bias you add: the mineral does not creep. A hydrogen bond costs a few $k_BT$ to break, so hydrogen bonds break and re-form constantly on their own, and a small persistent bias summed over very many such events is a drift you can measure. That is why the viscoelasticity cortical bone does show under sustained load has to live in the protein, the water and the interfaces between them, and it is where §4 and Module 6 go looking for it.

What the ratio does not explain at all is fracture. Bone fatigue is microcrack accumulation driven by stress concentration — a continuum process, not a thermally activated bond event — and a bad enough fall breaks a femur in one go, statically. The honest neighbouring claim, that physiological load cannot break bone in a single squeeze, comes from Module 2 §2's safety factor of $n\approx45$, not from $k_BT$. The ruler is powerful and narrow: it tells you which molecular events are available to the body, and it is silent about everything that happens above the scale of one bond.

1. The mole, concentration, and the $k_BT$ / $R_gT$ bridge

Module 5 §2 states the central bookkeeping fact of muscle contraction in one line: one ATP is hydrolysed per crossbridge per cycle. It never says what that costs. It cannot, because every biochemistry table prices ATP hydrolysis in kilojoules per mole, and every mechanical quantity in this course — a force, a displacement, the work of one pull — belongs to one object. Those are two languages for one physical fact. Until they become one sentence, the chemistry of Module 5 and the mechanics of Module 5 cannot be checked against each other at all, and the course cannot notice if they disagree.

This section builds the dictionary, in three entries. How to count molecules: the mole. How to say how crowded they are: concentration. How to turn an energy carried by one molecule into an energy quoted per mole, and back: the $k_BT \leftrightarrow R_gT$ bridge. The first two are conventions and cost nothing to accept. The third does the real work, and it carries one condition that is easy to miss and expensive to miss — the reason the same reaction is worth $-30.5\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ in a table and $-50\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ in a working muscle.

Counting: the mole

Chemistry counts. A reaction consumes its reagents in whole-number ratios of molecules, never in round numbers of grams, so the natural measure of "how much" is a count. Counts of molecules are inconveniently large, so chemistry keeps them in a bundle.

Definition 1.1 (Amount of substance; the mole). Let a sample contain $N$ specified entities — molecules, ions, formula units; the entity must be named. Its amount of substance is $$n \;=\; \frac{N}{N_A},\qquad N_A \;=\; 6.02214076\times10^{23}\ \mathrm{mol^{-1}},$$ where $N_A$ is the Avogadro constant. The unit of $n$ is the mole (mol); since the 2019 redefinition of the SI, one mole contains exactly $N_A$ entities by definition, so $N_A$ is exact and carries no experimental uncertainty. $N$ is a pure count and is dimensionless.

Two collisions to name before they bite. The letter $n$ is the Born repulsion exponent in §0 and the safety factor $n\approx45$ of Module 2 §2; it means amount of substance only where a mole is under discussion, and this course never puts two of the three in one equation. Molar mass — the mass of one mole of a substance, $M_m = m/n$ in $\mathrm{kg\,mol^{-1}}$ or the more usual $\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}$ — is written $M_m$ and not $M$, because $M$ is already the body mass $70\ \mathrm{kg}$ of Module 2 §2 and the bending moment of Module 2 §3.

The mole earns its place as a translator between two instruments. A balance weighs; it returns a mass. A molecule reacts; it responds to a count. $M_m$ converts one into the other, and $N_A$ converts a count of moles into a count of molecules. Every number in the rest of this module passes through that pair at least once.

Concentration, and the units the body is quoted in

A count alone does not say whether a molecule will meet a partner. Crowding does, and crowding is concentration.

Definition 1.2 (Molar concentration). For an amount $n$ of a named species dissolved in a solution of volume $V$, the molar concentration is $c = n/V$. The unit is the molar, $1\ \mathrm M = 1\ \mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}} = 10^{3}\ \mathrm{mol\,m^{-3}}$. Physiological concentrations are usually quoted in millimolar ($\mathrm{mM}$, $10^{-3}\ \mathrm M$), micromolar ($\mathrm{\mu M}$, $10^{-6}\ \mathrm M$) or nanomolar ($\mathrm{nM}$, $10^{-9}\ \mathrm M$). Note that $V$ is the volume of the solution, not of the solvent, and that $c$ is defined for a species in a phase — "the concentration of calcium" is meaningless until one says free calcium, in which compartment.

Molar concentration is a laboratory unit: it is what a pipette delivers. It is not yet a molecular picture. To get one, convert it into a number density and then into a distance, because a distance is a thing the reader can hold against a bond length.

$$n_V \;=\; c\,N_A, \qquad 1\ \mathrm M \;=\; 0.6022\ \mathrm{nm^{-3}}, \qquad \bar d \;=\; n_V^{-1/3} \;=\; \frac{1.184\ \mathrm{nm}}{(c/\mathrm M)^{1/3}}.$$

Here $n_V$ is the number density, the count of that species per unit volume, in $\mathrm{nm^{-3}}$; and $\bar d$ is the mean spacing, the edge of the cube of solution that contains on average one such molecule. The middle identity is the whole conversion in one line: a one-molar solution holds about six-tenths of a molecule per cubic nanometre, so its molecules sit about $1.2\ \mathrm{nm}$ apart — four or five bond lengths, on the scale §0 was working in. The cube root compresses hard, which is the useful part: dropping the concentration by a factor of a thousand only stretches the spacing by ten.

Two further conventions appear in this course because the tissues do. The first counts charge rather than molecules.

Definition 1.3 (Equivalents; osmolarity). For a species carrying charge number $z$ (so $+2$ for $\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}$, $-1$ for $\mathrm{Cl^-}$), the equivalent concentration is $c_{\mathrm{eq}} = |z|\,c$, in equivalents per litre ($\mathrm{Eq\,L^{-1}}$); it counts moles of elementary charge instead of moles of particles. The osmolarity is the total concentration of all dissolved particles, regardless of identity, in $\mathrm{osmol\,L^{-1}}$: a salt that fully dissociates contributes one osmole per ion, so $0.15\ \mathrm M$ sodium chloride is $0.30\ \mathrm{osmol\,L^{-1}}$.

Equivalents matter here for one reason. The cartilage literature quotes the fixed charge density of the tissue in milliequivalents per millilitre, and Module 4 §2 writes it as a molarity, $0.2\ \mathrm M$. Those are the same statement only because the fixed charges — the sulfate and carboxyl groups carried by the proteoglycans, the bottle-brush molecules of the cartilage matrix, each a protein core hung with many long sugar chains — are singly charged: for $|z|=1$, $1\ \mathrm{mEq\,mL^{-1}} = 1\ \mathrm M$ exactly. Reading a divalent number the same way would be wrong by a factor of two. Where that fixed charge density itself comes from, this course still owes an answer: Module 4 takes it from measurement, and §7 is the section that has to count the charged groups on a glycosaminoglycan — one of those sugar chains — and get $0.2\ \mathrm M$ out.

Osmolarity is recorded here only as a definition; the pressure it generates is §5's business and Module 4 §2's.

With the definitions in place, the body's own numbers can be laid on one scale. Read the last column first: it is the count of that species in a cube one micrometre on a side — a femtolitre, roughly the volume of a small bacterium, and about the volume of one half-sarcomere — half of the repeating contractile unit of a muscle fibre — of the muscle in Module 5 §2. Two rows below name the cytosol, the fluid filling a cell outside its compartments; that is where a signalling ion has to be counted.

The bridge: an energy per molecule, an energy per mole

Now the entry that does the work. A chemical table gives $\Delta G$ per mole — a change in Gibbs free energy, the thermodynamic potential a system at constant temperature and pressure drives downhill, written $G$ here and not to be confused with the shear modulus $G$ of Module 2 §4; a mechanical calculation gives an energy per molecule; §0 has already fixed the ruler, $k_BT = 4.28\ \mathrm{zJ}$ per molecule and $R_gT = 2.58\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ per mole at body temperature. The temptation is to declare the conversion trivial — multiply by $N_A$ — and move on. It is almost trivial, and the exception is the whole reason ATP is not worth what its table says.

The exception comes from counting, which makes it this section's business rather than §3's. A molecule's freedom of position carries free energy: a dilute molecule has more places it could be than a concentrated one, and places to be are entropy.

Lemma 1.1 (The free-energy cost of dilution). Model a solution as $M_s$ equivalent sites, each able to hold at most one solute molecule, containing $N$ identical solute molecules that do not interact with one another and each of which has the same internal energy $u_0$. Then at temperature $T$ the free-energy cost of adding one more solute molecule — its chemical potential $\mu$, written $\mu$ throughout the chemistry of this course, while the friction coefficient of Module 4 §7 is written $\mu_{\text{fric}}$ — is $$\mu \;=\; u_0 \;+\; k_BT\,\ln\!\frac{N}{M_s-N} \;\;\xrightarrow{\;N\,\ll\,M_s\;}\;\; u_0 + k_BT\ln\frac{c}{c_{\text{site}}},$$ where $c$ is the solute concentration and $c_{\text{site}}$ the concentration of sites. Consequently, for any two concentrations $c$ and $c^{\circ}$ of the same dilute species, $$\mu(c) \;=\; \mu(c^{\circ}) \;+\; k_BT\,\ln\frac{c}{c^{\circ}}.$$

Choosing which $N$ of the $M_s$ sites are occupied is the only freedom the model has, so the number of arrangements is the binomial coefficient $\Omega = M_s!\,/\,[N!\,(M_s-N)!]$ and the entropy is $S = k_B\ln\Omega$. For $M_s, N \gg 1$, Stirling's approximation $\ln X! \approx X\ln X - X$ gives

$$\frac{S}{k_B} \;=\; M_s\ln M_s - N\ln N - (M_s-N)\ln(M_s-N),$$

the linear terms having cancelled identically. Differentiating with respect to $N$,

$$\frac{1}{k_B}\frac{\partial S}{\partial N} \;=\; -\ln N - 1 + \ln(M_s-N) + 1 \;=\; -\ln\frac{N}{M_s-N}.$$

The free energy at fixed volume is the Helmholtz free energy $A = Nu_0 - TS$ — written $A$ and not the usual $F$, because $F$ is a force everywhere else in this course, including the femoral force of §0. So the cost of one more molecule at fixed $T$ and $M_s$ is $\mu = \partial A/\partial N = u_0 - T\,\partial S/\partial N = u_0 + k_BT\ln[N/(M_s-N)]$, which is the first claim. When $N \ll M_s$ the denominator is $M_s$ to first order, and dividing numerator and denominator by the volume turns the ratio of counts into the ratio of concentrations, $N/M_s = c/c_{\text{site}}$. The last claim follows by writing that result at $c$ and at $c^{\circ}$ and subtracting: $\epsilon$ and $c_{\text{site}}$ are properties of the species and the solvent, not of how much of it is present, so both cancel. $\blacksquare$

One bookkeeping note, since the next result speaks of Gibbs and this one of Helmholtz. Inserting one dilute solute molecule at constant pressure does $p\,\Delta V$ of work against the atmosphere, and for a molecule-sized $\Delta V\approx3\times10^{-29}\ \mathrm{m^3}$ at $10^{5}\ \mathrm{Pa}$ that is under one part in a thousand of $k_BT$. So $\partial A/\partial N$ and $\partial G/\partial N$ agree far beyond the precision of anything in this course, and $\mu$ means either.

Anchor the abstraction to water, since every solution in this book is aqueous. Water itself is $55.5\ \mathrm M$ — a litre weighs $1000\ \mathrm g$ and its molar mass is $18.015\ \mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}$; at body temperature water is slightly less dense and the figure is $55.1\ \mathrm M$, a difference too small to matter anywhere below — so a solute at $1\ \mathrm{mM}$ occupies about one site in fifty-five thousand, and the dilute limit of the lemma is not a courtesy; it is comfortable by four decades. The reference concentration $c^{\circ}$ is pure convention, and chemistry has chosen $c^{\circ} = 1\ \mathrm M$. Nothing physical happens at one molar — and note that the chosen reference is not a real one-molar solution, in which one site in $55.5$ is occupied and the dilute limit of the lemma is already $2\%$ wrong, but the hypothetical ideal-dilute solution extrapolated up to $1\ \mathrm M$. The convention is chosen precisely so that the logarithm stays exact. What is physical is the difference: the lemma says that moving a species from $1\ \mathrm M$ down to $1\ \mathrm{\mu M}$ lowers its chemical potential by $k_BT\ln(10^{6}) = 13.8\,k_BT$, and every such term must be carried through any conversion between the two languages.

Proposition 1.1 (The bridge, and its condition). Consider a reaction among dilute solutes with stoichiometric coefficients $\nu_i$, negative for a reactant and positive for a product, held at a fixed composition $\{c_i\}$. Define $\Delta g \equiv \sum_i \nu_i\mu_i$: the change in the free energy of the whole solution when the reaction advances by one molecule at that unchanged composition, with $\mu_i$ the chemical potential of Lemma 1.1. Then the change when it advances by one mole is $$\Delta G \;=\; N_A\,\Delta g, \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad \frac{\Delta G}{R_gT} \;=\; \frac{\Delta g}{k_BT},$$ so an energy of $x\,k_BT$ per molecule is an energy of $x\,R_gT$ per mole, and at $T = 310.15\ \mathrm K$ that is $2.58x\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$. Both sides must be read at the same composition. Two figures quoted at different compositions differ additionally by the Lemma 1.1 terms $$\Delta g - \Delta g^{\circ} \;=\; k_BT\sum_i \nu_i \ln\frac{c_i}{c_i^{\circ}},$$ which vanishes when that sum is zero — in particular at the standard state, where every $c_i = c_i^{\circ}$, but also at any other composition for which the products of the concentration ratios happen to cancel.

A molar quantity is by definition the change per mole of reaction advance, and one mole of advance is $N_A$ single-molecule advances. Because the composition is held fixed, each of those $N_A$ advances costs the same $\Delta g$ — that is the only place the dilute, non-interacting assumption is used, and it is what fails in a crowded solution — so the total is $N_A\Delta g$. Note what is not claimed: that the entropy of the solution is a sum of per-molecule entropies. It is not, and Lemma 1.1 is the statement of exactly how it fails; each $\mu_i$ already carries that non-additive part. Dividing by $N_Ak_BT = R_gT$ gives the dimensionless form, and the numerical factor is $R_gT$ evaluated in §0.

For the condition, write the per-molecule free energy of species $i$ using Lemma 1.1 as $\mu_i = \mu_i^{\circ} + k_BT\ln(c_i/c_i^{\circ})$, where $\mu_i^{\circ}$ is its value at the reference concentration. The free-energy change of a reaction is the stoichiometric sum $\Delta g = \sum_i \nu_i\mu_i = \sum_i \nu_i\mu_i^{\circ} + k_BT\sum_i \nu_i\ln(c_i/c_i^{\circ})$. The first sum is what a table reports; the second vanishes when the stoichiometric sum of the logarithms is zero, which the standard state guarantees but does not monopolise. Multiplying through by $N_A$ preserves the split, so the discrepancy between a tabulated molar value and a per-molecule value measured in a cell is exactly the second sum, and multiplying by $N_A$ cannot remove it. $\blacksquare$

That second sum is the quantity chemistry calls the reaction quotient, and assembling it into $\Delta G = \Delta G^{\circ} + R_gT\ln Q$ — with the equilibrium condition that follows from setting $\Delta G$ to zero — is §3's headline result, not this section's. What this section has established is narrower and is the part the dictionary needs: a molar energy is a per-molecule energy times $N_A$, plus a concentration term that vanishes at the standard state.

What the dictionary buys: one ATP, in piconewtons

Return to the opening claim. Module 5 §2 spends one ATP per crossbridge per cycle and, in that cycle, the myosin head — the projecting motor domain of the thick filament — pulls the thin filament, the actin cable it grips, about $d = 10\ \mathrm{nm}$. Ask the question the module could not ask: how hard can it pull?

Hydrolysing ATP to ADP and inorganic phosphate releases about $50\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ inside a working muscle cell. That number is not itself measured, and it is not the $30.5\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ of the standard tables: it is computed from the tabulated standard value and the cell's measured concentrations. The difference between the two figures is exactly the concentration term of Proposition 1.1. A cell holds ATP high and its products low, so the reaction quotient is far below one, its logarithm is negative, and the reaction is worth more in the cell than at the standard state. What the laboratory supplies is the ratio $[\mathrm{ATP}]\,/\,([\mathrm{ADP}][\mathrm{P_i}])$ — where $[\mathrm X]$ is the concentration $c$ of species X, the notation chemistry uses inside a reaction quotient — and §3 is where the $\ln Q$ machinery that turns that ratio into the cellular figure is derived. Here the result is quoted and the sign of the correction explained, no more.

Run it through the dictionary. Per molecule,

$$|\Delta g| \;=\; \frac{50\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}}{N_A} \;=\; 8.30\times10^{-20}\ \mathrm J \;=\; \frac{50}{2.58}\,k_BT \;=\; 19.4\,k_BT.$$

One ATP is worth about twenty thermal quanta. Now impose the second law in the one form needed here: for a process at constant temperature and pressure, the work delivered cannot exceed the fall in Gibbs free energy that paid for it, $W \le |\Delta g|$. That inequality is borrowed — §3 derives it, alongside the equilibrium condition — and it is used here only to bound one number. Writing $\bar F \equiv \int_0^{d} F\,\mathrm dx / d$ for the displacement-averaged force the head exerts over the stroke, $W = \bar F d$, so

$$\bar F \;\le\; \frac{|\Delta g|}{d} \;=\; \frac{8.30\times10^{-20}\ \mathrm J}{10\times10^{-9}\ \mathrm m} \;=\; 8.30\ \mathrm{pN}.$$

No crossbridge in Module 5 may pull harder than about eight piconewtons averaged over a ten-nanometre stroke, whatever the model of the head says, because there is nothing to pay for more. The bound is thermodynamic and therefore cheap; what makes it interesting is that it is nearly attained. Single-molecule optical-trap experiments — one protein held in a focused laser beam, which reports the force it pulls with — put the force of one myosin head in the range of a few piconewtons — call it $3$ to $6\ \mathrm{pN}$ — which at $d = 10\ \mathrm{nm}$ is $7$ to $14\,k_BT$ of work, or $36\%$ to $72\%$ of the quantum. The crossbridge is a converter running at roughly half efficiency, and the rest leaves as heat — the crossbridge's share of the heat production Module 5 treats as a whole-muscle observable, the calcium pumps of Module 5 §3 spending ATP on top of it.

Three cautions, because a bound is easy to over-read. First, $\bar F$ is a mean over the stroke, not a peak; a head that pulls hard early and weakly late satisfies the bound with a peak force above it. Second, the $10\ \mathrm{nm}$ is Module 5's model stroke, and the ceiling goes as $1/d$: halve the stroke and the bound doubles. The product $\bar F d$ is what the ATP fixes, not either factor alone. Third, the quoted $3$–$6\ \mathrm{pN}$ comes from isolated proteins in a trap, not from a head inside a lattice of filaments, and the two need not agree.

What could not be done before this section was to write the question down. That is the recurring value of a unit: it does not answer anything, but it makes an answer expressible, and an inexpressible question never gets checked.

Where "concentration" stops being a number

One limit has to be stated before the rest of the module leans on concentrations, and the ladder above already hinted at it. A concentration is a density, and a density presumes enough molecules to average. Physiology routinely violates that.

Proposition 1.2 (Counting noise). Let a dilute, non-interacting species at concentration $c$ occupy a large volume, and let $N$ be the number of its molecules found inside a fixed subvolume $V$. Then $N$ is Poisson-distributed with mean $\bar N = c\,N_A\,V$, and, writing $\sigma_N$ for the standard deviation of $N$ — not the stress $\sigma$ of §0, which shares the letter and nothing else — the relative fluctuation is $$\frac{\sigma_N}{\bar N} \;=\; \frac{1}{\sqrt{\bar N}} \;=\; \frac{1}{\sqrt{c\,N_A\,V}}.$$

Let the species have $M_{\text{tot}}$ molecules in a total volume $V_{\text{tot}} \gg V$ — written $M_{\text{tot}}$, since $M$ is the body mass and the bending moment of Module 2. Because the molecules do not interact, each is independently inside $V$ with probability $p = V/V_{\text{tot}}$, so $N$ is binomial with mean $M_{\text{tot}}p$ and variance $M_{\text{tot}}p(1-p)$. Take the limit $M_{\text{tot}}\to\infty$, $p\to0$ at fixed $M_{\text{tot}}p = \bar N = cN_AV$: the variance tends to $\bar N$ and the distribution to the Poisson law. Hence $\sigma_N = \sqrt{\bar N}$ and the ratio follows. $\blacksquare$

Apply it where it bites hardest. Model one half-sarcomere of a myofibril — one of the rod-like bundles of filaments that fill a muscle fibre — as a cylinder of diameter $1\ \mathrm{\mu m}$ and length $1.35\ \mathrm{\mu m}$ — half of the optimal sarcomere length $\ell_0\approx2.7\ \mathrm{\mu m}$ of Module 5 §2 — giving $V = 1.06\ \mathrm{\mu m^3} = 1.06\ \mathrm{fL}$. Both dimensions are idealisations of a structure that is neither a perfect cylinder nor uniformly filled, and the conclusion below is an order of magnitude, not a measurement.

What this section licenses

Three things are now available that were not. Any energy in this course can be written per molecule or per mole and the two forms compared, provided the composition is stated — Proposition 1.1. Any concentration can be turned into a spacing, a count, or a fluctuation, so a chemical number can be laid against a molecular length — the identity $n_V = cN_A$ and Proposition 1.2. And the interesting range is fixed at roughly $1$ to $40\,k_BT$, which is the band §2 through §7 live inside.

What is not available yet is a way to compute any of those energies. Lemma 1.1 priced the entropy of dilution and nothing else; the enthalpy of a bond, the entropy of a chain, the free energy of a reaction and the rate at which it runs are still quoted rather than derived. §2 takes the first of those and does it properly: it defines an interatomic potential, differentiates it twice, sums over neighbours, and pays back the debt §0 opened when it calibrated the stiffness ladder backwards.
