0. Motivation — why a bond energy sets a bone's stiffness

Stand on one leg. Module 2 §2 worked out what happens inside the thigh bone when you do: for a $70\ \mathrm{kg}$ person the hip pushes down the femoral shaft with roughly $F\approx1717\ \mathrm N$, the cortical shell that carries it — the dense outer wall of the bone, as against the spongy trabecular interior — has cross-sectional area $A\approx4.62\ \mathrm{cm^2}$, and so the compressive stress is $\sigma=F/A\approx3.72\ \mathrm{MPa}$. One further step gives the strain, $\varepsilon=\sigma/E$, and with the Young's modulus $E\approx17\ \mathrm{GPa}$ (the stress a material needs per unit of strain; Module 2 §1) that is $\varepsilon\approx2.19\times10^{-4}$ — about one part in $4600$. Over a $43\ \mathrm{cm}$ femur the whole bone shortens by $0.094\ \mathrm{mm}$, about the width of a human hair. You feel nothing, which is the point.

Every step of that calculation is honest except one. $F$ came from a free-body diagram. $A$ came from a ruler. $\sigma=F/A$ is a definition and $\varepsilon=\sigma/E$ is Hooke's law. But $E\approx17\ \mathrm{GPa}$ came from nowhere: it was quoted. Somebody put a piece of bone in a testing machine, and the course wrote down what the machine said.

measured, not derived: $E$ for whole cortical bone is a composite property of mineral, collagen, water and porosity in a particular anatomical arrangement, and this course reports it from mechanical test. What Module 0 derives is the two end members that a composite bound then combines — the stiffness of the mineral and the stiffness of the protein — because those are the quantities chemistry actually sets.

That is not a small gap, and it is not confined to bone. The same silence covers the fixed charge density of cartilage, the friction coefficient of a joint surface, the gain of the bone-remodeling law, Hill's constant for muscle, and every constitutive time constant in the book. A course that computes three significant figures downstream of a number it never explains has taught mechanics, not physiology.

The three-layer question. Every module from here on opens by asking it, and this module supplies the third answer.

  1. What do you observe? A bone that shortens by $0.094\ \mathrm{mm}$ under body load. That is physics, and Modules 1–17 already do it well.
  2. Which tissue does it? Cortical bone: collagen fibrils stiffened by plates of a calcium-phosphate mineral, laid down and removed by living cells. That is biology, and it is the layer the course mentions but rarely computes.
  3. Which molecule makes that tissue behave that way? A calcium ion bonded to an oxygen of a phosphate group, resisting displacement, along the direction the load takes, with an effective stiffness of roughly $25\ \mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}$. That is chemistry, and it is missing entirely.

The three layers are not three subjects. They are one chain, and Fig. 1 runs it downward and then back up: from the bone you stand on, to the mineralised fibril that builds it, to the single bond whose stiffness — divided by its own length — is the modulus of the mineral.

The bridge, in one line of dimensional reasoning

Why should a bond stiffness divided by a bond length be a modulus at all? Count. Take a cube of material whose atoms sit on a lattice of spacing $r_0$, and pull on it. Across any cross-section of area $A$ there are about $A/r_0^{2}$ bonds, because each bond claims a patch of area $r_0^{2}$. Stretch the material by strain $\varepsilon$ and — if the deformation is affine, meaning every bond stretches in the same proportion as the block does — each bond lengthens by $x=\varepsilon r_0$ and pulls back with $f=kx=k\varepsilon r_0$. The stress is the total force over the area:

$$\sigma \;=\; \frac{(A/r_0^{2})\,f}{A} \;=\; \frac{k\,\varepsilon\,r_0}{r_0^{2}} \;=\; \frac{k}{r_0}\,\varepsilon \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad E \;\sim\; \frac{k}{r_0}.$$

Here $k$ is the bond stiffness, the second derivative of the interatomic potential at its minimum, in $\mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}$; $r_0$ is the equilibrium bond length, in metres; $\sigma$, $\varepsilon$ and $E$ are the stress, strain and Young's modulus of Module 2 §1. The result is a scaling argument, not yet a theorem: the real answer carries a factor of order one that depends on the coordination number of the lattice (how many nearest neighbours each atom bonds to) and on which direction you pull. Where this $k$ could be confused with the remodeling gain $k$ of Module 2 §7 or the permeability $k$ of Module 4, this course writes it $k_{\text{bond}}$. §2 does it properly — defines the potential, takes the second derivative, sums over neighbours, and proves the bound. What the one-liner already buys is the order of magnitude, and the order of magnitude is the entire story.

Put the four bond types of biological matter into it — but read the column headed $k$ carefully, because it is not what a spectroscopist would quote. A bare $\mathrm{C\!-\!C}$ stretching force constant is about $450\ \mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}$, and an isolated $\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}\!-\!\mathrm{O^{2-}}$ pair is stiffer still. Coulomb attraction alone will not give the number: $-C/r$ with $C=q_1q_2/4\pi\epsilon_0$ has no minimum at all, and its curvature $-2C/r_0^{3}$ is negative. Add the short-range repulsion $B/r^{n}$ that stops the ions merging, impose $\mathrm{d}U/\mathrm{d}r=0$ at $r_0$, and the curvature becomes $k_{\text{pair}}=(n-1)C/r_0^{3}$, which for the Born exponent $n=8$ — Pauling’s rule averages the argon core of $\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}$ with the neon core of $\mathrm{O^{2-}}$, and §2 says why that number may not be fitted to a compressibility — and $r_0=0.24\ \mathrm{nm}$ is about $467\ \mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}$. Here $q_1=+2e$ and $q_2=-2e$ are the ionic charges and $\epsilon_0$ is the permittivity of free space — not the strain $\varepsilon$, which shares its letter and nothing else. Through $E\sim k/r_0$ that pair would predict about $1950\ \mathrm{GPa}$ for apatite: seventeen times what apatite actually is. The gap is geometry. Real bonds are not aligned with the load, lattices are not simple cubic, a triple helix uncoils before its hydrogen bonds ever stretch, and mineral platelets slide along the collagen they sit in. So the table lists the effective per-bond stiffness along the load path, defined as the $k$ for which $E=k/r_0$ holds. §2 is the section that has to earn the difference: it takes the bare force constant, puts in the coordination number and the loading direction, and shows what survives. Here the four numbers are simply displayed, so the ladder can be seen at once.

No interatomic potential yields a collagen fibril stiffness forward, and §2 shows the attempt overshoots fiftyfold. That is wrong, and §4 Proposition 4.3 is where it was caught — an entropic network reaches only $1.67\ \mathrm{MPa}$ at a real protein's crosslink spacing, and at most $108\ \mathrm{MPa}$ even with a crosslink at every single residue, against a fibril's $1070\ \mathrm{MPa}$. So the mechanism is short by $642\times$ at a real protein's crosslink spacing, and by $300$ to $500\times$ even at the tightest spacing for which Proposition 4.3's Gaussian hypothesis still holds — either way not by fifty. The real ladder is energetic throughout: helix bending and unwinding, then intermolecular slip between the staggered molecules inside a fibril. §4 is where that debt was examined and found to be misdescribed.

What §0 does establish is that the calibration lands where chemistry says it should. The stiff phase calibrates onto the ionic rung and the compliant phase onto the hydrogen rung: two rungs apart on a ladder spanning five decades, in the order and with roughly the spacing the bond types predict. That is a consistency check, not a derivation, and the difference is worth stating twice. Apatite is stiff because it is held by ionic bonds, short and strong. Collagen is a hundred times softer even though its own backbone is covalent and stiffer than either — because a collagen fibril loaded in tension does not stretch its backbone first. It uncoils, and it slides molecule against molecule, and both of those are resisted by hydrogen bonds and by water, not by the peptide chain. The general form of the lesson: a material is as stiff as the softest link in its load path, not as the stiffest bond anywhere in it. Which link that is depends on the architecture, which is why the ladder alone cannot finish the argument and §2 must.

The ruler: thermal energy

One more quantity has to be on the table before any of this can be judged, and it is the quantity the rest of the course has never needed: the size of a thermal kick. At body temperature $T=310.15\ \mathrm K$, Boltzmann's constant $k_B=1.381\times10^{-23}\ \mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}$ and Avogadro's number $N_A=6.022\times10^{23}\ \mathrm{mol^{-1}}$ give

$$k_BT \;=\; 4.28\times10^{-21}\ \mathrm J \;=\; 4.28\ \mathrm{zJ} \;=\; 26.7\ \mathrm{meV},\qquad R_gT \;=\; N_A k_B T \;=\; 2.58\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}.$$

Here $R_g=N_Ak_B$ is the gas constant — written $R_g$ throughout this course because $R$ is already the joint reaction force of Module 3 §4 — $\mathrm{zJ}$ is the zeptojoule, $10^{-21}\ \mathrm J$, the natural unit for one molecule; and $\mathrm{meV}$ is the millielectronvolt, $1.602\times10^{-22}\ \mathrm J$. $k_BT$ is the energy per molecule that temperature hands out for free. Anything a molecule must do that costs much less than $k_BT$ happens constantly, unbidden, whether or not it is useful. Anything that costs much more than $k_BT$ still happens, but exponentially rarely — at a rate carrying the Arrhenius factor $e^{-\Delta E/k_BT}$ — unless something pays for it. §6 makes that factor quantitative; here it is enough that rare and impossible are different words.

Now measure standing on one leg against that ruler. Each Ca–O bond in the loaded femur stretches by $x=\varepsilon r_0 = (2.19\times10^{-4})(0.24\ \mathrm{nm}) = 5.25\times10^{-14}\ \mathrm m$ — fifty femtometres, which is one part in $4600$ of the bond's own length. Under the affine assumption the bond carries the strain the whole bone carries, and $\varepsilon$ was $2.19\times10^{-4}$. That is an upper estimate: Module 2 §5 puts bone nearer the Reuss floor, so the mineral's share of the strain is smaller still, and the conclusion below only gets stronger. The elastic energy it stores is $\tfrac12kx^{2}=3.44\times10^{-26}\ \mathrm J$, which is

$$\frac{\tfrac12 k x^{2}}{k_BT} \;=\; 8.0\times10^{-6},\qquad\text{i.e. the thermal jiggle is about } 1.2\times10^{5} \text{ times larger.}$$

Standing on your leg perturbs a bond a hundred thousand times more gently than body temperature already does. Put the same comparison in displacement rather than energy and it reads the same way. The mechanical stretch is $52\ \mathrm{fm}$; the bond's own thermal amplitude is $\sqrt{k_BT/k}=\sqrt{(4.28\times10^{-21})/25}\approx13\ \mathrm{pm}$, two hundred and fifty times larger. Your body weight is a whisper laid on top of a shout.

Be careful about what that buys, because it is easy to claim too much. It establishes one thing cleanly: the bond stays harmonic. A displacement that small never leaves the quadratic bottom of the potential well, so the bond's response is linear. Whole-bone linearity needs more than that — it also needs no sliding at the mineral–collagen interfaces and no microcracks opening — and this ratio says nothing about either.

The ratio does not, by itself, say where bone's viscoelasticity lives either. The discriminator there is barrier height, not bias. Rearranging an ionic lattice means crossing a barrier of order an electronvolt, about $40\,k_BT$, so that event has a rate near zero whatever tiny bias you add: the mineral does not creep. A hydrogen bond costs a few $k_BT$ to break, so hydrogen bonds break and re-form constantly on their own, and a small persistent bias summed over very many such events is a drift you can measure. That is why the viscoelasticity cortical bone does show under sustained load has to live in the protein, the water and the interfaces between them, and it is where §4 and Module 6 go looking for it.

What the ratio does not explain at all is fracture. Bone fatigue is microcrack accumulation driven by stress concentration — a continuum process, not a thermally activated bond event — and a bad enough fall breaks a femur in one go, statically. The honest neighbouring claim, that physiological load cannot break bone in a single squeeze, comes from Module 2 §2's safety factor of $n\approx45$, not from $k_BT$. The ruler is powerful and narrow: it tells you which molecular events are available to the body, and it is silent about everything that happens above the scale of one bond.

1. The mole, concentration, and the $k_BT$ / $R_gT$ bridge

Module 5 §2 states the central bookkeeping fact of muscle contraction in one line: one ATP is hydrolysed per crossbridge per cycle. It never says what that costs. It cannot, because every biochemistry table prices ATP hydrolysis in kilojoules per mole, and every mechanical quantity in this course — a force, a displacement, the work of one pull — belongs to one object. Those are two languages for one physical fact. Until they become one sentence, the chemistry of Module 5 and the mechanics of Module 5 cannot be checked against each other at all, and the course cannot notice if they disagree.

This section builds the dictionary, in three entries. How to count molecules: the mole. How to say how crowded they are: concentration. How to turn an energy carried by one molecule into an energy quoted per mole, and back: the $k_BT \leftrightarrow R_gT$ bridge. The first two are conventions and cost nothing to accept. The third does the real work, and it carries one condition that is easy to miss and expensive to miss — the reason the same reaction is worth $-30.5\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ in a table and $-50\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ in a working muscle.

Counting: the mole

Chemistry counts. A reaction consumes its reagents in whole-number ratios of molecules, never in round numbers of grams, so the natural measure of "how much" is a count. Counts of molecules are inconveniently large, so chemistry keeps them in a bundle.

Definition 1.1 (Amount of substance; the mole). Let a sample contain $N$ specified entities — molecules, ions, formula units; the entity must be named. Its amount of substance is $$n \;=\; \frac{N}{N_A},\qquad N_A \;=\; 6.02214076\times10^{23}\ \mathrm{mol^{-1}},$$ where $N_A$ is the Avogadro constant. The unit of $n$ is the mole (mol); since the 2019 redefinition of the SI, one mole contains exactly $N_A$ entities by definition, so $N_A$ is exact and carries no experimental uncertainty. $N$ is a pure count and is dimensionless.

Two collisions to name before they bite. The letter $n$ is the Born repulsion exponent in §0 and the safety factor $n\approx45$ of Module 2 §2; it means amount of substance only where a mole is under discussion, and this course never puts two of the three in one equation. Molar mass — the mass of one mole of a substance, $M_m = m/n$ in $\mathrm{kg\,mol^{-1}}$ or the more usual $\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}$ — is written $M_m$ and not $M$, because $M$ is already the body mass $70\ \mathrm{kg}$ of Module 2 §2 and the bending moment of Module 2 §3.

The mole earns its place as a translator between two instruments. A balance weighs; it returns a mass. A molecule reacts; it responds to a count. $M_m$ converts one into the other, and $N_A$ converts a count of moles into a count of molecules. Every number in the rest of this module passes through that pair at least once.

Concentration, and the units the body is quoted in

A count alone does not say whether a molecule will meet a partner. Crowding does, and crowding is concentration.

Definition 1.2 (Molar concentration). For an amount $n$ of a named species dissolved in a solution of volume $V$, the molar concentration is $c = n/V$. The unit is the molar, $1\ \mathrm M = 1\ \mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}} = 10^{3}\ \mathrm{mol\,m^{-3}}$. Physiological concentrations are usually quoted in millimolar ($\mathrm{mM}$, $10^{-3}\ \mathrm M$), micromolar ($\mathrm{\mu M}$, $10^{-6}\ \mathrm M$) or nanomolar ($\mathrm{nM}$, $10^{-9}\ \mathrm M$). Note that $V$ is the volume of the solution, not of the solvent, and that $c$ is defined for a species in a phase — "the concentration of calcium" is meaningless until one says free calcium, in which compartment.

Molar concentration is a laboratory unit: it is what a pipette delivers. It is not yet a molecular picture. To get one, convert it into a number density and then into a distance, because a distance is a thing the reader can hold against a bond length.

$$n_V \;=\; c\,N_A, \qquad 1\ \mathrm M \;=\; 0.6022\ \mathrm{nm^{-3}}, \qquad \bar d \;=\; n_V^{-1/3} \;=\; \frac{1.184\ \mathrm{nm}}{(c/\mathrm M)^{1/3}}.$$

Here $n_V$ is the number density, the count of that species per unit volume, in $\mathrm{nm^{-3}}$; and $\bar d$ is the mean spacing, the edge of the cube of solution that contains on average one such molecule. The middle identity is the whole conversion in one line: a one-molar solution holds about six-tenths of a molecule per cubic nanometre, so its molecules sit about $1.2\ \mathrm{nm}$ apart — four or five bond lengths, on the scale §0 was working in. The cube root compresses hard, which is the useful part: dropping the concentration by a factor of a thousand only stretches the spacing by ten.

Two further conventions appear in this course because the tissues do. The first counts charge rather than molecules.

Definition 1.3 (Equivalents; osmolarity). For a species carrying charge number $z$ (so $+2$ for $\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}$, $-1$ for $\mathrm{Cl^-}$), the equivalent concentration is $c_{\mathrm{eq}} = |z|\,c$, in equivalents per litre ($\mathrm{Eq\,L^{-1}}$); it counts moles of elementary charge instead of moles of particles. The osmolarity is the total concentration of all dissolved particles, regardless of identity, in $\mathrm{osmol\,L^{-1}}$: a salt that fully dissociates contributes one osmole per ion, so $0.15\ \mathrm M$ sodium chloride is $0.30\ \mathrm{osmol\,L^{-1}}$.

Equivalents matter here for one reason. The cartilage literature quotes the fixed charge density of the tissue in milliequivalents per millilitre, and Module 4 §2 writes it as a molarity, $0.2\ \mathrm M$. Those are the same statement only because the fixed charges — the sulfate and carboxyl groups carried by the proteoglycans, the bottle-brush molecules of the cartilage matrix, each a protein core hung with many long sugar chains — are singly charged: for $|z|=1$, $1\ \mathrm{mEq\,mL^{-1}} = 1\ \mathrm M$ exactly. Reading a divalent number the same way would be wrong by a factor of two. Where that fixed charge density itself comes from, this course still owes an answer: Module 4 takes it from measurement, and §7 is the section that has to count the charged groups on a glycosaminoglycan — one of those sugar chains — and get $0.2\ \mathrm M$ out.

Osmolarity is recorded here only as a definition; the pressure it generates is §5's business and Module 4 §2's.

The bridge: an energy per molecule, an energy per mole

Now the entry that does the work. A chemical table gives $\Delta G$ per mole — a change in Gibbs free energy, the thermodynamic potential a system at constant temperature and pressure drives downhill, written $G$ here and not to be confused with the shear modulus $G$ of Module 2 §4; a mechanical calculation gives an energy per molecule; §0 has already fixed the ruler, $k_BT = 4.28\ \mathrm{zJ}$ per molecule and $R_gT = 2.58\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ per mole at body temperature. The temptation is to declare the conversion trivial — multiply by $N_A$ — and move on. It is almost trivial, and the exception is the whole reason ATP is not worth what its table says.

The exception comes from counting, which makes it this section's business rather than §3's. A molecule's freedom of position carries free energy: a dilute molecule has more places it could be than a concentrated one, and places to be are entropy.

Lemma 1.1 (The free-energy cost of dilution). Model a solution as $M_s$ equivalent sites, each able to hold at most one solute molecule, containing $N$ identical solute molecules that do not interact with one another and each of which has the same internal energy $u_0$. Then at temperature $T$ the free-energy cost of adding one more solute molecule — its chemical potential $\mu$, written $\mu$ throughout the chemistry of this course, while the friction coefficient of Module 4 §7 is written $\mu_{\text{fric}}$ — is $$\mu \;=\; u_0 \;+\; k_BT\,\ln\!\frac{N}{M_s-N} \;\;\xrightarrow{\;N\,\ll\,M_s\;}\;\; u_0 + k_BT\ln\frac{c}{c_{\text{site}}},$$ where $c$ is the solute concentration and $c_{\text{site}}$ the concentration of sites. Consequently, for any two concentrations $c$ and $c^{\circ}$ of the same dilute species, $$\mu(c) \;=\; \mu(c^{\circ}) \;+\; k_BT\,\ln\frac{c}{c^{\circ}}.$$

Choosing which $N$ of the $M_s$ sites are occupied is the only freedom the model has, so the number of arrangements is the binomial coefficient $\Omega = M_s!\,/\,[N!\,(M_s-N)!]$ and the entropy is $S = k_B\ln\Omega$. For $M_s, N \gg 1$, Stirling's approximation $\ln X! \approx X\ln X - X$ gives

$$\frac{S}{k_B} \;=\; M_s\ln M_s - N\ln N - (M_s-N)\ln(M_s-N),$$

the linear terms having cancelled identically. Differentiating with respect to $N$,

$$\frac{1}{k_B}\frac{\partial S}{\partial N} \;=\; -\ln N - 1 + \ln(M_s-N) + 1 \;=\; -\ln\frac{N}{M_s-N}.$$

The free energy at fixed volume is the Helmholtz free energy $A = Nu_0 - TS$ — written $A$ and not the usual $F$, because $F$ is a force everywhere else in this course, including the femoral force of §0. So the cost of one more molecule at fixed $T$ and $M_s$ is $\mu = \partial A/\partial N = u_0 - T\,\partial S/\partial N = u_0 + k_BT\ln[N/(M_s-N)]$, which is the first claim. When $N \ll M_s$ the denominator is $M_s$ to first order, and dividing numerator and denominator by the volume turns the ratio of counts into the ratio of concentrations, $N/M_s = c/c_{\text{site}}$. The last claim follows by writing that result at $c$ and at $c^{\circ}$ and subtracting: $\epsilon$ and $c_{\text{site}}$ are properties of the species and the solvent, not of how much of it is present, so both cancel. $\blacksquare$

One bookkeeping note, since the next result speaks of Gibbs and this one of Helmholtz. Inserting one dilute solute molecule at constant pressure does $p\,\Delta V$ of work against the atmosphere, and for a molecule-sized $\Delta V\approx3\times10^{-29}\ \mathrm{m^3}$ at $10^{5}\ \mathrm{Pa}$ that is under one part in a thousand of $k_BT$. So $\partial A/\partial N$ and $\partial G/\partial N$ agree far beyond the precision of anything in this course, and $\mu$ means either.

Anchor the abstraction to water, since every solution in this book is aqueous. Water itself is $55.5\ \mathrm M$ — a litre weighs $1000\ \mathrm g$ and its molar mass is $18.015\ \mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}$; at body temperature water is slightly less dense and the figure is $55.1\ \mathrm M$, a difference too small to matter anywhere below — so a solute at $1\ \mathrm{mM}$ occupies about one site in fifty-five thousand, and the dilute limit of the lemma is not a courtesy; it is comfortable by four decades. The reference concentration $c^{\circ}$ is pure convention, and chemistry has chosen $c^{\circ} = 1\ \mathrm M$. Nothing physical happens at one molar — and note that the chosen reference is not a real one-molar solution, in which one site in $55.5$ is occupied and the dilute limit of the lemma is already $2\%$ wrong, but the hypothetical ideal-dilute solution extrapolated up to $1\ \mathrm M$. The convention is chosen precisely so that the logarithm stays exact. What is physical is the difference: the lemma says that moving a species from $1\ \mathrm M$ down to $1\ \mathrm{\mu M}$ lowers its chemical potential by $k_BT\ln(10^{6}) = 13.8\,k_BT$, and every such term must be carried through any conversion between the two languages.

Proposition 1.1 (The bridge, and its condition). Consider a reaction among dilute solutes with stoichiometric coefficients $\nu_i$, negative for a reactant and positive for a product, held at a fixed composition $\{c_i\}$. Define $\Delta g \equiv \sum_i \nu_i\mu_i$: the change in the free energy of the whole solution when the reaction advances by one molecule at that unchanged composition, with $\mu_i$ the chemical potential of Lemma 1.1. Then the change when it advances by one mole is $$\Delta G \;=\; N_A\,\Delta g, \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad \frac{\Delta G}{R_gT} \;=\; \frac{\Delta g}{k_BT},$$ so an energy of $x\,k_BT$ per molecule is an energy of $x\,R_gT$ per mole, and at $T = 310.15\ \mathrm K$ that is $2.58x\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$. Both sides must be read at the same composition. Two figures quoted at different compositions differ additionally by the Lemma 1.1 terms $$\Delta g - \Delta g^{\circ} \;=\; k_BT\sum_i \nu_i \ln\frac{c_i}{c_i^{\circ}},$$ which vanishes when that sum is zero — in particular at the standard state, where every $c_i = c_i^{\circ}$, but also at any other composition for which the products of the concentration ratios happen to cancel.

A molar quantity is by definition the change per mole of reaction advance, and one mole of advance is $N_A$ single-molecule advances. Because the composition is held fixed, each of those $N_A$ advances costs the same $\Delta g$ — that is the only place the dilute, non-interacting assumption is used, and it is what fails in a crowded solution — so the total is $N_A\Delta g$. Note what is not claimed: that the entropy of the solution is a sum of per-molecule entropies. It is not, and Lemma 1.1 is the statement of exactly how it fails; each $\mu_i$ already carries that non-additive part. Dividing by $N_Ak_BT = R_gT$ gives the dimensionless form, and the numerical factor is $R_gT$ evaluated in §0.

For the condition, write the per-molecule free energy of species $i$ using Lemma 1.1 as $\mu_i = \mu_i^{\circ} + k_BT\ln(c_i/c_i^{\circ})$, where $\mu_i^{\circ}$ is its value at the reference concentration. The free-energy change of a reaction is the stoichiometric sum $\Delta g = \sum_i \nu_i\mu_i = \sum_i \nu_i\mu_i^{\circ} + k_BT\sum_i \nu_i\ln(c_i/c_i^{\circ})$. The first sum is what a table reports; the second vanishes when the stoichiometric sum of the logarithms is zero, which the standard state guarantees but does not monopolise. Multiplying through by $N_A$ preserves the split, so the discrepancy between a tabulated molar value and a per-molecule value measured in a cell is exactly the second sum, and multiplying by $N_A$ cannot remove it. $\blacksquare$

That second sum is the quantity chemistry calls the reaction quotient, and assembling it into $\Delta G = \Delta G^{\circ} + R_gT\ln Q$ — with the equilibrium condition that follows from setting $\Delta G$ to zero — is §3's headline result, not this section's. What this section has established is narrower and is the part the dictionary needs: a molar energy is a per-molecule energy times $N_A$, plus a concentration term that vanishes at the standard state.

What the dictionary buys: one ATP, in piconewtons

Return to the opening claim. Module 5 §2 spends one ATP per crossbridge per cycle and, in that cycle, the myosin head — the projecting motor domain of the thick filament — pulls the thin filament, the actin cable it grips, about $d = 10\ \mathrm{nm}$. Ask the question the module could not ask: how hard can it pull?

Hydrolysing ATP to ADP and inorganic phosphate releases about $50\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ inside a working muscle cell. That number is not itself measured, and it is not the $30.5\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ of the standard tables: it is computed from the tabulated standard value and the cell's measured concentrations. The difference between the two figures is exactly the concentration term of Proposition 1.1. A cell holds ATP high and its products low, so the reaction quotient is far below one, its logarithm is negative, and the reaction is worth more in the cell than at the standard state. What the laboratory supplies is the ratio $[\mathrm{ATP}]\,/\,([\mathrm{ADP}][\mathrm{P_i}])$ — where $[\mathrm X]$ is the concentration $c$ of species X, the notation chemistry uses inside a reaction quotient — and §3 is where the $\ln Q$ machinery that turns that ratio into the cellular figure is derived. Here the result is quoted and the sign of the correction explained, no more.

Run it through the dictionary. Per molecule,

$$|\Delta g| \;=\; \frac{50\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}}{N_A} \;=\; 8.30\times10^{-20}\ \mathrm J \;=\; \frac{50}{2.58}\,k_BT \;=\; 19.4\,k_BT.$$

One ATP is worth about twenty thermal quanta. Now impose the second law in the one form needed here: for a process at constant temperature and pressure, the work delivered cannot exceed the fall in Gibbs free energy that paid for it, $W \le |\Delta g|$. That inequality is borrowed — §3 derives it, alongside the equilibrium condition — and it is used here only to bound one number. Writing $\bar F \equiv \int_0^{d} F\,\mathrm dx / d$ for the displacement-averaged force the head exerts over the stroke, $W = \bar F d$, so

$$\bar F \;\le\; \frac{|\Delta g|}{d} \;=\; \frac{8.30\times10^{-20}\ \mathrm J}{10\times10^{-9}\ \mathrm m} \;=\; 8.30\ \mathrm{pN}.$$

No crossbridge in Module 5 may pull harder than about eight piconewtons averaged over a ten-nanometre stroke, whatever the model of the head says, because there is nothing to pay for more. The bound is thermodynamic and therefore cheap; what makes it interesting is that it is nearly attained. Single-molecule optical-trap experiments — one protein held in a focused laser beam, which reports the force it pulls with — put the force of one myosin head in the range of a few piconewtons — call it $3$ to $6\ \mathrm{pN}$ — which at $d = 10\ \mathrm{nm}$ is $7$ to $14\,k_BT$ of work, or $36\%$ to $72\%$ of the quantum. The crossbridge is a converter running at roughly half efficiency, and the rest leaves as heat — the crossbridge's share of the heat production Module 5 treats as a whole-muscle observable, the calcium pumps of Module 5 §3 spending ATP on top of it.

Three cautions, because a bound is easy to over-read. First, $\bar F$ is a mean over the stroke, not a peak; a head that pulls hard early and weakly late satisfies the bound with a peak force above it. Second, the $10\ \mathrm{nm}$ is Module 5's model stroke, and the ceiling goes as $1/d$: halve the stroke and the bound doubles. The product $\bar F d$ is what the ATP fixes, not either factor alone. Third, the quoted $3$–$6\ \mathrm{pN}$ comes from isolated proteins in a trap, not from a head inside a lattice of filaments, and the two need not agree. Fourth, and most consequentially, the $50\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ is itself a property of the cell's composition rather than of ATP, so the ceiling is not a constant of the tissue: §3 computes it as $10.38\ \mathrm{pN}$ in resting muscle and $8.76\ \mathrm{pN}$ at the end of a fatiguing bout, and the round figure used here sits at the fatigued end of that range.

What could not be done before this section was to write the question down. That is the recurring value of a unit: it does not answer anything, but it makes an answer expressible, and an inexpressible question never gets checked.

Where "concentration" stops being a number

One limit has to be stated before the rest of the module leans on concentrations, and the ladder above already hinted at it. A concentration is a density, and a density presumes enough molecules to average. Physiology routinely violates that.

Proposition 1.2 (Counting noise). Let a dilute, non-interacting species at concentration $c$ occupy a large volume, and let $N$ be the number of its molecules found inside a fixed subvolume $V$. Then $N$ is Poisson-distributed with mean $\bar N = c\,N_A\,V$, and, writing $\sigma_N$ for the standard deviation of $N$ — not the stress $\sigma$ of §0, which shares the letter and nothing else — the relative fluctuation is $$\frac{\sigma_N}{\bar N} \;=\; \frac{1}{\sqrt{\bar N}} \;=\; \frac{1}{\sqrt{c\,N_A\,V}}.$$

Let the species have $M_{\text{tot}}$ molecules in a total volume $V_{\text{tot}} \gg V$ — written $M_{\text{tot}}$, since $M$ is the body mass and the bending moment of Module 2. Because the molecules do not interact, each is independently inside $V$ with probability $p = V/V_{\text{tot}}$, so $N$ is binomial with mean $M_{\text{tot}}p$ and variance $M_{\text{tot}}p(1-p)$. Take the limit $M_{\text{tot}}\to\infty$, $p\to0$ at fixed $M_{\text{tot}}p = \bar N = cN_AV$: the variance tends to $\bar N$ and the distribution to the Poisson law. Hence $\sigma_N = \sqrt{\bar N}$ and the ratio follows. $\blacksquare$

Apply it where it bites hardest. Model one half-sarcomere of a myofibril — one of the rod-like bundles of filaments that fill a muscle fibre — as a cylinder of diameter $1\ \mathrm{\mu m}$ and length $1.35\ \mathrm{\mu m}$ — half of the optimal sarcomere length $\ell_0\approx2.7\ \mathrm{\mu m}$ of Module 5 §2 — giving $V = 1.06\ \mathrm{\mu m^3} = 1.06\ \mathrm{fL}$. Both dimensions are idealisations of a structure that is neither a perfect cylinder nor uniformly filled, and the conclusion below is an order of magnitude, not a measurement.

2. Bonding and the stiffness ladder

§0 ended owing a calculation, and named the debt in its own margin. It displayed a ladder of four bond types whose $k/r_0$ reproduces the two moduli Module 2 §5 takes as given — $104\ \mathrm{GPa}$ for the mineral, $1.07\ \mathrm{GPa}$ for the protein — and then admitted that the $k$ column had been obtained by running $E = k/r_0$ backwards from those very moduli. The ladder was a consistency check dressed as a prediction. This section runs the arrow forwards.

Forwards means one thing precisely: no elastic measurement may appear anywhere in the inputs. What is allowed is ionic charge, an equilibrium separation from a diffraction pattern, a repulsion exponent from an electron configuration, a Madelung constant from the geometry of a lattice, and a lattice energy from a thermochemical cycle of heats and ionization energies. None of those comes from a testing machine. If a modulus falls out of them, the modulus is derived.

The section does that for the mineral rung and reaches $E \approx 143\ \mathrm{GPa}$ against a measured $114\ \mathrm{GPa}$. It does not do it for the collagen rung, and the last part explains why the failure there is structural rather than a matter of effort: collagen's compliance is not a bond-stretching number at all, so no interatomic potential can produce it and §4 must.

The potential: what holds two ions apart

An ionic bond is the simplest bond to write down, because its attractive part is a law schoolchildren know and its repulsive part needs only one number.

Definition 2.1 (the Born ionic potential). For a pair of ions of charges $z_1e$ and $z_2e$ at separation $r$, with $z_1z_2 \lt 0$, the Born potential is $$U(r) \;=\; -\,\frac{|z_1z_2|\,C_e}{r} \;+\; \frac{B}{r^{\,n}}, \qquad C_e \equiv \frac{e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0} = 2.307\times10^{-28}\ \mathrm{J\,m}.$$ The first term is the Coulomb attraction; the second is the Born repulsion, the energy cost of pushing two closed electron shells into the same space, with $B \gt 0$ a constant and $n$ the Born exponent, a pure number. Here $e = 1.602\times10^{-19}\ \mathrm C$ is the elementary charge and $\epsilon_0 = 8.854\times10^{-12}\ \mathrm{F\,m^{-1}}$ the permittivity of free space — not the strain $\varepsilon$, whose letter it shares and nothing else.

The exponent $n$ is the one input that could be smuggled in from a measurement, and it must not be. Textbooks often fit $n$ to a crystal's compressibility, which would make every prediction below circular in exactly the way §0 was. This course uses Pauling's rule instead, which assigns $n$ from an ion's electron configuration alone: $5$ for a helium core, $7$ for neon, $9$ for argon, $10$ for krypton, $12$ for xenon, and for a compound the mean of the two ions. Sodium and chloride give $\tfrac12(7+9) = 8$. Calcium, with an argon core, and oxide, with a neon core, also give $\tfrac12(9+7) = 8$. Note carefully what this does and does not buy. No elastic datum of the crystal under study enters, and none of any oxide enters. But Pauling's table is not innocent of elasticity: those five integers were themselves chosen so that the Born model would reproduce the measured compressibilities of the alkali halides. The exponent is therefore transferred, not conjured, and the honest description of what follows is a prediction out of the class the parameter was fixed on — which is the ordinary situation of any transferable force field, and is a different thing from calibrating against the answer. Where a claim below depends on this, it says so.

The pair mean matters. Taking calcium's own $n = 9$ and forgetting that oxide brings a $7$ would give $534$ rather than $467\ \mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}$ — a $14\%$ error in every stiffness downstream, because a repulsion exponent describes two electron clouds meeting and both ions own it. Here $n$ is the Born exponent throughout, not the amount of substance $n$ of §1 Definition 1.1.

Lemma 2.1 (the pair stiffness). The Born potential of Definition 2.1 has a single minimum, at a separation $r_0$ fixed by $B$, and its curvature there is $$k_{\text{pair}} \;\equiv\; \left.\frac{\mathrm d^2U}{\mathrm dr^2}\right|_{r_0} \;=\; (n-1)\,\frac{|z_1z_2|\,C_e}{r_0^{\,3}} \;\gt\; 0.$$ Neither term alone has a minimum: the attraction falls monotonically and the repulsion rises monotonically. Both are needed, and the factor $(n-1)$ is what the repulsion contributes.

Write $\mathcal A \equiv |z_1z_2|C_e$ — script $\mathcal A$, to keep it clear of the Helmholtz free energy $A$ of §1 Lemma 1.1 — so $U = -\mathcal A/r + B r^{-n}$. Then $U'(r) = \mathcal A/r^2 - nB\,r^{-(n+1)}$, and $U'(r_0) = 0$ gives $B = \mathcal A r_0^{\,n-1}/n$ — one equation, one unknown, so the minimum is unique and $B$ is determined by $r_0$ rather than the other way round. Differentiating again, $U''(r) = -2\mathcal A/r^3 + n(n+1)B\,r^{-(n+2)}$, and substituting $B$ at $r = r_0$,

$$U''(r_0) \;=\; -\frac{2\mathcal A}{r_0^{\,3}} + \frac{n(n+1)}{n}\cdot\frac{\mathcal A}{r_0^{\,3}} \;=\; \frac{\mathcal A}{r_0^{\,3}}\,(n+1-2) \;=\; (n-1)\frac{\mathcal A}{r_0^{\,3}}.$$

This is positive for every $n \gt 1$, so the stationary point is a minimum. The attractive term's own curvature is $-2\mathcal A/r_0^{3}$, which is negative: Coulomb attraction alone is not a spring, and a calculation that takes its second derivative and calls the result a bond stiffness gets the sign wrong before it gets the size wrong. $\blacksquare$

For $\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}\!-\!\mathrm{O^{2-}}$ at the $r_0 = 0.240\ \mathrm{nm}$ of §0, with $|z_1z_2| = 4$ and $n = 8$, that is $k_{\text{pair}} = 467\ \mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}$. Fig. 5 draws the potential and the parabola that Lemma 2.1 fits to its floor, and shows how narrow the harmonic region is compared with the well that contains it.

Why a pair curvature is not a modulus

Feed $467\ \mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}$ into the one-line bridge $E \sim k/r_0$ of §0 and it returns $1950\ \mathrm{GPa}$. Apatite is $114\ \mathrm{GPa}$. The estimate is seventeen times too stiff, and the temptation is to call the discrepancy a fudge factor and absorb it. It is not a fudge factor. It is two separate errors, one of which can be proved to have a definite sign.

The first error is a census. The bridge assumed a simple cubic lattice in which every atom sits at a corner of a cube of side $r_0$, so that one bond crosses every patch of area $r_0^2$ and every bond lies along the pull. Real ionic crystals are not simple cubic, their bonds point in many directions at once, and the volume each formula unit occupies is not $r_0^3$. That error has no fixed sign and has to be computed, which the next part does.

The second error does have a fixed sign, and it is worth a proposition because it settles the status of every back-of-envelope stiffness estimate in this course and in the literature it draws on.

Proposition 2.1 (the affine estimate is an upper bound). Let a solid's energy density, to second order about its relaxed state, be $$U(\boldsymbol\varepsilon, \mathbf u) \;=\; \tfrac12\,\boldsymbol\varepsilon^{\!\top}\!\mathsf A\,\boldsymbol\varepsilon \;+\; \boldsymbol\varepsilon^{\!\top}\!\mathsf B\,\mathbf u \;+\; \tfrac12\,\mathbf u^{\!\top}\!\mathsf D\,\mathbf u,$$ where $\boldsymbol\varepsilon$ is the macroscopic strain, $\mathbf u$ collects the internal displacements — the shifts of atoms within the unit cell that the macroscopic strain does not dictate — and $\mathsf D \succ 0$. That last step needs care: lattice stability gives only $\mathsf D \succeq 0$, because a rigid translation of the whole cell changes no energy and is a zero mode of $\mathbf u$. Removing those modes — hold one atom fixed — leaves $\mathsf D$ strictly positive definite, which the proof needs in order to invert it. Throughout, $\mathsf X \succ 0$ means $\mathsf X$ is positive definite, $\mathsf X \succeq 0$ positive semidefinite, and $\mathsf X \preceq \mathsf Y$ means $\mathsf Y - \mathsf X \succeq 0$: an ordering of quadratic forms, not of numbers. Let $\mathsf C_{\text{aff}} = \mathsf A$ be the stiffness obtained by forcing $\mathbf u = 0$, that is, by assuming every atom moves exactly as the macroscopic strain says (an affine deformation), and let $\mathsf C_{\text{true}}$ be the stiffness obtained by letting the internal coordinates relax. Then $$\mathsf C_{\text{true}} \;=\; \mathsf A - \mathsf B\mathsf D^{-1}\mathsf B^{\!\top} \;\preceq\; \mathsf C_{\text{aff}},$$ with equality if and only if $\mathsf B = 0$. An affine estimate can therefore be too stiff, and can never be too soft.

Fix $\boldsymbol\varepsilon$ and minimise over $\mathbf u$. The gradient is $\mathsf B^{\!\top}\boldsymbol\varepsilon + \mathsf D\mathbf u$, which vanishes at $\mathbf u^{*} = -\mathsf D^{-1}\mathsf B^{\!\top}\boldsymbol\varepsilon$; because $\mathsf D \succ 0$ this stationary point is the unique minimum. Substituting,

$$U(\boldsymbol\varepsilon, \mathbf u^{*}) \;=\; \tfrac12\,\boldsymbol\varepsilon^{\!\top}\!\left(\mathsf A - \mathsf B\mathsf D^{-1}\mathsf B^{\!\top}\right)\boldsymbol\varepsilon,$$

which identifies $\mathsf C_{\text{true}}$ as the Schur complement $\mathsf A - \mathsf B\mathsf D^{-1}\mathsf B^{\!\top}$. Since $\mathsf D^{-1} \succ 0$, the matrix $\mathsf B\mathsf D^{-1}\mathsf B^{\!\top}$ is positive semidefinite, so $\mathsf C_{\text{aff}} - \mathsf C_{\text{true}} \succeq 0$; it is zero exactly when $\mathsf B\mathsf D^{-1}\mathsf B^{\!\top} = 0$, which for invertible $\mathsf D$ means $\mathsf B = 0$. The physical reading is that $\mathbf u = 0$ is one admissible configuration among many, so the relaxed energy cannot exceed it — the same variational logic that makes a Rayleigh quotient an upper bound on a fundamental frequency. $\blacksquare$

Two consequences are worth naming. The coupling $\mathsf B$ vanishes whenever every atom sits at a centre of inversion symmetry, which is true of rocksalt and false of almost everything else; so the rocksalt calculations below are exact within their model, while a low-symmetry framework crystal like apatite — hexagonal, forty-four atoms in its cell, phosphate groups free to rotate — has room for internal relaxation and will fall below its own affine bound. And the same proposition is why Module 2 §5's Voigt average is an upper bound on a composite's modulus: assuming both phases share one strain is exactly the assumption $\mathbf u = 0$, one level of description higher. It is the same theorem twice.

The lattice done properly: stiffness is a cohesive energy density

The census error is fixed by refusing to count bonds at all. Count energy instead. A crystal has a lattice energy $U_{\text{latt}}$ — the energy of one formula unit of the solid (one $\mathrm{Ca_{10}(PO_4)_6(OH)_2}$'s worth of ions, say) measured relative to those same ions infinitely far apart, so that $U_{\text{latt}} \lt 0$ and $|U_{\text{latt}}|$ is the energy released on assembling it — and a volume $V_f$ per formula unit that a diffraction pattern gives directly. Their ratio is an energy per unit volume, and an energy per unit volume has the units of a modulus. The following says that is not a coincidence, and fixes the constant.

Proposition 2.2 (the cohesive energy density theorem). Let a crystal's energy per formula unit have the Born form as a function of a single length $r$ that scales the whole lattice, $$U(r) \;=\; -\frac{\mathcal A}{r} + \frac{\mathcal B}{r^{\,n}}, \qquad V(r) = \gamma_s\,r^3,$$ with $\mathcal A, \mathcal B, \gamma_s \gt 0$ and $\gamma_s$ a dimensionless, purely geometric constant of the structure — written $\gamma_s$ rather than the customary $c$, which is a concentration in §1 and a lattice parameter forty lines below. Then at the equilibrium spacing $r_0$ the bulk modulus is $$\boxed{\;K \;=\; \frac{n\,|U_{\text{latt}}|}{9\,V_f}\;}$$ where $U_{\text{latt}} = U(r_0)$ and $V_f = V(r_0)$. The structure constant $\gamma_s$ has cancelled: the bulk modulus of such a solid depends on its lattice energy and its formula-unit volume, and on nothing else about its geometry.

Equilibrium first. $U'(r_0) = \mathcal A/r_0^{2} - n\mathcal B r_0^{-(n+1)} = 0$ gives $\mathcal B = \mathcal A r_0^{\,n-1}/n$, so

$$U_{\text{latt}} \;=\; -\frac{\mathcal A}{r_0} + \frac{\mathcal A}{n\,r_0} \;=\; -\frac{\mathcal A}{r_0}\left(1 - \frac1n\right), \qquad\text{hence}\qquad \frac{\mathcal A}{r_0} \;=\; \frac{n}{n-1}\,|U_{\text{latt}}|.$$

The bulk modulus is $K = V\,(\mathrm d^2U/\mathrm dV^2)$ evaluated at equilibrium. Changing variable from $V$ to $r$ with $V = \gamma_sr^3$, so $\mathrm dV = 3\gamma_sr^2\,\mathrm dr$, and using $\mathrm dU/\mathrm dV = 0$ at $r_0$ to kill the term in $U'$,

$$\left.\frac{\mathrm d^2U}{\mathrm dV^2}\right|_{r_0} = \frac{1}{3\gamma_sr_0^{2}}\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dr}\!\left(\frac{U'(r)}{3\gamma_sr^{2}}\right)_{r_0} = \frac{U''(r_0)}{9\gamma_s^{2}r_0^{4}}, \qquad\text{so}\qquad K = \gamma_s\,r_0^{3}\cdot\frac{U''(r_0)}{9\gamma_s^{2}r_0^{4}} = \frac{U''(r_0)}{9\,\gamma_s\,r_0}.$$

The algebra of Lemma 2.1 used only the form $-\mathcal A/r + \mathcal B r^{-n}$ and not the meaning of $\mathcal A$, so it applies verbatim here: $U''(r_0) = (n-1)\mathcal A/r_0^{3}$. Substituting that and then $\mathcal A/r_0 = n|U_{\text{latt}}|/(n-1)$,

$$K \;=\; \frac{(n-1)\mathcal A}{9\,\gamma_s\,r_0^{4}} \;=\; \frac{(n-1)}{9\,\gamma_s\,r_0^{3}}\cdot\frac{n\,|U_{\text{latt}}|}{n-1} \;=\; \frac{n\,|U_{\text{latt}}|}{9\,\gamma_s\,r_0^{3}} \;=\; \frac{n\,|U_{\text{latt}}|}{9\,V_f}.$$

The factor $(n-1)$ has cancelled against the $(n-1)$ hidden in the well depth, and $\gamma_s$ has cancelled against $V_f = \gamma_sr_0^3$. $\blacksquare$

Read the boxed result slowly, because it is the section's engine and it is not obvious. The bulk modulus of an ionic solid is its lattice energy density $|U_{\text{latt}}|/V_f$, multiplied by $n/9$. It does not care whether the structure is cubic or hexagonal, how many nearest neighbours each ion has, or which direction the load takes. All of that is folded into the two numbers already, because $U_{\text{latt}}$ was summed over the real lattice and $V_f$ was measured on it. Independent evidence that this is the right variable: Zhang and Tamilselvan (J. Mater. Sci. Mater. Med. 18, 79–87, 2007) tabulated nineteen simple ionic crystals and found their Voigt and Reuss moduli linearly proportional to lattice-energy density. Proposition 2.2 is the proof of that empirical line, and it supplies the slope.

For the special case of a rocksalt lattice the theorem can be run without any thermochemistry at all, because the lattice energy is a Coulomb sum. Summing $\pm C_e/r$ over every ion in a rocksalt lattice gives $\mathcal A = \alpha_M|z_1z_2|C_e$ with the Madelung constant $\alpha_M = 1.7476$, a pure number that depends only on where the ions sit; and rocksalt packs one formula unit into $V_f = 2r_0^{3}$, so $\gamma_s = 2$. Substituting both into Proposition 2.2,

$$K_{\text{rocksalt}} \;=\; \frac{(n-1)\,\alpha_M\,|z_1z_2|\,C_e}{18\,r_0^{\,4}}.$$

Everything on the right is a charge, a distance from a diffraction pattern, an electron configuration and a lattice sum.

Apatite, forwards

Now the number this module was built to produce. Bone mineral is hydroxyapatite, $\mathrm{Ca_{10}(PO_4)_6(OH)_2}$: a hexagonal crystal, space group $P6_3/m$, with lattice parameters $a = 0.94166\ \mathrm{nm}$ and $c = 0.68745\ \mathrm{nm}$ and exactly one formula unit per cell. Its volume per formula unit is therefore a piece of trigonometry,

$$V_f \;=\; \frac{\sqrt3}{2}\,a^2c \;=\; 0.5279\ \mathrm{nm^3} \;=\; 5.279\times10^{-28}\ \mathrm{m^3},$$

and its lattice energy has been measured thermochemically. Zhang and Tamilselvan report $|U_{\text{latt}}| = 34\,191\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ from a Born–Fajans–Haber cycle, and two independent estimates — a generalized Kapustinskii equation, which predicts a lattice energy from ionic radii and charges alone, and the Glasser–Jenkins method, which predicts it from the formula-unit volume — that agree within $4\%$. One feature of that cycle matters for what follows: its reference state is the gaseous polyatomic ions, so the phosphate group is assembled and intact, and its internal $\mathrm{P\!-\!O}$ covalent bonds are not part of $U_{\text{latt}}$. What the number prices is the electrostatic assembly of $\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}$, $\mathrm{PO_4^{3-}}$ and $\mathrm{OH^-}$, and the $\mathrm{Ca}$–$\mathrm{O}$ contact it describes is calcium against a phosphate oxygen, not against a bare $\mathrm{O^{2-}}$. The lattice energy is enormous because the formula unit is enormous: ten doubly charged cations and eight anions, six of them triply charged. Divided by the cell it occupies, it is a lattice energy density of $108\ \mathrm{GJ\,m^{-3}}$. With the Pauling exponent $n = 8$ for the $\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}$–oxygen contacts that bind the framework, Proposition 2.2 gives

$$K_{\text{apatite}} \;=\; \frac{n\,|U_{\text{latt}}|}{9V_f} \;=\; \frac{8 \times (34\,191\times10^{3}/N_A)\ \mathrm J}{9 \times 5.279\times10^{-28}\ \mathrm{m^3}} \;=\; 95.6\ \mathrm{GPa}.$$

The measured bulk modulus of dense hydroxyapatite is about $85\ \mathrm{GPa}$. The prediction is high by $12\%$, and the two alternative lattice energies give $91.7$ and $92.3\ \mathrm{GPa}$, so the spread from the least certain input is $4\%$ and not the source of the residual.

Young's modulus needs one more step, and it must not import a measured Poisson ratio. Here $\nu$ is Poisson's ratio — minus the transverse strain per unit axial strain — not the stoichiometric coefficient $\nu_i$ of §1 Proposition 1.1; and $C_{11}$, $C_{12}$, $C_{44}$ are the Voigt-notation components of the stiffness tensor, the coefficients relating stress components to strain components. A solid held together by central pair forces between atoms that each sit at a centre of inversion obeys the Cauchy relation $C_{12} = C_{44}$; combined with the isotropy condition $C_{11} - C_{12} = 2C_{44}$, that gives $\nu = C_{12}/(C_{11}+C_{12}) = \tfrac14$. Sodium chloride, the crystal this model handles best, measures $\nu = 0.25$.

State plainly what that step costs, because this section has already denied one of its hypotheses. The paragraph after Proposition 2.1 said that the inversion-centre condition holds for rocksalt and fails for a low-symmetry framework like apatite — and it is that same condition the Cauchy relation needs. So $\nu = \tfrac14$ is not derived for apatite. It is the value the central-force model carries with it, adopted here as an explicit assumption rather than as a result, because the alternative is to import a measured elastic constant and forfeit the whole claim. Real apatite measures $\nu \approx 0.27$, and using that instead would lower the prediction from $143$ to $132\ \mathrm{GPa}$ — so the assumption costs about $8\%$, in the same direction as every other error in this model. And it is less accurate than the $104\ \mathrm{GPa}$ that §0's calibrated ladder returned, which is exactly what one should expect and is the whole point of the distinction: a number reverse-engineered from the answer agrees with the answer perfectly and predicts nothing.

Where the factor of seventeen went

The naive bridge said $1950\ \mathrm{GPa}$ and the crystal is $114\ \mathrm{GPa}$. With the calculation done properly, the gap splits into two computed factors and one residual — which is a better account than "geometry", though only the first two are computed and the third is what the model does not contain. Compare the two formulas at the same bond length. The naive estimate is $E_{\text{naive}} = k_{\text{pair}}/r_0 = (n-1)|z_1z_2|C_e/r_0^{4}$; the boxed rocksalt result is $K = (n-1)\alpha_M|z_1z_2|C_e/(18r_0^{4})$; and $E = \tfrac32K$. Every factor cancels but two:

$$\frac{E_{\text{lattice}}}{E_{\text{naive}}} \;=\; \frac{3}{2}\cdot\frac{\alpha_M}{18} \;=\; \frac{\alpha_M}{12} \;=\; 0.146 \;=\; \frac{1}{6.87}.$$

So a factor of $6.87$ of the seventeen is pure lattice bookkeeping, and it is not a fitted correction — it is $\alpha_M/12$, a Madelung constant over a number that came out of Proposition 2.2's chain rule. It splits into two opposing pieces worth separating. The Madelung constant $\alpha_M = 1.75$ makes the lattice stiffer than an isolated pair, because every ion is pulled on by all its shells and not merely its nearest neighbour. Against that, the factor $1/12$ says the naive census was far too generous: one bond per $r_0^2$ of cross-section with every bond aligned to the pull is a much denser and better-oriented load path than a real crystal offers, where a formula unit occupies $2r_0^{3}$ rather than $r_0^{3}$ and volumetric strain is shared among bonds pointing every way.

That closes the gap for a dense $\mathrm{Ca}$–$\mathrm{O}$ rocksalt: $1950 \to 284\ \mathrm{GPa}$, against a measured $\mathrm{CaO}$ Young's modulus near $200\ \mathrm{GPa}$. The remaining descent from $284$ to apatite's $143$ is not geometry at all — it is dilution. Apatite is not a dense lattice of $\mathrm{Ca}$–$\mathrm{O}$ contacts; it is a framework of phosphate tetrahedra and hydroxyl channels through which calcium is threaded, so the same chemistry is spread over more volume and the lattice energy density drops. Proposition 2.2 measures precisely that: $\mathrm{CaO}$ carries $204\ \mathrm{GJ\,m^{-3}}$ of lattice energy and apatite $108$, a ratio of $0.53$. The $\times0.51$ drawn in the figure is that $0.53$ carrying a $4\%$ rider, because the $284$ above it came by the Coulomb route while apatite's $143$ came by the thermochemical one, and the two routes differ by that much on $\mathrm{CaO}$. The last step, $143 \to 114$, is the polarizability residual. Fig. 7 lays the descent out as a ladder with each factor named.

This revises, in emphasis, what §0 said when it wrote that "the gap is geometry" and listed bond misalignment and platelet sliding. Misalignment is real and is inside the $1/12$; sliding and uncoiling are real and belong to the composite, not the mineral. But the dominant term for the mineral is neither. It is that a modulus is a cohesive energy density and the naive count got the energy density wrong — too much energy assigned to too little volume — which is a bookkeeping error about the whole lattice rather than a geometric fact about one bond.

Why collagen is not a rung on this ladder

Which leaves the debt that will not be paid here. Module 2 §5 needs a collagen modulus near $1\ \mathrm{GPa}$, and §0's ladder produced $1.07\ \mathrm{GPa}$ by calibrating an effective hydrogen-bond stiffness of $0.30\ \mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}$ backwards from it. Try the forward calculation and it fails immediately, and the size of the failure is the diagnosis. A real hydrogen bond has a bare stretching stiffness near $15\ \mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}$ at $r_0 = 0.28\ \mathrm{nm}$; through $E \sim k/r_0$ that is $54\ \mathrm{GPa}$, fifty-four times what a collagen fibril actually is. Even applying the full $1/6.87$ lattice correction derived above leaves $7.8\ \mathrm{GPa}$, still eightfold too stiff. And the fibril's own backbone is covalent and stiffer still.

No adjustment of the potential will close that, because the premise is wrong. Proposition 2.2 assumes the load path runs through the bonds — that straining the solid strains every bond in proportion. A collagen fibril in tension does no such thing. It is a rope of triple helices, themselves supercoiled, staggered end to end on the $67\ \mathrm{nm}$ D-period — the axial offset by which each molecule sits displaced from its neighbour — and embedded in water. Fig. 8 lays the four stages of that load path side by side. Pulling it first straightens the molecular crimp, then uncoils the supercoil, then slides molecule past molecule through a shear layer of water and cross-links. Not one of those steps stretches a bond length. They rearrange the molecule rather than stretching a bond, so the affine bond-counting estimate cannot apply to them. It is tempting to conclude that the restoring force is therefore entropic, $f = -T\,\partial S/\partial x$, with a stiffness set by $k_BT$ and a contour length. That conclusion is wrong, and §4 disproves it by computing the entropic modulus: it reaches $1.67\ \mathrm{MPa}$ at a protein's real crosslink spacing and $108\ \mathrm{MPa}$ at the physical limit, against a fibril's $1070\ \mathrm{MPa}$. The first three of the four steps are instead energetic compliances — bond angles rather than bond lengths, which are far softer than a stretch without being entropic at all. Proposition 2.1 gives the sign of the miss and only the sign: the uncoiling degrees of freedom are exactly the internal coordinates $\mathbf u$ the affine bound freezes, so relaxing them can only lower the modulus. It says nothing about the size, and it cannot, because it is a harmonic bound on an energy, while the compliance here is a bond-angle relaxation whose size a harmonic bound cannot give. The factor of fifty is §4's to compute, not this section's to bound — and when §4 computes it, the answer is that no Gaussian entropic network produces fifty, or comes within two orders of magnitude of a gigapascal.

So the collagen rung stays owed, and it is owed to a different section. §4 builds the freely jointed and worm-like chain models and computes an entropic modulus from $k_BT$, a persistence length and a contour length — and finds that it accounts for elastin and not for collagen; §7 supplies the Gly-X-Y architecture — the repeating triplet in which every third residue is glycine, the only amino acid small enough to sit where the three strands touch — that sets those lengths. Nothing about collagen is boxed here, because a number this section cannot derive should not be given the typography of one.

What this section licenses

Four things are now available. A modulus can be computed from chemistry alone, by Proposition 2.2, for any solid whose cohesion is ionic — and the recipe needs only a lattice energy and a cell volume. The accuracy of that recipe is known and quantified rather than hoped for: within $6\%$ for the alkali halide its repulsion exponent was calibrated on, high by about $70\%$ for a divalent oxide, high by $26\%$ for bone mineral. Any affine estimate built on the same energy is now known to be an upper bound, by Proposition 2.1 — which is the same theorem that makes Module 2 §5's Voigt average an upper bound on a composite whose phase moduli are taken as given. And the five bond types are priced against §1's thermal ruler, which sorts them into the permanent and the reversible and tells the rest of the course which molecular events are available to a living tissue at all.

What remains missing is everything driven by entropy rather than energy. This section computed a bond's enthalpy and stopped; it could say nothing about collagen, nothing about why a reaction runs in one direction, and nothing about how fast. §3 supplies the free energy that decides direction, and §4 turns it into the force in a stretched chain — where it finds that the chain in question is elastin's and not collagen's, so the collagen rung stays measured and this section's fiftyfold miss has a different cause than the one named above.

3. Thermodynamics: free energy, chemical potential, and coupling

§1 stopped in the middle of a sentence and said so. It had split the free-energy change of a reaction into a composition-independent part and a concentration part, and then wrote: assembling it into $\Delta G = \Delta G^{\circ} + R_gT\ln Q$ — with the equilibrium condition that follows from setting $\Delta G$ to zero — is §3's headline result, not this section's. It borrowed a second thing on the same page: the inequality $W \le |\Delta g|$, which converted one ATP into a ceiling on how hard a crossbridge can pull, and which was labelled at the time as something §3 derives.

This section pays both debts and then spends what it has bought. The assembly and the equilibrium condition are Propositions 3.3 and 3.4; the work inequality is Proposition 3.1, and it comes first because it is the reason free energy is the interesting quantity in the first place. What the section spends them on is the question Module 5 cannot ask on its own: not merely what one ATP is worth, but why a muscle's own chemistry changes that worth as it tires, and why the pump that ends a contraction moves exactly two calcium ions per ATP and not three.

The two laws, in the one form this course needs

A tissue is not an isolated system and never operates at fixed volume. It sits at roughly constant temperature and constant pressure, exchanging heat freely with the rest of the body. That single fact selects which thermodynamic potential is worth carrying.

Definition 3.1 (enthalpy, entropy, and the Gibbs free energy). For a system of internal energy $U$, volume $V$, at pressure $p$ and absolute temperature $T$, the enthalpy is $H \equiv U + pV$ and the Gibbs free energy is $$G \;\equiv\; H - TS \;=\; U + pV - TS,$$ where $S$ is the entropy, the state function whose differential is $\mathrm dS = \delta q_{\text{rev}}/T$ for heat $\delta q_{\text{rev}}$ absorbed reversibly. All three are extensive state functions: their values depend only on the present state, not on the path that reached it, and they scale with the amount of material. Four letters must be nailed down before they collide. $H$ here is an enthalpy, not the aggregate modulus $H_A$ of Module 4 §3. $T$ is absolute temperature in kelvin, not the torque $T$ of Module 2 §4. $S$ is entropy, not a surface area. And $G$ is the Gibbs free energy, not the shear modulus $G$, exactly as §1 warned when it first used the letter.

At the fixed temperature of a working body the definition collapses to the form every biochemistry table is quoted in. Taking the change in $G$ between two states at the same $T$,

$$\Delta G \;=\; \Delta H - T\,\Delta S \qquad\text{(at constant } T\text{)}.$$

This is a rearranged definition, not a theorem, and it earns no proof. It earns attention instead, because it is the whole reason a reaction that absorbs heat can still run: $\Delta H$ may be positive provided $T\Delta S$ is larger. Melting is the everyday case; the hydrophobic effect of §7, which folds a protein by releasing ordered water rather than by forming bonds, is the case this course needs. Note also what the equation licenses numerically. If a table gives $\Delta H$ and $\Delta G$ at one temperature, it has implicitly given $\Delta S$. Moving that pair to another temperature then needs one approximation, stated here in the form it is actually used below: that the reaction's heat-capacity change $\Delta C_p$ is small enough to neglect, which is what makes both $\Delta H$ and $\Delta S$ effectively constant over the interval. Over twelve kelvin that is a mild assumption for a reaction like ATP hydrolysis; over a hundred it would not be. That is how a value measured at $25\ ^\circ\mathrm C$ is moved to body temperature, and it is done explicitly below rather than ignored.

Now the theorem. It is the one that makes $G$ worth defining, and it is the inequality §1 borrowed.

Proposition 3.1 (the maximum-work theorem). Consider a closed system whose initial and final states are both at the temperature $T$ and pressure $p$ of its surroundings, exchanging heat only with a reservoir at that temperature $T$ and doing expansion work only against the constant external pressure $p$. (Stating it this way rather than as "held at constant $T$ and $p$ throughout" matters, because an irreversible step passes through states with no well-defined single temperature; the endpoints are what the argument needs.) Let $W$ denote the non-expansion work the system delivers to its surroundings — work other than the $p\,\mathrm dV$ it spends pushing the atmosphere aside, so mechanical work against a load, electrical work, the pull of a crossbridge. Then $$\boxed{\;W \;\le\; -\Delta G\;}$$ with equality if and only if the process is carried out reversibly. Here $W$ is a work, not the body weight $W$ of Module 1 §3; where both appear in one calculation the weight keeps the symbol and the work is written $W_{\text{ne}}$.

Write the first law for an infinitesimal step as $\mathrm dU = \delta q + \delta w$, with $\delta q$ the heat absorbed by the system and $\delta w$ the total work done on it. The two notations are not decoration: $\mathrm d$ marks an exact differential, the increment of a state function, whose integral depends only on the endpoints; $\delta$ marks a path-dependent increment, and there is no function "$q$" whose change $\delta q$ is. Heat and work are things a process does, not properties a state has. Split that work into the expansion part and the rest: $\delta w = -p\,\mathrm dV + \delta w_{\text{ne}}$, where $\delta w_{\text{ne}}$ is the non-expansion work done on the system, so that the non-expansion work delivered by the system is $\delta W = -\delta w_{\text{ne}}$.

The second law, in the Clausius form appropriate to a system exchanging heat with a single reservoir at temperature $T$, is $\delta q \le T\,\mathrm dS$, with equality exactly when the exchange is reversible. Differentiating Definition 3.1 at constant $T$ and $p$ removes the $V\,\mathrm dp$ and $S\,\mathrm dT$ terms and leaves $\mathrm dG = \mathrm dU + p\,\mathrm dV - T\,\mathrm dS$. Substituting the first law,

$$\mathrm dG \;=\; \left(\delta q - p\,\mathrm dV + \delta w_{\text{ne}}\right) + p\,\mathrm dV - T\,\mathrm dS \;=\; \underbrace{\left(\delta q - T\,\mathrm dS\right)}_{\le\, 0} + \delta w_{\text{ne}} \;\le\; \delta w_{\text{ne}}.$$

So $\delta w_{\text{ne}} \ge \mathrm dG$, and therefore $\delta W = -\delta w_{\text{ne}} \le -\mathrm dG$. Integrating along the process at constant $T$ and $p$ gives $W \le -\Delta G$. Equality holds precisely when $\delta q = T\,\mathrm dS$ at every step, which is the definition of reversibility. $\blacksquare$

One corollary is used twice below and is worth stating separately.

Corollary 3.1.1 (the direction of spontaneous change). A process at constant $T$ and $p$ that delivers no non-expansion work satisfies $\Delta G \le 0$. Consequently a step with $\Delta G \lt 0$ is thermodynamically permitted to proceed forwards, a step with $\Delta G \gt 0$ is not, and $\Delta G = 0$ is equilibrium. Permitted is the whole claim: the corollary says nothing about rate, which is §6's subject.

Set $W = 0$ in Proposition 3.1, which gives $0 \le -\Delta G$, that is $\Delta G \le 0$. Reversing the sign of the advance reverses the sign of $\Delta G$, so the forward direction is the one with $\Delta G \lt 0$ and the reverse step, having $\Delta G \gt 0$, is excluded. Equality is the reversible case, in which neither direction is preferred. $\blacksquare$

Three further readings of Proposition 3.1 matter later. First, it is a bound, and a bound only: it says what a process cannot exceed, never what it achieves, and a real crossbridge running at finite speed is irreversible and falls short. Second, the bound is on non-expansion work specifically, which is why it applies cleanly to a motor protein pulling a filament in a nearly incompressible cell. Third, and this is what §1 used it for, a negative $\Delta G$ is a budget: $|\Delta G|$ is the largest amount of useful work the reaction can be made to yield, and any accounting that claims more has made an error somewhere else.

Composition: the chemical potential

The free energy of a beaker depends on what is in it. To reach a reaction, $G$ must be resolved into a contribution per species.

Definition 3.2 (chemical potential). For a system containing $N_i$ moles of species $i$ — an amount in moles, note, not the entity count that §1 Definition 1.1 writes $N$; the letter $n$ that §1 used for amount has since been taken by §2's Born exponent, so this section carries the amount on $N_i$ and says so — the chemical potential of that species is $$\mu_i \;\equiv\; \left(\frac{\partial G}{\partial N_i}\right)_{T,\,p,\,N_{j \ne i}},$$ the free energy added by one further mole of $i$ at fixed temperature, pressure and amounts of everything else. Its units are joules per mole. The symbol $\mu$ is the chemical potential throughout the chemistry sections; it is not the friction coefficient $\mu$ of Module 4 §7, which this course writes $\mu_{\text{fric}}$ wherever the two could meet, and it is not the Poisson ratio of §2, which is $\nu$.

§1 Lemma 1.1 already did the statistical work behind the next result. It counted the ways $M_{\text{tot}}$ molecules can be placed on a lattice of sites, and found that diluting a species from $c^{\circ}$ to $c$ changes its free energy per molecule by $k_BT\ln(c/c^{\circ})$. Converting that to a molar quantity is one multiplication, and it is worth stating separately only because everything downstream is built on it.

Proposition 3.2 (the composition term). For a species $i$ in a dilute solution at concentration $c_i$, $$\mu_i \;=\; \mu_i^{\circ} + R_gT\,\ln\!\frac{c_i}{c^{\circ}},$$ where $\mu_i^{\circ}$ is the standard chemical potential, the value of $\mu_i$ when $c_i$ equals the standard concentration $c^{\circ} = 1\ \mathrm M$ fixed in §1 Definition 1.2, and $R_g = 8.314\ \mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}K^{-1}}$ is the gas constant, written with its subscript throughout this course to keep it clear of a joint reaction $R$.

The conversion is the chain rule, not an addition. Amount in moles and molecule count are related by $N_i = N_i^{\text{molec}}/N_A$, so $$\mu_i \;=\; \frac{\partial G}{\partial N_i} \;=\; N_A\,\frac{\partial G}{\partial N_i^{\text{molec}}} \;=\; N_A\,\mu_i^{\text{molec}},$$ an identity between derivatives that holds for any $G$ whatever, dilute or not. It is emphatically not the claim that a mole's worth of free energy is a sum of $N_A$ separate single-molecule contributions; that claim is false for identical particles, and §1 Lemma 1.1 is the statement of exactly how it fails.

§1 Lemma 1.1 supplies the per-molecule form $\mu_i^{\text{molec}} = \mu_i^{\circ,\text{molec}} + k_BT\ln(c_i/c^{\circ})$, obtained there by counting lattice configurations. Multiplying by $N_A$ and using $N_Ak_B = R_g$ — an exact relation, since the gas constant is defined as that product — carries it over term by term, the logarithm untouched because it is dimensionless. The dilute hypothesis is inherited from Lemma 1.1's own hypotheses (non-interacting solute, occupancy small against the number of sites) and is not an extra assumption introduced here. It is what fails in the crowded cytosol at high occupancy, where an activity replaces the concentration. $\blacksquare$

One definition remains before the assembly.

Definition 3.3 (reaction quotient and standard free-energy change). Write a reaction with stoichiometric coefficients $\nu_i$, negative for reactants and positive for products, so that hydrolysis of ATP has $\nu = -1$ for ATP and $+1$ for each of ADP and phosphate. The reaction quotient is the dimensionless product $$Q \;\equiv\; \prod_i \left(\frac{c_i}{c^{\circ}}\right)^{\nu_i},$$ evaluated at whatever composition the system currently holds, and the standard free-energy change is $\Delta G^{\circ} \equiv \sum_i \nu_i\,\mu_i^{\circ}$. Dividing every concentration by $c^{\circ}$ is what makes $Q$ a pure number, so that its logarithm is defined; a $Q$ quoted without a stated $c^{\circ}$ is not a number at all. The solvent is omitted from $Q$: water is present at $55.5\ \mathrm M$ in §1's ladder and its concentration does not measurably change, so it is folded into $\Delta G^{\circ}$ by convention.

The assembly §1 promised now takes three lines.

Proposition 3.3 (the free energy at any composition). For a reaction among dilute species at temperature $T$, $$\boxed{\;\Delta G \;=\; \Delta G^{\circ} + R_gT\,\ln Q\;}$$ with $Q$ and $\Delta G^{\circ}$ as in Definition 3.3. The standard term carries every chemical fact about the reaction; the logarithm carries everything about the composition, and nothing else appears.

The free-energy change per mole of reaction advance, at fixed composition, is the stoichiometric sum of the chemical potentials, $\Delta G = \sum_i \nu_i\mu_i$ — this is the definition of a molar reaction quantity, and §1 Proposition 1.1 is the statement that it is legitimate to read it as an energy per $N_A$ single-molecule events. Substituting Proposition 3.2 into it,

$$\Delta G \;=\; \sum_i \nu_i\left(\mu_i^{\circ} + R_gT\ln\frac{c_i}{c^{\circ}}\right) \;=\; \underbrace{\sum_i \nu_i\mu_i^{\circ}}_{\Delta G^{\circ}} \;+\; R_gT\sum_i \nu_i\ln\frac{c_i}{c^{\circ}}.$$

The remaining sum is a sum of logarithms with coefficients, which is the logarithm of a product of powers: $\sum_i \nu_i\ln(c_i/c^{\circ}) = \ln\prod_i (c_i/c^{\circ})^{\nu_i} = \ln Q$. $\blacksquare$

Equilibrium is where the line crosses zero

Proposition 3.3 makes $\Delta G$ an affine function of $\ln Q$ with slope $R_gT$ — a straight line, drawn in Fig. 9. A straight line of nonzero slope crosses zero exactly once, and that single crossing is the whole content of chemical equilibrium.

Proposition 3.4 (the equilibrium constant). A reaction at fixed $T$ and $p$ has exactly one value of the reaction quotient at which $\Delta G = 0$ — not one composition, since infinitely many compositions share a given $Q$. Writing that value $K_{\text{eq}}$, $$\boxed{\;\Delta G^{\circ} \;=\; -R_gT\,\ln K_{\text{eq}}\;}, \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad \Delta G = R_gT\ln\frac{Q}{K_{\text{eq}}}.$$ The reaction runs forwards when $Q \lt K_{\text{eq}}$ and backwards when $Q \gt K_{\text{eq}}$. The subscript is not decoration: $K$ alone is the bulk modulus in §2 Proposition 2.2, and the equilibrium constant is $K_{\text{eq}}$ everywhere in this course.

By Proposition 3.3, $\Delta G(\ln Q) = \Delta G^{\circ} + R_gT\ln Q$ is affine in $\ln Q$ with slope $R_gT \gt 0$, hence strictly increasing and therefore injective; it takes the value zero for exactly one $\ln Q$, namely $\ln Q = -\Delta G^{\circ}/(R_gT)$. Naming $K_{\text{eq}}$ as the $Q$ at that point and exponentiating gives the boxed relation. Substituting $\Delta G^{\circ} = -R_gT\ln K_{\text{eq}}$ back into Proposition 3.3 gives $\Delta G = R_gT(\ln Q - \ln K_{\text{eq}}) = R_gT\ln(Q/K_{\text{eq}})$, whose sign is the sign of $\ln(Q/K_{\text{eq}})$: negative for $Q \lt K_{\text{eq}}$, positive for $Q \gt K_{\text{eq}}$. Uniqueness is a statement about $Q$, not about the individual concentrations; infinitely many compositions share one $Q$, and all of them are equilibria. $\blacksquare$

Two things about this deserve emphasis because they are routinely garbled. $K_{\text{eq}}$ is fixed by $\Delta G^{\circ}$ and temperature alone — it knows nothing about how much material is present. And $\Delta G^{\circ}$, despite its name and its ubiquity in tables, is not the free-energy change of any reaction a cell ever performs. It is the value at one arbitrary reference composition, chosen so that tables can be published. The quantity that decides direction is $\Delta G$, and the gap between the two is the entire $R_gT\ln Q$ term.

How far from equilibrium is it? With the standard value derived below, $K_{\text{eq}} = 1.60\times10^{5}$ at $310.15\ \mathrm K$. If a resting muscle ever reached that balance while holding ADP and phosphate at their measured resting levels, its ATP concentration would settle at $1.6\times10^{-13}\ \mathrm M$ — about $3.4\times10^{10}$ times below the $5.5\ \mathrm{mM}$ it actually holds. Put that through §1's spacing formula, $1.184\ \mathrm{nm}\,(c/\mathrm M)^{-1/3}$, and it is one ATP molecule per cube $22\ \mu\mathrm m$ on a side: about $10\ \mathrm{pL}$, the volume of an ordinary mammalian cell. A living cell is not near chemical equilibrium in any respect; equilibrium is death, and the distance from it is what the mitochondria are for.

The biochemical standard state, and moving a table to body temperature

One complication has to be faced squarely, because skipping it is the commonest quiet error in applied bioenergetics. Definition 3.3 puts every species in $Q$, and ATP hydrolysis releases a proton. At $\mathrm{pH}\ 7$ the hydrogen-ion concentration is $10^{-7}\ \mathrm M$, so the factor $(c_{\mathrm H^+}/c^{\circ})$ contributes $R_gT\ln 10^{-7} = -41.5\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ — a term larger than the reaction's own standard free energy. Worse, the reaction is really a family of reactions, because ATP exists as a mixture of $\mathrm{ATP^{4-}}$, $\mathrm{HATP^{3-}}$ and $\mathrm{MgATP^{2-}}$ whose proportions shift with pH and magnesium.

Definition 3.4 (the transformed standard state). Biochemistry therefore quotes a transformed standard free energy $\Delta G^{\circ\prime}$, defined at $\mathrm{pH}\ 7$ with free $\mathrm{Mg^{2+}}$ held at $1\ \mathrm{mM}$ — equivalently $p\mathrm{Mg} = 3$, where $p\mathrm{Mg} \equiv -\log_{10}([\mathrm{Mg^{2+}}]/c^{\circ})$ on the model of pH — and ionic strength $I = 0.25\ \mathrm M$, a charge-weighted total ion concentration $I = \tfrac12\sum_i c_iz_i^2$ whose role in screening the interactions between charged species is §5's subject. In this convention each reactant is the sum of its protonation and magnesium-bound states. Because pH and $p\mathrm{Mg}$ are held fixed by definition, $\mathrm H^+$ and $\mathrm{Mg^{2+}}$ do not appear in the reaction quotient; the quotient for ATP hydrolysis is $Q = ([\mathrm{ADP}][\mathrm{P_i}]/c^{\circ})/[\mathrm{ATP}]$ and nothing more. Everywhere below, a primed standard quantity is the transformed one and an unprimed one is the $c^{\circ} = 1\ \mathrm M$ convention of Definition 3.3; the two differ by exactly the terms that were absorbed, and mixing them is an error of tens of kilojoules per mole. Propositions 3.3 and 3.4 hold in the transformed convention, with $\Delta G^{\circ}$ replaced by $\Delta G^{\circ\prime}$ and $Q$ by the transformed quotient. That carry-over is a borrowed theorem, not an obvious relabelling, and it is stated here rather than proved: it is Alberty's Legendre-transform result (R. A. Alberty, Thermodynamics of Biochemical Reactions, Wiley, 2003), which defines a transformed potential $G' = G - N_{\mathrm H}\mu_{\mathrm H^+} - N_{\mathrm{Mg}}\mu_{\mathrm{Mg^{2+}}}$, where $N_{\mathrm H}$ and $N_{\mathrm{Mg}}$ count bound hydrogen and magnesium. Differentiating $G'$ along the extent of reaction gives the same stoichiometric sum as before, now taken over pseudoisomer groups — each reactant treated as one species whose chemical potential is the equilibrium average over its protonation and magnesium-bound forms. Because that derivative has the same form, Propositions 3.3 and 3.4 apply to it unchanged. Everything numerical below rests on this, so it is flagged as an import rather than buried.

One notational economy, declared once. From here to the end of the section every reaction quotient and equilibrium constant is the transformed one, so the prime is dropped from $Q$ and $K_{\text{eq}}$ to keep the expressions readable; it is kept on $\Delta G^{\circ\prime}$, where confusing the two conventions would cost tens of kilojoules per mole. Where a later module mixes transformed and untransformed quantities it must restore the prime on all three.

The tables give $\Delta G^{\circ\prime} = -30.5\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ and $\Delta H^{\circ\prime} = -20.5\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ for ATP hydrolysis — at $298.15\ \mathrm K$, which is not body temperature. Moving them is the first use of the boxed $\Delta G = \Delta H - T\Delta S$. From the two tabulated values, $\Delta S^{\circ\prime} = (\Delta H^{\circ\prime} - \Delta G^{\circ\prime})/T = 33.5\ \mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}K^{-1}}$, and treating $\Delta H^{\circ\prime}$ and $\Delta S^{\circ\prime}$ as constant across the twelve-kelvin interval,

$$\Delta G^{\circ\prime}(310.15\ \mathrm K) \;=\; \Delta H^{\circ\prime} - T\,\Delta S^{\circ\prime} \;=\; -20.5 - 310.15\,(0.0335) \;=\; -30.90\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}.$$

measured, not derived: $\Delta G^{\circ\prime}$ for ATP hydrolysis is a thermochemical measurement, not something this course computes. It is worth naming precisely what kind, and precisely at what temperature. This course uses $\Delta G^{\circ\prime} = -30.5\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ and $\Delta H^{\circ\prime} = -20.5\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$, both at $298.15\ \mathrm K$, $\mathrm{pH}\ 7$, $1\ \mathrm{mM}$ free $\mathrm{Mg^{2+}}$ and $I = 0.25\ \mathrm M$ — the widely tabulated textbook pair. Neither is a single calorimetric reading of one reaction. Both are outputs of a model fitted to measured equilibrium constants for the hydrolysis combined with measured acid-dissociation and magnesium-binding constants for every species, then transformed to the chosen reference conditions by the Legendre construction of Definition 3.4. Different sources quote $-28$ to $-32.5\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$, and the spread is a difference of reference conditions and of which binding constants were used, not of chemistry. The stated temperature matters here more than the value: some compilations report the pair already at $310$ or $311\ \mathrm K$, and applying the twelve-kelvin transport below to such a figure would count the same correction twice. It is worth only $0.40\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$, so the arithmetic would survive; the provenance story would not. The lesson §2 learned about Pauling's exponent applies here too: a number that arrives as a table entry may have been fitted, and the fit's conditions are part of the number.

The shift from $25\ ^\circ\mathrm C$ to $37\ ^\circ\mathrm C$ is only $-0.40\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$, about one percent, and it is tempting to skip it. It is kept because the habit is what matters: the same manoeuvre applied to a reaction with a large $|\Delta S|$ would move the answer substantially, and §6 will need exactly this reasoning in its Arrhenius argument and its $Q_{10}$ — the factor by which a rate changes for a ten-kelvin change in temperature — where a twelve-kelvin interval is the entire subject rather than a rounding error.

The honest statement is that the thermodynamic ceiling on one crossbridge falls from about $10.4$ to about $8.8\ \mathrm{pN}$ as a muscle fatigues, a drop of $1.62\ \mathrm{pN}$, or $16\%$.

Two things must be said about what that does and does not explain. It is not the cause of fatigue, and the reason is quantitative rather than rhetorical: §1 puts the measured force of one myosin head at $3$ to $6\ \mathrm{pN}$, which lies below both ceilings, so the thermodynamic limit never binds and a muscle stops long before it runs into one. The measured force loss in fatigue is far larger than $16\%$, and its mechanism is phosphate acting on the crossbridge cycle itself — shifting the balance of attached states rather than exhausting a budget — which is Module 5's business. What the ceiling does give is an audit: a model that lets a crossbridge average more than about $8.8\ \mathrm{pN}$ over a $10\ \mathrm{nm}$ stroke in a phosphate-loaded cell is claiming more work than the reaction can pay for, and the error is in its bookkeeping.

Coupling: how an uphill reaction runs

Every interesting thing a cell does is thermodynamically uphill. It concentrates ions against their gradients, builds polymers from monomers, and holds a membrane potential. Proposition 3.1 says such a process cannot happen on its own. What makes it happen is coupling, and coupling is a consequence of $G$ being a state function.

Proposition 3.5 (coupled reactions). Suppose two processes are made to occur together, in fixed proportion, through a shared chemical intermediate — so that one advance of the composite consists of one advance of process 1 and $m$ advances of process 2. Then, at fixed $T$ and $p$, $$\Delta G_{\text{net}} \;=\; \Delta G_1 + m\,\Delta G_2,$$ and the composite is thermodynamically permitted to run forwards if and only if $\Delta G_{\text{net}} \lt 0$. In particular process 2 may have $\Delta G_2 \gt 0$, provided $|\Delta G_1| \gt m\,\Delta G_2$. The hypothesis that carries the weight is the shared intermediate: the two processes must proceed through a common chemical state, so that the composite is a single path in state space. Two reactions merely occurring in the same volume are not coupled, and the favourable one does not drag the unfavourable one anywhere.

Introduce an extent of reaction $\xi_j$ for each process — the number of moles of advance, so that species $i$ changes by $\mathrm dN_i = \sum_j \nu_{ij}\,\mathrm d\xi_j$. Free energy is a state function of composition, so $\mathrm dG = \sum_i \mu_i\,\mathrm dN_i = \sum_j \Delta G_j\,\mathrm d\xi_j$ with $\Delta G_j \equiv \sum_i \nu_{ij}\mu_i$. That much is true whether or not the processes are coupled, and it is only linearity.

What coupling supplies is a constraint. A shared chemical intermediate means neither process can advance without the other: the mechanism forces $\xi_2 = m\,\xi_1$, so the two independent extents collapse to one coordinate $\xi \equiv \xi_1$. Substituting,

$$\frac{\mathrm dG}{\mathrm d\xi} \;=\; \Delta G_1 + m\,\Delta G_2 \;\equiv\; \Delta G_{\text{net}},$$

and by the Corollary to Proposition 3.1 a forward step is permitted exactly when this is negative. Note where the hypothesis does its work. Without the constraint, $\xi_1$ and $\xi_2$ vary independently and $\mathrm dG \le 0$ must hold for each separately, so an uphill process 2 simply does not advance however favourable process 1 is; the sum rule still holds arithmetically and buys nothing. Coupling does not change the thermodynamics of either half. It removes the freedom for the unfavourable half to stand still. $\blacksquare$

Note what the proposition does not say. It gives a necessary and sufficient condition for the composite to be thermodynamically allowed, and says nothing whatever about whether it is fast. A reaction with $\Delta G_{\text{net}} = -15\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ may take a microsecond or a century; the barrier between the endpoints is invisible to a state function, and §6 is where rate enters. Thermodynamics permits; kinetics disposes.

The calcium pump, and why the stoichiometry is two

Here is coupling doing real anatomical work. When a muscle relaxes, calcium must be cleared from the cytosol and returned to the sarcoplasmic reticulum — the membrane-bound internal store, wrapped around each myofibril (one contractile strand of a muscle fibre, a chain of the sarcomeres Module 5 §2 describes), that releases calcium to start a contraction and reabsorbs it to end one. The pump that does this is SERCA, the sarco-endoplasmic reticulum calcium ATPase, and it is the single largest consumer of ATP in a resting muscle.

The climb is steep. §1's concentration ladder supplies the cytosolic end, resting free calcium at $0.1\ \mu\mathrm M$; the luminal end is a new measured input, free calcium inside the reticulum at about $1\ \mathrm{mM}$. measured, not derived: free calcium inside the sarcoplasmic reticulum is read with low-affinity fluorescent indicators targeted to the lumen, and reported values spread from $0.3$ to $1.5\ \mathrm{mM}$ across preparations and species — a factor of five in the ratio, which spans $8.30\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ in the doubled climb and spans it asymmetrically about the value used here: $41.29$ at $0.3\ \mathrm{mM}$, $47.50$ at $1\ \mathrm{mM}$, $49.59$ at $1.5\ \mathrm{mM}$. The cytosolic $0.1\ \mu\mathrm M$ is the better-determined of the two. Where a conclusion below depends on the spread, it says so. Together they give a ratio of $10^{4}$. Moving one mole of calcium up that gradient is a process whose free-energy change is given by Proposition 3.3 with $\Delta G^{\circ} = 0$ — there is no chemical change, only a change of location and therefore of concentration — so

$$\Delta G_{\text{climb}} \;=\; R_gT\,\ln\!\frac{[\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}]_{\text{SR}}}{[\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}]_{\text{cyt}}} \;=\; 2.58\,\ln(10^{4}) \;=\; +23.75\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}.$$

Two conditions make that formula legitimate here, and both must be checked rather than assumed. First, calcium carries charge, so in general the climb costs electrical work as well, and the transported species would need the electrochemical potential that §5 builds. It is omitted because the reticulum membrane carries potassium and chloride channels that let counter-ions follow, holding its transmembrane potential near zero; that is a property of this particular membrane and would be false at the cell surface. Second, most calcium inside the reticulum is not free but bound to calsequestrin, a low-affinity, high-capacity binding protein, so the total luminal calcium is some $20\ \mathrm{mM}$ while the free concentration stays near $1\ \mathrm{mM}$. The pump does work against the free concentration, which is the one that enters a chemical potential; calsequestrin's function is precisely to store a large quantity at a small thermodynamic cost.

Now put the ledger together. With an observed stoichiometry of two calcium ions per ATP, Proposition 3.5 gives the following. One assumption is built into the second column and should be visible: the calcium gradient is held at $10^{4}$ in both states, so the climb costs the same $47.5\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ in each. That is conservative for the argument but not exactly true — rising phosphate precipitates calcium inside the reticulum and lowers the free luminal concentration, which would reduce the climb and slightly widen the fatigued margin.

The measured stoichiometry of SERCA is two, and it is worth being exact about what has just been shown. Thermodynamics supplies an upper bound: at most two calcium ions per ATP. It does not select two over one, because one ion per ATP is permitted too and merely wastes half the budget. Why evolution took the bound rather than something safely under it is an efficiency argument, not this one. Still, a ceiling on a biological stoichiometry, computed from five measured concentrations and one tabulated standard free energy with no fitting, is the most valuable thing this section does.

The same ledger, run backwards, gives the steepest gradient the pump could hold if it ever reached equilibrium. Setting $\Delta G_{\text{net}} = 0$ in Proposition 3.5 with $m = 2$ gives a maximum concentration ratio of $\exp\!\left(|\Delta G_1|/2R_gT\right)$, which is $1.8\times10^{5}$ at rest and $2.8\times10^{4}$ when fatigued. The resting ceiling is eighteen times the gradient the muscle actually maintains, so at rest the pump has ample thermodynamic headroom and its speed is set by kinetics. By the end of a hard bout the ceiling has fallen to less than three times the working gradient. The pump is then close enough to its thermodynamic stall point that the driving force for reuptake is materially reduced, and slowed reuptake is a known feature of fatigued muscle: relaxation takes longer. This is one contributor and not the whole account — most of the measured slowing is kinetic, from phosphate and ADP inhibiting the pump directly and from calcium-phosphate precipitation inside the reticulum lowering the free luminal concentration. Module 5 describes the slowing; this section supplies the thermodynamic part of its cause.

What this section licenses

Four things are now available to the rest of the course. Direction is decidable: any reaction whose standard free energy and composition are known has a computable $\Delta G$, and its sign settles which way it runs. Work is bounded: Proposition 3.1 converts a free energy into a hard ceiling on force or displacement, which is how §1's crossbridge bound was licensed and how any later claim about a molecular motor must be checked. Uphill processes are explicable: Proposition 3.5 shows how, and imposes the arithmetic that decides whether a proposed stoichiometry is possible. And a table value can be transported: $\Delta G = \Delta H - T\Delta S$ moves a standard quantity from the temperature it was measured at to the temperature the body works at.

What remains missing is again everything about rate, and now also everything about force in a soft material. This section can say that a stretched ligament stores free energy, but not why: §4 shows which tissue proteins carry $-T\,\partial S/\partial x$ — elastin does, collagen does not — and licenses Module 6 §1's assumption that a straightened fibril is linear, which is the assumption its crimp-and-recruitment derivation of the toe rests on. §5 adds the electrical work this section was able to drop at the reticulum membrane and cannot drop at the cell surface. And §6 supplies the barrier heights that decide whether an allowed reaction is a millisecond event or a geological one.

4. Entropic elasticity: the chain, and what it can and cannot stiffen

§2 ended by handing this section a bill. It had run the arrow forwards for bone mineral and reached $143\ \mathrm{GPa}$ against a measured $114$; it had then tried the same calculation on collagen, overshot fiftyfold, and explained the miss by saying that a fibril's compliance "is entropic, not a bond length being stretched". §0 says the same thing in its provenance marker. The factor of fifty, both sections agreed, was §4's to compute.

This section builds the machinery they were pointing at, and then does something neither of them expected: it shows that the bill cannot be paid as written, because the diagnosis was wrong. Entropic elasticity is real, it is quantitative, and it predicts a tissue protein's modulus forwards to within a factor of $1.4$ with no elastic datum among the inputs. But the protein is not collagen. Applied to a collagen fibril the same law falls short by a factor of $642$ at a real protein's crosslink spacing, and the flexible-chain network it would need in order to close that gap cannot exist. Correcting that is the most useful thing §4 does, and it is the third section in a row in which the previous section's confident attribution did not survive being computed.

Where the force in a stretched chain comes from

Everything here rests on one distinction: a spring can pull because stretching it raises the energy, or because stretching it lowers the entropy. A steel wire is the first kind. A rubber band is the second, and so is a protein chain.

Definition 4.1 (a polymer chain and its lengths). Model a long flexible molecule as a chain of $N$ rigid Kuhn segments, each of length $b$, joined so that successive segments point in directions that are statistically independent. Its contour length is $L_c = Nb$, the length of the molecule pulled perfectly straight. Its end-to-end vector $\mathbf R$ joins the two ends, and $x \equiv R = |\mathbf R|$ is the extension — a length, not the joint reaction $R$ that §0 and Module 3 §4 reserve the letter for. An ideal chain is one in which no segment interacts with any other except through the joints, so the internal energy $U$ does not depend on $\mathbf R$ at all. Four letters need pinning down. $N$ is the number of Kuhn segments in one chain, not the entity count $N$ of §1 Definition 1.1 nor the amount in moles $N_i$ of §3 Definition 3.2; $b$ is the Kuhn length, not §2's Born repulsion constant $B$; $x$ is an extension in metres, not a mole fraction; and $f$ is the tension in one chain, reserved lower case against the $F$ that is a whole-body force in Module 1.

The thermodynamics is §3's, run at fixed volume rather than fixed pressure, which is why the potential is Helmholtz rather than Gibbs.

Proposition 4.1 (the two sources of tension). For a chain held at temperature $T$ and extension $x$, the tension is $$f \;=\; \left(\frac{\partial A}{\partial x}\right)_T \;=\; \underbrace{\left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial x}\right)_T}_{\text{energetic}} \;-\; T\underbrace{\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial x}\right)_T}_{\text{entropic}},$$ where $A = U - TS$ is the Helmholtz free energy of §1 Lemma 1.1. For an ideal chain the first term vanishes identically and $$\boxed{\;f \;=\; -\,T\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial x}\right)_T\;}$$ so the entire tension is the price of the configurations that straightening destroys. A chain of this kind pulls harder when it is warmer. The proportionality to $T$ needs one condition, which the freely jointed chain of the next part satisfies and a worm-like chain does not: the entropy must be purely configurational, with no temperature of its own. A worm-like chain fails it, because its persistence length is $\ell_p = \kappa/k_BT$ by Definition 4.3, so at fixed bending rigidity $\kappa$ the Marko–Siggia force carries an extra factor of $T$. The discriminator built at the end of this section is therefore stated for a flexible-chain network, which is the case it is applied to.

Work done reversibly on the chain at constant $T$ and $V$ raises its Helmholtz free energy by exactly that work — this is the fixed-volume counterpart of §3 Proposition 3.1, and follows the same way: $\mathrm dA = \mathrm dU - T\,\mathrm dS$ at constant $T$, with $\mathrm dU = \delta q + f\,\mathrm dx$ and $\delta q \le T\,\mathrm dS$, so $\mathrm dA \le f\,\mathrm dx$ with equality when reversible. Hence $f = (\partial A/\partial x)_T$. Substituting $A = U - TS$ and differentiating at constant $T$ splits it into the two displayed terms.

For an ideal chain $U$ is independent of $\mathbf R$ by Definition 4.1 — there is no bond being stretched and no pair of segments whose separation has changed in energy, because non-adjacent segments do not interact. So $(\partial U/\partial x)_T = 0$ and only the entropic term survives. Note what this does not assume: it does not require the chain to be Gaussian, only ideal. The Gaussian form comes next and is a separate step. $\blacksquare$

The proportionality to $T$ in the boxed result is not a curiosity. It is the section's experimental signature, and the last part builds a measurement around it.

The freely jointed chain, and how soft one molecule really is

Definition 4.2 (the freely jointed chain). The freely jointed chain is the ideal chain of Definition 4.1 in which each segment direction is drawn uniformly and independently from the unit sphere. Its end-to-end vector is then the sum of $N$ independent random vectors of fixed length $b$.
Lemma 4.1 (the random-walk statistics). For a freely jointed chain, $\langle\mathbf R\rangle = \mathbf 0$ and $\langle R^2\rangle = Nb^2$, so the root-mean-square end-to-end distance is $\sqrt N\,b$ — shorter than the contour length $Nb$ by a factor $\sqrt N$. For $N \gg 1$ the distribution of $\mathbf R$ approaches a Gaussian, $$P(\mathbf R) \;\propto\; \exp\!\left(-\frac{3R^2}{2Nb^2}\right).$$

Write $\mathbf R = \sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf b_i$ with the $\mathbf b_i$ independent, $|\mathbf b_i| = b$, and $\langle\mathbf b_i\rangle = \mathbf 0$ by the uniform choice of direction. Then $\langle\mathbf R\rangle = \mathbf 0$ term by term, and $$\langle R^2\rangle = \Big\langle \sum_i\sum_j \mathbf b_i\!\cdot\!\mathbf b_j \Big\rangle = \sum_i \langle b^2\rangle + \sum_{i \ne j} \langle\mathbf b_i\rangle\!\cdot\!\langle\mathbf b_j\rangle = Nb^2,$$ the cross terms vanishing because independence lets the average factorise and each factor is zero. The Gaussian form is the central limit theorem applied to a sum of $N$ independent identically distributed vectors, with the variance per Cartesian component equal to $\langle R^2\rangle/3 = Nb^2/3$; the exponent $-3R^2/(2Nb^2)$ is that variance written for all three components at once. The approximation is good in the region $R \ll L_c$ where the constraint $R \le L_c$ is not felt, and it fails near full extension, which is exactly where the next model is needed. $\blacksquare$

Proposition 4.2 (an ideal chain is a Hookean spring). A freely jointed chain in its Gaussian regime has $$S(x) \;=\; S_0 - \frac{3k_Bx^2}{2Nb^2}, \qquad\text{hence}\qquad \boxed{\;f \;=\; \frac{3k_BT}{Nb^2}\,x \;\equiv\; k_{\text{chain}}\,x\;}$$ with chain stiffness $k_{\text{chain}} = 3k_BT/(Nb^2) = 3k_BT/(L_cb)$. The chain is linear in extension, and its stiffness rises with temperature.

By Boltzmann's definition the entropy of the constrained chain is $S = k_B\ln\Omega$, with $\Omega$ the number of configurations consistent with the constraint. It matters exactly what is held. Fix the end-to-end vector $\mathbf R$ — which is what an experiment does, holding both ends at stated positions — and $\Omega(\mathbf R) \propto P(\mathbf R)$ from Lemma 4.1, so $S(\mathbf R) = S_0 - 3k_BR^2/(2Nb^2)$ with the constant absorbing every factor independent of $\mathbf R$. Differentiating along the direction of $\mathbf R$, with $x = |\mathbf R|$, gives the result below.

Had the magnitude alone been fixed, leaving the direction free, the count would carry the spherical-shell Jacobian, $\Omega(x) \propto 4\pi x^2P(\mathbf R)$, and the entropy an extra $2k_B\ln x$ — contributing a spurious inward term $-2k_BT/x$ to the tension, which diverges at $x \to 0$ and is an artefact of letting the chain choose its direction rather than a force any apparatus measures. The distinction is easy to lose and changes the small-$x$ behaviour completely. Differentiating and inserting into the boxed result of Proposition 4.1, $$f = -T\frac{\partial S}{\partial x} = -T\left(-\frac{3k_Bx}{Nb^2}\right) = \frac{3k_BT}{Nb^2}x.$$ Using $L_c = Nb$ rewrites the stiffness as $3k_BT/(L_cb)$. $\blacksquare$

One hypothesis needs checking before a real molecule goes in. Lemma 4.1's Gaussian form asks for $N \gg 1$, and tropocollagen will turn out to have $N = 10.3$, which is not large. The stiffness survives anyway, and it is worth seeing why: Proposition 4.2's $3k_BT/(Nb^2)$ needs only the second moment $\langle R^2\rangle = Nb^2$, which Lemma 4.1 proves exactly for every $N$, with no appeal to the central limit theorem. The same expression is also the small-force limit of the exact Langevin law quoted below. What $N = 10.3$ does compromise is the shape of the distribution away from small extension, which is why the next part replaces it rather than trusting it there.

Now put a real molecule into it, and the reason §2's diagnosis fails becomes visible before any network is assembled. Tropocollagen — the single triple-helical collagen molecule, about $300\ \mathrm{nm}$ long and $1.5\ \mathrm{nm}$ across, the rod that Module 6 §1 shows staggering into a fibril — has a contour length $L_c = 300\ \mathrm{nm}$ and a persistence length $\ell_p = 14.5\ \mathrm{nm}$, the distance over which the molecule forgets which way it was pointing (Definition 4.3 below makes that precise). A worm-like chain of persistence length $\ell_p$ behaves as a freely jointed chain of Kuhn length $b = 2\ell_p = 29\ \mathrm{nm}$, so $N = L_c/b = 10.3$ segments. Its free-coil size is $\sqrt N\,b = 93\ \mathrm{nm}$, less than a third of its straight length. And its entropic spring constant is

$$k_{\text{chain}} \;=\; \frac{3k_BT}{Nb^2} \;=\; 1.48\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}.$$

measured, not derived: the persistence length of tropocollagen is obtained by fitting a worm-like-chain force–extension curve to single-molecule optical-trap or atomic-force data, so it is a fitted parameter of the very model used here, not an independent measurement. Reported values span roughly $10$ to $180\ \mathrm{nm}$ depending on preparation, ionic conditions and which part of the curve is fitted; this course uses $14.5\ \mathrm{nm}$. The spread does not threaten anything below, and for a reason worth stating rather than waving at: the network modulus of Proposition 4.3, $E = 3nk_BT$, does not contain $\ell_p$ at all. It depends on the number of strands per unit volume and on temperature, and on nothing else. The persistence length enters only the single-chain force curves of Fig. 12, where a different choice would move the curves but not the modulus. So no value of $\ell_p$ in that range rescues the entropic hypothesis for collagen, which fails below by $642\times$ at a real crosslink spacing.

Set that against §2 Lemma 2.1, which found a single $\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}\!-\!\mathrm{O^{2-}}$ bond to have $k_{\text{pair}} = 467\ \mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}$: the entropic spring is softer by a factor of $3.2\times10^{8}$.

Comparing two spring constants is not yet an argument, because a $300\ \mathrm{nm}$ spring and a $0.24\ \mathrm{nm}$ spring are not interchangeable parts; the comparison has to be made per unit volume. Do that. Pack tropocollagen at a fibril's lateral spacing, one molecule per $(1.5\ \mathrm{nm})^2$ of cross-section, and the strand density is $n = 1.5\times10^{24}\ \mathrm{m^{-3}}$, so Proposition 4.3 below returns $E = 3nk_BT = 19\ \mathrm{kPa}$. That is not within a factor of fifty of a fibril's gigapascal; it is $4.8$ decades under it. The estimate that follows only sharpens a conclusion this arithmetic already forces.

The worm-like chain, for a molecule that is stiff over its own length

The freely jointed chain treats the molecule as floppy on the scale of $b$ and says nothing about what happens between joints. A real protein bends smoothly, and the length over which it forgets its direction is the quantity to name.

Definition 4.3 (persistence length). The persistence length $\ell_p$ of a semiflexible filament is the decay length of the correlation between its tangent directions: $\langle \hat{\mathbf t}(s)\cdot\hat{\mathbf t}(0)\rangle = e^{-s/\ell_p}$ for arc length $s$. It is set by the filament's bending rigidity $\kappa$ through $\ell_p = \kappa/k_BT$, which is why it shortens as temperature rises. A filament with $\ell_p \gg L_c$ is effectively a rigid rod; one with $\ell_p \ll L_c$ is a Gaussian coil with Kuhn length $b = 2\ell_p$. Tropocollagen, at $\ell_p/L_c = 0.048$, is a coil — floppy over its full length, stiff over a twentieth of it.

The exact force–extension law of the worm-like chain has no closed form. Two results fill the gap. The freely jointed chain does have one, the Langevin function: $x/L_c = \coth\beta - 1/\beta$ with $\beta = fb/k_BT$, of which Proposition 4.2 is the small-$\beta$ limit. For the worm-like chain the standard tool is an interpolation, and it is labelled as one here because it is not derived:

$$f \;=\; \frac{k_BT}{\ell_p}\left[\frac{x}{L_c} + \frac{1}{4\left(1 - x/L_c\right)^2} - \frac14\right].$$

This is the Marko–Siggia formula. It is constructed to reproduce the two limits that are known exactly — the Gaussian result at small extension and the correct divergence as $x \to L_c$ — and it interpolates between them with an error reaching about $10\%$ at intermediate extension (Bouchiat and co-workers, 1999, who give a seven-term correction that removes it). It earns its box as a working tool, not as a theorem, and no proof follows because it is an interpolation rather than a theorem; that is a different status from every other boxed result in this module, and the reader should hold it differently.

From one chain to a modulus

A single chain is not a material. Crosslink many of them and the network has a modulus, and the step from one to the other is where the prediction becomes testable.

Proposition 4.3 (the rubber-elasticity modulus). Consider an incompressible network of $n$ ideal Gaussian chains per unit volume, crosslinked at their ends, deforming affinely — every chain's end-to-end vector transforming exactly as the macroscopic strain dictates. Its shear modulus is $G = nk_BT$ — $G$ here is a shear modulus, not the Gibbs free energy $G$ of §3 Definition 3.1, a collision this section walks straight into by having just said "Helmholtz rather than Gibbs" — and its Young modulus is $$\boxed{\;E \;=\; 3\,n\,k_BT\;}$$ Equivalently, for a network of density $\rho$ whose chains carry molar mass $M_c$ between crosslinks, $n = \rho N_A/M_c$ and $$E \;=\; \frac{3\rho N_Ak_BT}{M_c}, \qquad\text{so}\qquad M_c \;=\; \frac{3\rho N_Ak_BT}{E}.$$ Here $n$ is a number of chains per unit volume — not §2's Born exponent and not §1's amount of substance, both of which took the letter first; where confusion is possible this course writes $n_{\text{ch}}$.

By Proposition 4.2 a chain at extension $\mathbf R$ carries free energy $A(\mathbf R) = A_0 + 3k_BTR^2/(2Nb^2)$. Apply a homogeneous deformation with principal stretches $\lambda_1,\lambda_2,\lambda_3$ — the factors by which the three mutually perpendicular directions of pure stretch are lengthened, so $\lambda = 1$ is undeformed; affinity means each chain's vector goes from $(R_1,R_2,R_3)$ to $(\lambda_1R_1,\lambda_2R_2,\lambda_3R_3)$. Averaging over an unstrained population that is isotropic and Gaussian, so $\langle R_i^2\rangle = Nb^2/3$ for each component, the strain-energy density — written $W$ by convention, and it is neither §3's non-expansion work $W$ nor Module 1's body weight $W$, so where all three could meet this course writes the density $W_s$ — is $$W \;=\; n\,\frac{3k_BT}{2Nb^2}\sum_i \left(\lambda_i^2 - 1\right)\langle R_i^2\rangle \;=\; \frac{nk_BT}{2}\left(\lambda_1^2 + \lambda_2^2 + \lambda_3^2 - 3\right).$$

For uniaxial extension $\lambda_1 = \lambda$ with incompressibility $\lambda_2 = \lambda_3 = \lambda^{-1/2}$, this is $W = \tfrac12nk_BT(\lambda^2 + 2/\lambda - 3)$. The nominal stress is $\sigma = \mathrm dW/\mathrm d\lambda = nk_BT(\lambda - \lambda^{-2})$. Setting $\lambda = 1 + \varepsilon$ and expanding to first order, $\lambda - \lambda^{-2} \approx 3\varepsilon$, so $\sigma \approx 3nk_BT\,\varepsilon$ and the small-strain Young modulus is $E = 3nk_BT$. Comparing with the standard form $W = \tfrac12G(\lambda_1^2+\lambda_2^2+\lambda_3^2-3)$ identifies $G = nk_BT$, and $E = 3G$ is the incompressible case $\nu = \tfrac12$ of the isotropic relation $E = 2G(1+\nu)$ — consistent, and worth noting because §2 got $\nu = \tfrac14$ for a central-force crystal. A rubber and a rock have genuinely different Poisson ratios, and each is derived rather than assumed.

The chain count converts by species bookkeeping: one mole of network strands has mass $M_c$, so a unit volume of density $\rho$ holds $\rho/M_c$ moles of strand and $n = \rho N_A/M_c$ strands. $\blacksquare$

Read the boxed result for what it claims. A rubbery modulus is counting, not chemistry: it depends on how many independent chains cross a unit area and on the temperature, and on nothing whatever about the strength of the bonds inside them. Two networks of completely different chemistry with the same crosslink spacing have the same modulus. That is the opposite of §2's ionic solid, where the modulus was the bond energy density, and the contrast is the whole point.

Rubber lands at $0.98$ times its measured modulus and elastin at $1.41$. That is a genuine forward prediction of a tissue property from chemistry, and it is §4's counterpart to §2's apatite calculation. It also settles what entropic elasticity is for in this course: the megapascal-scale, highly extensible, thermally driven elasticity of elastin, which is the protein that gives skin, artery wall and the ligamentum flavum — the elastic band along the back of the spinal canal — their recoil.

Now run the same law at collagen. A fibril's modulus is the $1.07\ \mathrm{GPa}$ that Module 2 §5 takes as given. Inverting Proposition 4.3 for the crosslink spacing that would be required:

$$M_c \;=\; \frac{3\rho N_Ak_BT}{E} \;=\; \frac{3(1400)(6.022\times10^{23})(4.28\times10^{-21})}{1.07\times10^{9}} \;=\; 10.1\ \mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}.$$

An average amino-acid residue weighs about $100\ \mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}$. The Gaussian entropic law therefore demands a crosslink every tenth of one residue, which is not a chain at all, and a chain spacing of $0.23\ \mathrm{nm}$ — a single bond length, the $r_0$ of §2. The requirement is not merely unmet; it is incoherent.

It is worth putting the refutation the other way round as well, because that is the direction in which the margin can be stated honestly and it is smaller than a first pass suggests. Run Proposition 4.3 forwards at collagen's density and ask how stiff an entropic network could possibly be. At elastin's measured crosslink spacing it gives $1.67\ \mathrm{MPa}$, short of a fibril by $642$. Tightening the spacing raises it, but not without limit, and the limit must be one Proposition 4.3 actually covers. The proposition assumes Gaussian chains, which requires $N \gg 1$ Kuhn segments between crosslinks. An unfolded polypeptide has a Kuhn length of roughly $1$–$2\ \mathrm{nm}$, or three to five residues, so demanding $N \gtrsim 10$ puts the smallest legitimate strand at $M_c \approx 3$–$5\ \mathrm{kDa}$:

$$E^{\max}_{\text{Gaussian}} \;=\; \frac{3\rho N_Ak_BT}{M_{c,\min}} \;=\; 2.2\text{–}3.6\ \mathrm{MPa} \qquad\text{against a measured}\qquad 1070\ \mathrm{MPa}.$$

That is short by $300$ to $500$ times, and at elastin's actual crosslink spacing by $642$. It is worth saying why the box stops there rather than at a smaller $M_c$. Crosslinking every residue would give $108\ \mathrm{MPa}$ arithmetically, but at $M_c = 100\ \mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}$ a strand is shorter than one Kuhn segment: it is a rigid rod with no configurational entropy left to trade, and Proposition 4.3's hypothesis has failed. Quoting that number as a ceiling would give result typography to something the theorem does not produce — the same error, in a smaller way, as the one this section is correcting in §0 and §2. The honest headline is therefore $642$, and no Gaussian entropic network comes within two orders of magnitude of a gigapascal.

So what is a collagen fibril's compliance? The honest answer is a ladder of three energetic steps, none of them entropic, and this course can locate each one without deriving it.

  1. A pure bond-stretch count gives about $50\ \mathrm{GPa}$. This is §2's estimate for the protein rung, and it is the number that overshoots fiftyfold.
  2. One tropocollagen molecule measures about $6\ \mathrm{GPa}$ in tension — from X-ray diffraction on strained tendon, from Brillouin scattering, and from molecular dynamics, with reported values spanning $3$ to $9\ \mathrm{GPa}$. Note that this is deliberately not attributed to the single-molecule stretching experiments that supply the persistence length above: those are read as an entropic worm-like-chain fit at low force, and reading one experiment as both an entropic fit and a tensile modulus would be having it both ways. The factor of eight lost between rungs 1 and 2 is not entropy: it is that pulling a triple helix unwinds and bends it as well as stretching its bonds, and bending a rod is far more compliant than stretching it. §2 Proposition 2.1 gives exactly the sign of this — those are internal coordinates the affine bound freezes — and it is an energetic compliance, stored in bond angles rather than in configurational counting.
  3. A fibril measures about $1\ \mathrm{GPa}$. The remaining factor of six is lost to intermolecular shear — slip between molecules inside one fibril, so intrafibrillar, and not the fibril-on-fibril sliding that lowers a whole fascicle below a single fibril. Molecules are staggered end-to-side rather than continuous, so load transfers between them through a compliant layer of crosslinks and water, and the assembly is softer than its parts.

One escape route has to be closed before the ladder, because Proposition 4.3 is a theorem about Gaussian networks and not about entropic elasticity in general. A semiflexible network — one crosslinked at intervals shorter than its persistence length — is not bounded by $3nk_BT$ at all. Its modulus instead scales as $\kappa^2/(k_BT\,\xi^2\ell_c^3)$ with $\kappa$ the bending rigidity, $\xi$ the mesh size and $\ell_c$ the distance between crosslinks (MacKintosh, Käs and Janmey, Phys. Rev. Lett. 75, 4425, 1995), and it can be orders of magnitude stiffer. Two facts shut the route. First, collagen is not in that regime: a mature fibril carries one or two load-bearing crosslinks per $300\ \mathrm{nm}$ molecule, so $\ell_c \sim 150$–$300\ \mathrm{nm}$ against $\ell_p = 14.5\ \mathrm{nm}$, and $\ell_c \gg \ell_p$ is precisely the flexible regime Proposition 4.3 covers. Second, and independently, the semiflexible modulus carries $k_BT$ in the denominator, so a stiff-regime network softens on warming — it would follow the brown line of Fig. 14, not the blue one, and the discriminator of the next part would not classify it as entropic either. The bound and the measurement agree.

Every step of that ladder is enthalpic. Not one of them is $-T\,\partial S/\partial x$. The place entropy genuinely enters collagen mechanics is elsewhere and smaller: the telopeptides, the short non-helical tails at each molecule's ends, are the one flexible element of the molecule and therefore the only candidate for an entropic contribution, at the very low loads where a fibril is being taken up rather than stretched. Even that is a candidate rather than a measurement: inside a mature fibril the telopeptides are crosslinked and partly folded, which is exactly what would suppress the configurational freedom an entropic spring needs. That is a real effect at the edge of the toe of a stress–strain curve, and it is not the fibril's modulus.

measured, not derived, and the reason has now changed. §0 and §2 both attributed the collagen rung's shortfall to entropic elasticity and deferred the calculation to this section. That attribution is wrong, and §4 is where it is withdrawn: §4 Proposition 4.3 shows a Gaussian entropic network gives $1.67\ \mathrm{MPa}$ at a real protein's crosslink spacing, and at most $2.2$–$3.6\ \mathrm{MPa}$ at the tightest spacing for which its own $N \gg 1$ hypothesis still holds, so it is short of a fibril's gigapascal by $642\times$ in practice and by $300$ to $500\times$ at that limit — not the fifty §2 expected, and the shortfall cannot be repaired by adjusting the spacing. The claim is about Gaussian networks, which is the regime collagen is in: its crosslinks sit far apart compared with its persistence length. The correct account is the three-step energetic ladder above — bond count $50\ \mathrm{GPa}$, helix bending and unwinding to $\approx 6\ \mathrm{GPa}$, intermolecular shear to $\approx 1\ \mathrm{GPa}$ — whose middle and lower rungs this course reports from measurement rather than deriving. $E_{\text{collagen}}$ therefore stays measured, and it stays measured for a better reason than before.

That module asserts, correctly, that "a straightened fibril is nearly linear", and uses the assertion to build the toe region out of crimp geometry and fibre recruitment instead. Proposition 4.2 is why the assertion holds. Had a fibril's compliance been entropic, its force–extension law would have been the strongly curved worm-like chain of Fig. 12, and a straightened fibril would not be linear — the J-shape would be molecular and Module 6's population argument would be double-counting. Because the compliance is instead bond-angle elasticity at strains of a few per cent, the straightened fibril is linear to good accuracy, and Module 6's crimp-and-recruitment derivation of the toe is the correct one. §4 does not supply the origin of the toe. It supplies the licence for Module 6 to derive it the way it does.

The discriminator: warm it and see which way it goes

The distinction this section rests on is not a matter of interpretation. It is measurable, with a thermometer, and the two mechanisms answer with opposite signs.

By Proposition 4.1 an entropic spring's tension is proportional to $T$ at fixed extension, so its stiffness rises with temperature: from $20\ ^\circ\mathrm C$ to body temperature is a factor $310.15/293.15$, or $+5.8\%$. An energetic spring does the opposite, because warming loosens the very bonds whose curvature §2 Lemma 2.1 computed; for steel $\mathrm dE/\mathrm dT \approx -0.03\%$ per kelvin, giving $-0.51\%$ over the same interval. A stretched rubber band pulls harder when heated and a steel wire pulls less hard, and the same test applied to a tissue says which kind of elasticity it has.

Two remarks keep this from being over-read. The rise is small, $5.8\%$ over seventeen kelvin, so the measurement needs care; but its sign is unambiguous and that is what is being used. And the same physics has a companion effect that is easier to feel than to measure: stretch a rubber band quickly and it warms, because the work done against $-T\,\partial S/\partial x$ appears as heat when the entropy falls at nearly constant energy. This is the Gough–Joule effect, and the fact that a rubber band warms on stretching while a steel wire cools slightly is the same discriminator run adiabatically.

What this section licenses

Three things are now available. A molecular force law exists for any flexible biopolymer, so a chain's tension can be computed from $k_BT$, a contour length and a persistence length — which §7 will supply for each of the macromolecules from its architecture. A network modulus can be predicted forwards from crosslink chemistry alone, by Proposition 4.3, accurate to within about $40\%$ for the two networks it has been tested on. And the character of any tissue's elasticity can be settled experimentally by warming it, which is the cleanest available test of whether a model should be reaching for a bond stiffness or for an entropy.

What this section has also done is remove a wrong claim from two earlier ones, which is worth stating plainly rather than leaving in a marker. §0 and §2 said collagen's compliance was entropic; it is not, and their factor of fifty was never §4's to compute. The reader should take from this the same lesson the module has now learned three times: an attribution that sounds mechanistic and goes unchecked survives every automated test in the build and fails the first time somebody puts numbers in it.

What remains missing is the medium. Every chain in this section sat in an unspecified solvent, and Definition 4.3's persistence length was quoted without saying what sets it. For a charged biopolymer — which collagen, the glycosaminoglycans and DNA all are — a large part of the bending rigidity is electrostatic self-repulsion, and it collapses when the surrounding salt screens it. §5 supplies that screening, along with the water and ions that Module 4 §2's swelling pressure and every membrane potential in the course depend on.

5. Water, ions, and the electric double layer

Four earlier sections stopped at the same wall, and this one is where they are paid. §3 dropped the electrical work of moving a charged ion across a membrane, justified it by the reticulum's near-zero potential, and said in the same breath that the excuse "would be false at the cell surface". §3 also used an ionic strength inside Definition 3.4 and glossed it as §5's subject. §4 quoted a persistence length for collagen without saying what sets it, and closed by warning that for a charged polymer much of the bending stiffness is electrostatic and collapses when salt screens it — which, if true at body conditions, would move §4's numbers. And Module 4 §2 takes a swelling pressure from a fixed charge density as given.

All four turn on one length. Water is a strong dielectric and a body fluid is a concentrated salt solution, and between them they cut electrostatics off at about eight tenths of a nanometre. Everything in this section is a consequence of that number.

Water, and what it does to a charge

Definition 5.1 (relative permittivity and the Bjerrum length). A medium's relative permittivity $\epsilon_r$ is the factor by which it weakens the Coulomb interaction between two charges: the energy of charges $q_1, q_2$ at separation $r$ becomes $q_1q_2/(4\pi\epsilon_0\epsilon_r r)$. It is dimensionless, and $\epsilon_r = 1$ in vacuum. The Bjerrum length $\ell_B$ is the separation at which two elementary charges interact with exactly one unit of thermal energy, $$\ell_B \;\equiv\; \frac{e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0\epsilon_rk_BT} \;=\; \frac{C_e}{\epsilon_rk_BT},$$ with $C_e = e^2/4\pi\epsilon_0 = 2.307\times10^{-28}\ \mathrm{J\,m}$ as in §2 Definition 2.1. Here $\epsilon_r$ is a relative permittivity and $\epsilon_0$ the permittivity of free space; neither is the strain $\varepsilon$ whose letter they share, as §2 also had to say.

For water at body temperature $\epsilon_r = 73.2$, and the number is worth carrying to three figures because it is not the $78.4$ of a physical-chemistry table: water's permittivity falls as it warms, and using the room-temperature value at $37\ ^\circ\mathrm C$ overstates every screened interaction by $7\%$.

measured, not derived: the relative permittivity of water is a dielectric measurement and nothing in this course computes it. It is worth saying why it is so large and so temperature-sensitive, because that is what makes it a tissue parameter rather than a constant: the value near $73$–$78$ comes almost entirely from the reorientation of permanent molecular dipoles held in a hydrogen-bonded network, not from electron-cloud polarisation, which contributes under $2$. Warming disorders that network, so $\epsilon_r$ falls with temperature — $78.4$ at $25\ ^\circ\mathrm C$, $73.2$ at $37\ ^\circ\mathrm C$ — and it also falls sharply in the first hydration layers next to a charged surface, where the water is already oriented and cannot reorient again. This course uses the bulk value throughout, which overstates screening very close to a protein surface.

The consequence for §2 is immediate and large. §2 computed the Coulomb energy of a $\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}\!-\!\mathrm{O^{2-}}$ pair at $r_0 = 0.240\ \mathrm{nm}$ in vacuum, where it is $898\,k_BT$ — permanent on §1's thermal ruler, which is exactly why apatite is a solid. Put the same pair in water and divide by $73.2$:

$$U_{\text{pair}}\big|_{\text{water}} \;=\; \frac{4C_e}{\epsilon_rr_0} \;=\; 12.3\,k_BT.$$

Still above thermal energy, but only by an order of magnitude rather than three, and that is the whole reason a mineral crystal dissolves at all, why calcium exists as a free ion in the cytosol at $0.1\ \mu\mathrm M$, and why §3's pumps have anything to pump. Water does not weaken the ionic bond a little. It removes it from the permanent category on §1's ladder and puts it into the reversible one.

Screening: the Debye length

A dielectric weakens a charge everywhere by the same factor. A salt solution does something different and more drastic: mobile ions rearrange around a charge, the counter-ions crowding in and the like ions retreating, and the resulting cloud cancels the charge beyond a finite distance. First the quantity §3 owed a definition.

Definition 5.2 (ionic strength). For a solution containing species $i$ at molar concentration $c_i$ with valences $z_i$, the ionic strength is $$I \;\equiv\; \tfrac12\sum_i c_iz_i^2.$$ The square is what matters: a divalent ion counts four times as much as a monovalent one at the same concentration, because screening depends on charge squared and not on charge. The symbol $I$ is an ionic strength in this course's chemistry; it is not the second moment of area $I$ of Module 2 §3, and where the two could meet the area moment keeps the letter. Blood plasma, with the composition tabulated below, has $I = 0.1425\ \mathrm M$.
Proposition 5.1 (the Debye length). Let a small potential $\phi$ be applied within an electrolyte of ionic strength $I$ at temperature $T$ in a medium of permittivity $\epsilon_0\epsilon_r$, with the ions distributed by Boltzmann's law. Then to first order in $\phi$ the potential obeys $\nabla^2\phi = \phi/\lambda_D^2$ with $$\boxed{\;\lambda_D \;=\; \sqrt{\frac{\epsilon_0\epsilon_rk_BT}{2e^2N_AI}}\;}$$ so that the potential of a point charge $q$ falls as $\phi(r) = \dfrac{q}{4\pi\epsilon_0\epsilon_rr}e^{-r/\lambda_D}$ rather than as $1/r$. The length $\lambda_D$ is the Debye length, and it scales as $I^{-1/2}$: quadrupling the salt halves the reach. The symbol $\lambda$ is a screening length here, not the principal stretch $\lambda$ of §4 Proposition 4.3, and the subscript is kept for that reason.

Two equations and one linearisation. Poisson's equation relates the potential to the local charge density $\rho$: $\nabla^2\phi = -\rho/(\epsilon_0\epsilon_r)$. Boltzmann's law gives the local concentration of each species in that potential, $c_i(\mathbf r) = c_i^{\infty}\exp\!\left(-z_ie\phi/k_BT\right)$, where $c_i^{\infty}$ is the concentration far away. The charge density is the sum over species, $\rho = N_Ae\sum_i z_ic_i$, with $c_i$ in moles per unit volume.

Now linearise, which is the step that limits the result. For $|z_ie\phi| \ll k_BT$ — equivalently for potentials well under $26.7\ \mathrm{mV}$, since $k_BT/e = 26.7\ \mathrm{mV}$ at body temperature — expand the exponential as $1 - z_ie\phi/k_BT$. Then $$\rho \;\approx\; N_Ae\sum_i z_ic_i^{\infty} \;-\; \frac{N_Ae^2\phi}{k_BT}\sum_i z_i^2c_i^{\infty}.$$

The first sum vanishes: the bulk solution is electrically neutral, which is what $\sum_i z_ic_i^{\infty} = 0$ says. The second sum is $2I$ by Definition 5.2. So $\rho = -2N_Ae^2I\phi/(k_BT)$, and substituting into Poisson's equation,

$$\nabla^2\phi \;=\; \frac{2N_Ae^2I}{\epsilon_0\epsilon_rk_BT}\,\phi \;\equiv\; \frac{\phi}{\lambda_D^2},$$

which identifies $\lambda_D$ as displayed. In spherical symmetry the substitution $\phi = u(r)/r$ turns this into $u'' = u/\lambda_D^2$, whose decaying solution is $u \propto e^{-r/\lambda_D}$; matching the $r \to 0$ limit to the unscreened Coulomb potential fixes the prefactor. $\blacksquare$

At plasma strength the answer is $\lambda_D = 0.794\ \mathrm{nm}$, which is about three water molecules. Two charges further apart than that do not interact at all in any way a tissue cares about, and this single fact organises most of what follows: it is why proteins must touch to bind, why the fixed charge of cartilage acts osmotically rather than electrostatically at a distance, and why a membrane potential is a property of a surface a nanometre thick rather than of a cell's interior.

One honesty is owed here, because the derivation invites more confidence than it deserves. Debye–Hückel is a mean-field theory: it replaces the discrete ions by a smooth charge cloud. Ask how many ions actually sit inside one Debye sphere at plasma strength, using §1's counting, and the answer is 0.3 — the total ion concentration of $0.276\ \mathrm M$ gives a mean ion spacing of $1.82\ \mathrm{nm}$, more than twice $\lambda_D$. There is, on average, no ion at all within a screening length. The theory is therefore being used past the regime where its own averaging is justified, and at physiological salt it should be read as a scaling law that gets the length right to a factor of order one, not as a precise result. It is used that way throughout this course, and the places where it would matter — a divalent counter-ion condensing onto a highly charged surface, where the linearisation also fails — are named when they arise.

The electrical work §3 was allowed to drop

§3 priced the calcium pump using a chemical potential alone. For a charged species that is incomplete, and the completion is a definition.

Definition 5.3 (electrochemical potential). For an ion of valence $z_i$ in a region at electric potential $\phi$, the electrochemical potential is $$\tilde\mu_i \;=\; \mu_i^{\circ} + R_gT\ln\frac{c_i}{c^{\circ}} + z_iF\phi,$$ where $F = 96\,485\ \mathrm{C\,mol^{-1}}$ is the Faraday constant, the charge of one mole of elementary charges. It is written $F$ here and $F_{\text{faraday}}$ wherever a mechanical force $F$ is also in play, as Module 4 §2 requires. The first two terms are §3 Proposition 3.2 unchanged; the third is the work of carrying charge $z_iF$ to a place held at potential $\phi$. Equilibrium for a species free to cross a boundary is equality of $\tilde\mu_i$, not of $c_i$ and not of $\mu_i$.
Proposition 5.2 (the Nernst equation). Let an ion of valence $z$ be at equilibrium across a membrane, with concentration $c_{\text{out}}$ outside and $c_{\text{in}}$ inside. Then the membrane potential, defined as $E = \phi_{\text{in}} - \phi_{\text{out}}$, satisfies $$\boxed{\;E \;=\; \frac{R_gT}{zF}\,\ln\frac{c_{\text{out}}}{c_{\text{in}}}\;}$$ with $R_gT/F = 26.73\ \mathrm{mV}$ at body temperature. This $E$ is the equilibrium or Nernst potential of that ion: the one membrane potential at which it has no tendency to move. It is not a modulus, and where both appear this course writes $E_{\text{Nernst}}$.

Equilibrium is equality of the electrochemical potential of Definition 5.3 on the two sides: $$\mu^{\circ} + R_gT\ln\frac{c_{\text{in}}}{c^{\circ}} + zF\phi_{\text{in}} \;=\; \mu^{\circ} + R_gT\ln\frac{c_{\text{out}}}{c^{\circ}} + zF\phi_{\text{out}}.$$

The standard terms cancel because it is the same species on both sides — which is the hypothesis doing the work, and it fails for a species that changes its binding state on crossing. Collecting, $$zF\left(\phi_{\text{in}} - \phi_{\text{out}}\right) \;=\; R_gT\left(\ln\frac{c_{\text{out}}}{c^{\circ}} - \ln\frac{c_{\text{in}}}{c^{\circ}}\right) \;=\; R_gT\ln\frac{c_{\text{out}}}{c_{\text{in}}},$$

and dividing by $zF$ gives the boxed result. The reference concentration $c^{\circ}$ cancels, which is why a Nernst potential needs no standard state. $\blacksquare$

Now the bill. §3 costed the reticulum pump at $23.75\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ per mole of calcium, chemical work only, and justified dropping the electrical term because the reticulum membrane carries counter-ion channels that hold its potential near zero. That excuse is unavailable at the cell surface, where the potential is $-90\ \mathrm{mV}$ and calcium carries $z = 2$. Pumping one mole of calcium out of the cell costs the chemical work of the gradient plus the electrical work of carrying $2F$ against $90\ \mathrm{mV}$:

$$\Delta G_{\text{PM}} \;=\; \underbrace{R_gT\ln\frac{c_{\text{out}}}{c_{\text{in}}}}_{25.3} \;+\; \underbrace{zF\left(\phi_{\text{out}} - \phi_{\text{in}}\right)}_{17.4} \;=\; 42.6\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}.$$

Nearly twice the reticulum figure, for the same ion across the same concentration ratio, and the entire difference is the term §3 was allowed to leave out. This is why a cell has two distinct calcium pumps with different stoichiometries and different energetics, and why the reticulum — not the cell surface — is where a muscle does its fast, cheap, repeated calcium handling. It also sharpens §3's stoichiometry argument: with an ATP worth $52.7$ to $62.5\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$, a plasma-membrane pump at $42.6$ per calcium can afford one ion per ATP and not two. The observed stoichiometry of the plasma-membrane calcium ATPase is one.

Donnan: why a charged tissue swells

Cartilage carries a fixed charge: its glycosaminoglycans bear sulfate and carboxyl groups that cannot leave, at a concentration §1's ladder puts at $c_F = 0.2\ \mathrm M$. measured, not derived: the fixed charge density of cartilage is obtained from biochemical assay — sulfate and carboxyl groups counted per wet weight from glycosaminoglycan content — or inferred from the equilibrium salt partitioning this section derives, which would be circular if used here and is not. Nothing in this course predicts it, because it is set by how much proteoglycan a chondrocyte has secreted, which is biology rather than physical chemistry. It varies strongly with depth through the cartilage layer and falls with age and with osteoarthritis, from roughly $0.25$ to under $0.1\ \mathrm M$; the $0.2\ \mathrm M$ used here is a mid-depth healthy value, and Proposition 5.3 makes the swelling pressure roughly linear in it over that range, so the spread carries straight through to the pressure. A tissue like that cannot reach the same ion composition as the fluid around it, and the mismatch is an osmotic pressure.

Proposition 5.3 (Donnan equilibrium and swelling pressure). Let a matrix hold immobile negative charge at concentration $c_F$, bathed in a $1{:}1$ salt solution at concentration $c_{\text{ext}}$, with mobile monovalent ions at equilibrium. Then electroneutrality inside and equality of electrochemical potentials give $$c_+ \;=\; \frac{c_F}{2} + \sqrt{c_{\text{ext}}^2 + \left(\frac{c_F}{2}\right)^{\!2}}, \qquad c_- \;=\; c_+ - c_F,$$ and the swelling pressure, the excess osmotic pressure of the interior, is $$\boxed{\;\Pi \;=\; 2R_gT\left[\sqrt{c_{\text{ext}}^2 + \left(\frac{c_F}{2}\right)^{\!2}} \;-\; c_{\text{ext}}\right]\;}$$ It is positive for any $c_F \gt 0$, and it falls as the external salt rises.

Two conditions. Electroneutrality of the interior requires $c_+ - c_- - c_F = 0$. Equality of electrochemical potential for each mobile ion across the boundary, by Definition 5.3, gives $c_+^{\text{in}} = c_{\text{ext}}e^{-F\phi_D/R_gT}$ and $c_-^{\text{in}} = c_{\text{ext}}e^{+F\phi_D/R_gT}$ for the Donnan potential $\phi_D$ of the interior relative to the bath. Multiplying the two eliminates $\phi_D$ and gives the Donnan product $c_+c_- = c_{\text{ext}}^2$.

Substituting $c_- = c_+ - c_F$ into that product gives $c_+^2 - c_Fc_+ - c_{\text{ext}}^2 = 0$, whose positive root is the displayed $c_+$; the negative root is discarded as unphysical. Then $c_+ + c_- = 2c_+ - c_F = 2\sqrt{c_{\text{ext}}^2 + (c_F/2)^2}$, the $c_F$ cancelling exactly.

For the pressure, use van 't Hoff's law — that a dilute solute contributes $R_gT$ per mole of osmotically active particle, which is §3 Proposition 3.2 read at a semipermeable boundary. The interior has $c_+ + c_-$ moles of mobile ion per unit volume against $2c_{\text{ext}}$ outside, so $$\Pi = R_gT\left[(c_+ + c_-) - 2c_{\text{ext}}\right] = 2R_gT\left[\sqrt{c_{\text{ext}}^2 + (c_F/2)^2} - c_{\text{ext}}\right].$$

The bracket is positive because $\sqrt{a^2+b^2} \gt a$ for $b \ne 0$, and it decreases as $c_{\text{ext}}$ grows, since $\sqrt{a^2+b^2} - a \to b^2/2a$ for large $a$. The fixed charge itself does not appear in the count of osmotic particles because it is bound to the matrix and cannot move; only the mobile-ion excess it forces contributes. $\blacksquare$

Put in the numbers. With $c_F = 0.2\ \mathrm M$ and $c_{\text{ext}} = 0.15\ \mathrm M$, the interior holds $c_+ = 0.280\ \mathrm M$ and $c_- = 0.080\ \mathrm M$ against $0.150$ of each outside, an excess osmolarity of $0.0606\ \mathrm M$, and

$$\Pi \;=\; 0.0606\ \mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}\times R_gT \;=\; 156\ \mathrm{kPa} \;\approx\; 1.5\ \text{atmospheres}.$$

That is the pressure Module 4 §2 uses, now derived from a charge density rather than assumed, and it is a large number: a cartilage layer is pre-stressed to more than an atmosphere before any joint load arrives, which is what keeps its collagen network in tension and its water in place. Two readings follow at once. The pressure falls when the bath is saltier, which is measurable — cartilage shrinks in concentrated saline and swells in dilute, a standard laboratory demonstration and a standard experimental artefact. And it falls when $c_F$ falls, which is what happens in osteoarthritis as glycosaminoglycan is lost; Module 4 follows that consequence.

pH, pKa, and how a tissue holds its acidity

The one ion left is the proton, and it is the one whose concentration a tissue must hold most tightly. Blood pH is regulated to about $\pm 0.03$, a $7\%$ window in $[\mathrm H^+]$, which is far tighter than any other ion.

Proposition 5.4 (the Henderson–Hasselbalch relation). For a weak acid dissociating as $\mathrm{HA} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm H^+ + \mathrm A^-$ with equilibrium constant $K_a$, define $\mathrm{pH} = -\log_{10}([\mathrm H^+]/c^{\circ})$ and $\mathrm{p}K_a = -\log_{10}K_a$. At equilibrium $$\boxed{\;\mathrm{pH} \;=\; \mathrm{p}K_a + \log_{10}\frac{[\mathrm A^-]}{[\mathrm{HA}]}\;}$$ so a buffer holds the pH near its own $\mathrm{p}K_a$, and the ratio of base to acid is fixed by how far the pH sits from it.

By §3 Proposition 3.4 the reaction is at equilibrium when its quotient equals $K_a$, that is when $[\mathrm H^+][\mathrm A^-]/[\mathrm{HA}] = K_a$, every concentration divided by $c^{\circ}$. Taking $-\log_{10}$ of both sides and using $\log(xy) = \log x + \log y$, $$-\log_{10}[\mathrm H^+] - \log_{10}\frac{[\mathrm A^-]}{[\mathrm{HA}]} \;=\; -\log_{10}K_a,$$

which rearranges to the boxed relation. Note that no new physics enters: this is §3's equilibrium condition written in logarithms, and the whole content of "buffering" is already in the boxed result of Proposition 3.4. $\blacksquare$

Test it on the body's principal buffer. Carbonic acid has $\mathrm{p}K_a' = 6.10$ under blood conditions, plasma bicarbonate is $24\ \mathrm{mM}$, and dissolved carbon dioxide is $0.0301\ \mathrm{mM}$ per mmHg times an arterial $40\ \mathrm{mmHg}$, which is $1.20\ \mathrm{mM}$. Then $\mathrm{pH} = 6.10 + \log_{10}(24/1.20) = 6.10 + 1.30 = 7.40$, which is arterial blood pH to three figures.

How well a buffer works is a separate quantity from where it works.

Definition 5.4 (buffer capacity). The buffer capacity $\beta$ is the amount of strong base that must be added per unit rise in pH, $\beta \equiv \mathrm dC_b/\mathrm{dpH}$. For a single weak acid of total concentration $C_{\text{tot}}$, $$\beta \;=\; \ln 10\;C_{\text{tot}}\,\frac{K_a[\mathrm H^+]}{\left(K_a + [\mathrm H^+]\right)^2},$$ which is maximal at $\mathrm{pH} = \mathrm{p}K_a$, where it equals $C_{\text{tot}}\ln 10/4 = 0.576\,C_{\text{tot}}$, and falls to half its peak about $0.6$ pH units either side. The symbol $\beta$ is a buffer capacity here, not the dimensionless force $\beta = fb/k_BT$ of §4's Langevin function.

Now the interesting part, which is a genuine consequence rather than a definition. At blood pH the bicarbonate system is $1.3$ units above its own $\mathrm{p}K_a$, so Definition 5.4 gives it a capacity of only $2.5\ \mathrm{mM}$ per pH unit — on paper a poor buffer, working far from its optimum. It is nevertheless the dominant one, and the reason is that the body does not run it as a closed system. The lungs hold dissolved carbon dioxide fixed by adjusting ventilation, so adding acid consumes bicarbonate without accumulating $\mathrm{CO_2}$, and the capacity becomes $\beta = \ln 10\,[\mathrm{HCO_3^-}] = 55\ \mathrm{mM}$ per pH unit instead:

$$\frac{\beta_{\text{open}}}{\beta_{\text{closed}}} \;=\; \frac{55.3}{2.51} \;=\; 22.$$

A factor of twenty-two, bought not by chemistry but by coupling the buffer to an organ that removes one of its products — which is §3 Proposition 3.5's shared-intermediate argument applied to a physiological system rather than to a pump. It is also why respiratory and metabolic acid–base disturbances behave so differently: one moves the fixed variable, the other does not.

One consequence reaches back to §2 and forward to bone. The skeleton is a carbonate-bearing mineral reservoir, and apatite dissolves under acid load: sustained metabolic acidosis is buffered in part by dissolving bone mineral, releasing carbonate and calcium. The mechanism is exactly §2's lattice energy competing with §5's proton activity, and it is why chronic acid loading is a risk factor for bone loss. This course does not model that competition quantitatively; naming it is enough to show that the mineral of §2 and the pH of §5 are one subject.

What actually sets a persistence length — §4's debt

§4 used $\ell_p = 14.5\ \mathrm{nm}$ for tropocollagen and warned that for a charged polymer part of the stiffness is electrostatic self-repulsion, which screening removes. If that part were large at body conditions, §4's chain numbers would be unsafe. It is now computable.

A charged filament resists bending partly because bending brings its own like charges closer together. The standard estimate splits the persistence length into an intrinsic part and an electrostatic addition, $\ell_p = \ell_p^{0} + \ell_{\text{OSF}}$, with

$$\ell_{\text{OSF}} \;=\; \frac{\ell_B\lambda_D^2}{4A^2},$$

where $A$ is the spacing between charges along the contour. This is the Odijk–Skolnick–Fixman result, and it is quoted rather than derived: it comes from expanding the screened Coulomb energy of a weakly bent charged rod to second order in curvature, which is a calculation this course does not need to reproduce. Note its structure, which is the part that matters: $\ell_{\text{OSF}} \propto \lambda_D^2 \propto 1/I$, so the electrostatic stiffening is inversely proportional to salt concentration and vanishes fast as salt rises.

Tropocollagen carries roughly one net charge per nanometre of helix. At plasma ionic strength, with $\ell_B = 0.736\ \mathrm{nm}$ and $\lambda_D = 0.794\ \mathrm{nm}$, that gives $\ell_{\text{OSF}} = 0.116\ \mathrm{nm}$: eight tenths of one per cent of $14.5\ \mathrm{nm}$. §4's persistence length is intrinsic, its numbers are safe, and its warning — correctly hedged as a warning — does not bite at body conditions.

It bites hard elsewhere, and that is worth keeping. Repeat the calculation in a dilute $1\ \mathrm{mM}$ buffer, where $\lambda_D = 9.47\ \mathrm{nm}$, and $\ell_{\text{OSF}} = 16.5\ \mathrm{nm}$ — slightly larger than the intrinsic stiffness, so the same molecule would be more than twice as stiff. Any single-molecule persistence length measured in low salt and quoted as a property of the molecule is therefore reporting the buffer as much as the protein, which is a plausible part of why the reported values for tropocollagen span $10$ to $180\ \mathrm{nm}$. That is a caution about the literature §4 draws on, delivered by the section §4 deferred it to.

What this section licenses

Five things are now available. Electrostatics has a range, $\lambda_D \approx 0.8\ \mathrm{nm}$ in any body fluid, so any claim about charges interacting at a distance in a tissue is wrong unless it explains why. Charged species have a complete potential, Definition 5.3, so no later pump or channel calculation may omit the electrical term without saying that the membrane holds no potential and why. Equilibrium potentials are computable, which is what Module 5's excitation needs. A fixed charge density gives a swelling pressure, Proposition 5.3, which is Module 4 §2's starting point derived rather than assumed. And pH is a thermodynamic variable like any other, with a capacity that can be computed and a coupling to ventilation that explains why the number is held so tightly.

What remains missing is rate, and it is now the only thing missing. Every result in §3 and §5 is an equilibrium statement: it says where a reaction or a gradient ends up, and nothing whatever about how long it takes. A pump with a favourable $\Delta G$ may turn over a thousand times a second or once an hour; a buffer at equilibrium says nothing about how fast it absorbs a proton; a channel's Nernst potential says nothing about its conductance. §6 supplies the barrier heights and rate constants that decide all of it, and with them the one clinical fact this module has been circling since §1: why a cold muscle is weak.

6. Kinetics: how fast, and why a cold muscle is weak

Everything in §3 and §5 is an equilibrium statement. Each says where a reaction or a gradient ends up, and each is silent about how long it takes to get there. That silence is not a small gap. A reaction with $\Delta G = -15\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ may complete in a microsecond or sit untouched for a century; §3 Proposition 3.5 said so explicitly and deferred the matter here. This section supplies the missing half, and with it the one clinical fact the module has been circling since §1: a cold muscle is weak, and by a factor that can be computed.

The section also closes a loop that will look like a contradiction until it is resolved. §3 made a great deal of the ATP concentration: the free energy depends on it logarithmically, and a fatiguing bout moves $|\Delta G|$ by $16\%$. §6 will show that the same bout moves the crossbridge rate by $0.09\%$. Both are right, and the reason they differ is the whole difference between a thermodynamic and a kinetic dependence on one number.

Rate, mass action, and a speed limit

Definition 6.1 (rate constants and mass action). For an elementary reaction $\mathrm A + \mathrm B \to \mathrm{AB}$, the law of mass action states that the rate is proportional to the product of reactant concentrations, $v = k_{\text{on}}[\mathrm A][\mathrm B]$, with $k_{\text{on}}$ the association rate constant in units of $\mathrm M^{-1}\mathrm s^{-1}$. The reverse reaction $\mathrm{AB} \to \mathrm A + \mathrm B$ is first order, $v = k_{\text{off}}[\mathrm{AB}]$, with $k_{\text{off}}$ in $\mathrm s^{-1}$. The letter $k$ is a rate constant throughout this section; it is not §4's chain stiffness $k_{\text{chain}}$, not §2's bond stiffness $k_{\text{pair}}$, not Boltzmann's constant $k_B$, and not the remodelling gain $k_{\text{remodel}}$ of Module 2 §7. Where two appear together this course subscripts both.

Mass action is not a law of nature in the way §3's inequalities are. It is a statement that reaction requires encounter and that encounters are independent, which is true for a dilute solution and fails in a crowded one or on a surface. It is used here as the standard idealisation, with that limitation named once.

Proposition 6.1 (the dissociation constant is a ratio of rates). A binding reaction obeying Definition 6.1 reaches equilibrium when association and dissociation balance, and the equilibrium dissociation constant is $$\boxed{\;K_d \;=\; \frac{k_{\text{off}}}{k_{\text{on}}} \;=\; \frac{[\mathrm A][\mathrm B]}{[\mathrm{AB}]}\;}$$ so an equilibrium quantity is fixed entirely by two kinetic ones. Consequently a tight binder can be tight in two quite different ways — by binding fast or by releasing slowly — and $K_d$ alone cannot tell them apart.

At equilibrium the two rates of Definition 6.1 are equal: $k_{\text{on}}[\mathrm A][\mathrm B] = k_{\text{off}}[\mathrm{AB}]$. Rearranging gives $[\mathrm A][\mathrm B]/[\mathrm{AB}] = k_{\text{off}}/k_{\text{on}}$, and the left-hand side is the reaction quotient of §3 Definition 3.3 for the dissociation direction, which at equilibrium is $K_{\text{eq}}$ by Proposition 3.4. So $K_d = k_{\text{off}}/k_{\text{on}}$, and by §3 Proposition 3.4 it also equals $\exp(-\Delta G^{\circ}/R_gT)$ for the dissociation. The two descriptions must agree, and their agreement is a constraint: a mechanism may not choose $k_{\text{on}}$, $k_{\text{off}}$ and $\Delta G^{\circ}$ independently. $\blacksquare$

There is a ceiling on $k_{\text{on}}$, and it is set by diffusion rather than by chemistry. Two molecules cannot react faster than they can find each other, and how fast they find each other is a diffusion problem solved by Smoluchowski: the encounter rate for spheres of combined diffusion coefficient $D_{\mathrm A} + D_{\mathrm B}$ meeting at separation $R_{\text{enc}}$ is $k_{\text{diff}} = 4\pi(D_{\mathrm A}+D_{\mathrm B})R_{\text{enc}}N_A$. For a calcium ion, whose diffusion coefficient in water is $7.9\times10^{-10}\ \mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}$, meeting a protein site at $R_{\text{enc}} = 0.5\ \mathrm{nm}$,

$$k_{\text{diff}} \;=\; 4\pi\left(D_{\mathrm{Ca}} + D_{\text{prot}}\right)R_{\text{enc}}N_A \;=\; 3.0\times10^{9}\ \mathrm{M^{-1}\,s^{-1}}.$$

Measured association rates for calcium onto its binding proteins run near $10^{8}\ \mathrm{M^{-1}\,s^{-1}}$, which is $3.3\%$ of that limit. Two readings follow. Calcium binding is close to as fast as physics allows, so essentially every encounter that could bind does — and therefore no evolutionary improvement in the binding site can speed it up much. And with $k_{\text{off}} = 100\ \mathrm s^{-1}$ the dissociation constant is $K_d = k_{\text{off}}/k_{\text{on}} = 1.0\ \mu\mathrm M$, sitting exactly between §1's resting calcium of $0.1\ \mu\mathrm M$ and its activated $10\ \mu\mathrm M$. That placement is the design: a switch must have its threshold inside the range of the signal.

measured, not derived: association and dissociation rate constants come from stopped-flow and temperature-jump kinetics, and nothing in this course predicts them. Two cautions. The pair quoted here, $k_{\text{on}} = 10^{8}\ \mathrm{M^{-1}s^{-1}}$ and $k_{\text{off}} = 100\ \mathrm s^{-1}$, are round order-of-magnitude figures chosen to give the measured $K_d \approx 1\ \mu\mathrm M$ of cardiac troponin C's regulatory site; individual studies scatter over a factor of several in each, and only their ratio is well constrained, because that ratio is an equilibrium quantity measurable by titration. So the arithmetic below that uses $K_d$ is on firm ground and the arithmetic that uses $k_{\text{off}}$ alone — the $10\ \mathrm{ms}$ release time — is not, to better than a factor of two or three. And in a filament the site's rates are not the isolated protein's: cooperativity, discussed next, means the neighbours change them.

Proposition 6.2 (fractional occupancy). For a single site binding a ligand at concentration $c$ with dissociation constant $K_d$, the equilibrium fraction of sites occupied is $$\boxed{\;\theta \;=\; \frac{c}{K_d + c}\;}$$ a hyperbola rising from $0$ to $1$ with $\theta = \tfrac12$ at $c = K_d$. It takes a factor of $81$ in $c$ — $1.91$ decades — to move $\theta$ from $0.1$ to $0.9$.

Let $S_{\text{tot}}$ be the total site concentration and write $\theta = [\mathrm{AB}]/S_{\text{tot}}$, so that free sites are $S_{\text{tot}}(1-\theta)$. Proposition 6.1 at equilibrium gives $K_d = c\,S_{\text{tot}}(1-\theta)/(S_{\text{tot}}\theta) = c(1-\theta)/\theta$. Solving for $\theta$: $K_d\theta = c - c\theta$, so $\theta(K_d + c) = c$ and $\theta = c/(K_d+c)$.

For the width, $\theta = 0.1$ requires $c = K_d/9$ and $\theta = 0.9$ requires $c = 9K_d$, a ratio of $81$, whose base-ten logarithm is $2\log_{10}9 = 1.908$. Note the hypothesis that makes this a single-site result: the ligand concentration $c$ is taken as fixed, which requires the ligand to be in excess over the sites. In a half-sarcomere holding $64$ free calcium ions, by §1 Proposition 1.2, that assumption is badly violated and the buffering by the sites themselves must be solved for. $\blacksquare$

Cooperativity: how a graded signal becomes a switch

A hyperbola is a poor switch. Proposition 6.2 needs nearly two decades of ligand to go from a tenth to nine tenths occupied, and a muscle has only two: §1's calcium runs from $0.1$ to $10\ \mu\mathrm M$. A single-site thin filament would therefore be $9\%$ activated at rest — a permanently half-contracted muscle. Real thin filaments are not like that, and the reason is that their sites are not independent.

Definition 6.2 (the Hill equation and the Hill coefficient). The Hill equation describes occupancy of a cooperative system as $$\theta \;=\; \frac{c^{\,n_H}}{K^{\,n_H} + c^{\,n_H}},$$ with $n_H$ the Hill coefficient and $K$ the ligand concentration giving half occupancy. It reduces to Proposition 6.2 at $n_H = 1$. The width of the transition narrows as $1/n_H$: the $10\%$-to-$90\%$ range spans $2\log_{10}(9)/n_H$ decades. This is a fitting form, not a mechanism. A Hill coefficient is a measured steepness, and $n_H$ is not in general the number of binding sites; it equals the site count only in the limit of infinitely strong cooperativity, and a system with four sites typically fits $n_H$ between $2$ and $3$. Reporting $n_H$ as a site count is the commonest misuse of this equation. No proof follows, because there is nothing to prove: unlike every other boxed relation in this module, Definition 6.2 is a curve chosen to fit data, and it is not boxed for that reason.

measured, not derived: a Hill coefficient and a $Q_{10}$ are both fitted steepnesses, not predicted quantities, and neither is a property of a molecule on its own. $n_H \approx 3$ for thin filaments comes from fitting Definition 6.2 to a measured force–calcium curve on skinned fibres, and it varies with sarcomere length, temperature and preparation over roughly $2$ to $5$. That spread matters more than it looks: the $10\%$–$90\%$ width goes as $1/n_H$, so it is a factor of $2.5$ in the sharpness of the switch this section is about. $Q_{10} \approx 2.5$ for crossbridge cycling is likewise a fit, taken over a temperature range narrower than the one it is then extrapolated across, and different steps of the cycle have different values — so a single $Q_{10}$ is an average over a mechanism, not a barrier height for one step. The $70\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ derived from it inherits all of that, and the $2.98\times$ cooling factor below should be read as ‘about threefold’.

Thin-filament activation fits $n_H \approx 3$ with half-activation near $1\ \mu\mathrm M$, which is $\mathrm{pCa}\ 6$ on the convention $\mathrm{pCa} = -\log_{10}[\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}]$. Put §1's two calcium levels through it and the difference from a single site is stark:

Cooperativity converts a two-decade graded signal into something that is off at rest and fully on when activated. It buys that at a price worth naming: a steep switch is also a sensitive one, and a system with $n_H = 3$ amplifies any error in the calcium level threefold in the logarithm. A muscle whose resting calcium creeps up by a factor of three goes from $0.1\%$ to $2.6\%$ activated, which is a measurable resting tension. Steepness and robustness trade against each other, and the body has chosen steepness.

Temperature: Arrhenius, and the cold muscle

Rate constants depend on temperature far more strongly than equilibrium constants do, and the reason is that a rate is controlled by a barrier while an equilibrium is controlled by a difference of endpoints.

Proposition 6.3 (Arrhenius, and the $Q_{10}$). If reaction requires surmounting a barrier of height $E_a$ and the fraction of attempts with enough energy is Boltzmann-distributed, the rate constant is $$\boxed{\;k(T) \;=\; A\,e^{-E_a/R_gT}\;}$$ with $E_a$ the activation energy and $A$ a prefactor. Defining the temperature coefficient $Q_{10} \equiv k(T+10)/k(T)$, the two are related by $$\boxed{\;E_a \;=\; \frac{R_g\,T(T+10)}{10}\,\ln Q_{10}\;}$$ so a measured $Q_{10}$ determines $E_a$ and hence the rate at any other temperature. Note $E_a$ is an energy per mole and not Module 2 §5's modulus $E$; the subscript is not decoration.

Take the barrier picture as given — it is the assumption, not the result. If a reaction proceeds only when the reacting pair holds at least $E_a$ per mole in the relevant coordinate, then by the Boltzmann distribution the fraction of pairs so equipped is $e^{-E_a/R_gT}$, and the rate is that fraction times an attempt frequency $A$ which collects everything else: encounter rate, geometry, and how often an energetic attempt actually crosses. Hence $k = Ae^{-E_a/R_gT}$.

For the second relation, form the ratio at two temperatures ten kelvin apart, assuming $A$ and $E_a$ are constant over the interval: $$Q_{10} = \frac{k(T+10)}{k(T)} = \exp\!\left[\frac{E_a}{R_g}\left(\frac{1}{T} - \frac{1}{T+10}\right)\right] = \exp\!\left[\frac{E_a}{R_g}\cdot\frac{10}{T(T+10)}\right].$$

Taking the logarithm and solving for $E_a$ gives the boxed form. The assumption that $A$ is temperature-independent is the weak point: transition-state theory gives $A \propto T$, which over a ten-kelvin interval near body temperature contributes about $3\%$ — negligible beside the exponential, which is why the Arrhenius form survives despite being an approximation to the better theory. $\blacksquare$

Now the fact. Crossbridge cycling in skeletal muscle has a measured $Q_{10}$ of about $2.5$. Proposition 6.3 turns that into a barrier:

$$E_a \;=\; \frac{R_g(298.15)(308.15)}{10}\ln 2.5 \;=\; 70.0\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}},$$

and with $E_a$ in hand the cooling penalty is a calculation rather than a guess. A limb cooled from $37\ ^\circ\mathrm C$ to $25\ ^\circ\mathrm C$ — a cold morning, a long swim, an ice bath — has every rate constant multiplied by

$$\frac{k(298.15)}{k(310.15)} \;=\; \exp\!\left[-\frac{E_a}{R_g}\left(\frac{1}{298.15} - \frac{1}{310.15}\right)\right] \;=\; 0.335,$$

a threefold slowdown. That is why a cold muscle is weak, and the mechanism is worth stating precisely because "weak" is ambiguous. Cooling barely changes the force a fully activated crossbridge can hold: that is a thermodynamic quantity, and §3's ceiling moved only $5\%$ over the same interval, while §4's discriminator showed an energetic spring stiffening by half a per cent. What cooling changes is every rate: how fast calcium is released and re-sequestered, how fast crossbridges attach and detach, and therefore how fast the muscle can develop and relax tension and how much power it can deliver. A cold muscle is not a weaker spring. It is a slower engine, and at any speed above zero a slower engine delivers less force.

One consistency check is available and worth running, because it tests the barrier picture rather than just using it. Transition-state theory gives the prefactor explicitly as $A = k_BT/h$, which at body temperature is $6.5\times10^{12}\ \mathrm s^{-1}$. A crossbridge cycles at roughly $10\ \mathrm s^{-1}$, so if that prefactor is right the barrier must be $\Delta G^{\ddagger} = R_gT\ln(6.5\times10^{12}/10) = 70\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$. That is the same number the $Q_{10}$ gave, arrived at from a completely different measurement — the absolute cycling rate rather than its temperature dependence. The agreement is not a triviality, and it is not quite an identity either: $E_a$ and $\Delta G^{\ddagger}$ differ, with $E_a \approx \Delta H^{\ddagger} + R_gT$ and $\Delta G^{\ddagger} = \Delta H^{\ddagger} - T\Delta S^{\ddagger}$. Their coinciding at $70$ therefore says something specific: that $\Delta S^{\ddagger} \approx -8\ \mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}K^{-1}}$, a small negative entropy of activation, meaning the transition state is slightly more ordered than the ground state and not dramatically so. That is a real inference about the crossbridge, obtained from two numbers neither of which was measured on a transition state.

Enzymes, and why §3's ATP dependence does not show up in the rate

A catalysed reaction does not obey mass action in the enzyme's substrate, because the enzyme can be saturated: there are only so many of them.

Proposition 6.4 (Michaelis–Menten). For an enzyme $\mathrm E$ binding substrate $\mathrm S$ reversibly and converting it irreversibly, $\mathrm E + \mathrm S \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{ES} \to \mathrm E + \mathrm P$ with rate constants $k_1, k_{-1}, k_2$, the steady-state rate is $$\boxed{\;v \;=\; \frac{v_{\max}\,c}{K_M + c}\;}, \qquad v_{\max} = k_2[\mathrm E]_{\text{tot}}, \qquad K_M = \frac{k_{-1}+k_2}{k_1},$$ where $c = [\mathrm S]$. The form is Proposition 6.2's hyperbola again, but $K_M$ is not a dissociation constant: it exceeds $K_d = k_{-1}/k_1$ by $k_2/k_1$, and equals it only when catalysis is slow compared with release.

Apply the steady-state approximation: after a brief transient the complex concentration is nearly constant, $\mathrm d[\mathrm{ES}]/\mathrm dt \approx 0$. Formation is $k_1[\mathrm E][\mathrm S]$ and loss is $(k_{-1}+k_2)[\mathrm{ES}]$, so $$k_1[\mathrm E]c = (k_{-1}+k_2)[\mathrm{ES}] \quad\Longrightarrow\quad [\mathrm{ES}] = \frac{[\mathrm E]c}{K_M}, \qquad K_M \equiv \frac{k_{-1}+k_2}{k_1}.$$

Conservation of enzyme gives $[\mathrm E]_{\text{tot}} = [\mathrm E] + [\mathrm{ES}]$, so $[\mathrm E] = [\mathrm E]_{\text{tot}} - [\mathrm{ES}]$ and substituting, $$[\mathrm{ES}] = \frac{\left([\mathrm E]_{\text{tot}} - [\mathrm{ES}]\right)c}{K_M} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad [\mathrm{ES}] = [\mathrm E]_{\text{tot}}\frac{c}{K_M + c}.$$

The product appears at $v = k_2[\mathrm{ES}]$, which gives the boxed form with $v_{\max} = k_2[\mathrm E]_{\text{tot}}$. The steady-state hypothesis requires the substrate to be in large excess over the enzyme, which is what makes $c$ effectively constant while the complex equilibrates; for a muscle at $5\ \mathrm{mM}$ ATP against micromolar myosin heads that is comfortably satisfied. $\blacksquare$

Now resolve the apparent contradiction with §3. Myosin's $K_M$ for ATP is about $50\ \mu\mathrm M$. Cellular ATP is $5\ \mathrm{mM}$, a hundred times higher, so

$$\frac{v}{v_{\max}} \;=\; \frac{5.5\ \mathrm{mM}}{0.05 + 5.5\ \mathrm{mM}} \;=\; 0.991 \qquad\text{falling to}\qquad 0.990 \ \text{at } 5.0\ \mathrm{mM}.$$

Across a whole fatiguing bout the rate changes by $0.09\%$, while §3 showed the free energy of the same reaction changing by $15.7\%$. Both are correct, and the difference is structural rather than a matter of precision. The free energy depends on $\ln[\mathrm{ATP}]$ and on the product concentrations, which change a great deal; the rate depends on $[\mathrm{ATP}]/(K_M + [\mathrm{ATP}])$, which is saturated and therefore flat. A cell holds ATP a hundred times above $K_M$ for exactly this reason: it decouples the speed of its machinery from its ATP level, so that the level can be allowed to fall without the machinery slowing, right up to the point where the thermodynamic budget fails. Saturation is a design choice, and $99\%$ of maximal rate needs only $4.95\ \mathrm{mM}$.

What this section licenses

Four things. A binding affinity is now two numbers rather than one, so any model that fits $K_d$ has not thereby fitted the kinetics, and a system's speed of response is a separate measurement from its threshold. Cooperativity is quantified, so the steepness of any biological switch can be stated as a Hill coefficient and its cost in robustness read off. Temperature is a computable variable: one measured $Q_{10}$ fixes an activation energy and hence the rate at any temperature a limb reaches, which is what Module 5 needs to explain cold weakness and what Module 4 §7 needs for the temperature dependence of synovial viscosity. And saturation is available as an explanation, which is the resolution of the ATP puzzle above and the reason enzyme kinetics and reaction thermodynamics must be kept in separate columns.

What remains missing is the molecules themselves. Every section so far has treated proteins as objects with a stiffness, a charge, a $K_d$ and a $Q_{10}$, and none has said what they are made of or why a collagen triple helix has the persistence length §4 used, or why §5's cartilage carries fixed charge, or which residues do the buffering §5 attributed to protein histidine. §7 supplies the architecture: the amino acid, the peptide bond, the Gly-X-Y triple helix, the glycosaminoglycan and the phospholipid bilayer, and with them the structural reasons behind the parameters this module has so far taken from measurement.

7. The macromolecules: where the parameters come from

Six sections have now treated proteins as objects with numbers attached. §4 gave collagen a contour length of $300\ \mathrm{nm}$ and a persistence length of $14.5\ \mathrm{nm}$; §5 gave cartilage a fixed charge of $0.2\ \mathrm M$, gave a membrane a potential, and attributed intracellular buffering to protein histidine; §6 gave a binding site a $K_d$. Every one of those came from measurement, and none of them was explained.

This section supplies the architecture, and its test is not whether the pictures are pretty. It is whether the architecture predicts the numbers. Four times below it does: the triple helix's residue count and rise recover §4's contour length, the Gly-X-Y rule predicts collagen's glycine content, the glycosaminoglycan disaccharide predicts §5's fixed charge from a biochemical assay, and the bilayer's geometry both gives a membrane capacitance and settles an assumption §5 made silently and never checked.

The amino acid and the peptide bond

Definition 7.1 (amino acid, residue, peptide bond). An amino acid is a molecule carrying an amino group $\mathrm{-NH_2}$, a carboxyl group $\mathrm{-COOH}$, and a side chain $\mathrm R$, all on one carbon. A peptide bond is the amide link formed when one acid's carboxyl condenses with the next one's amino group, releasing water; the repeating unit it leaves in the chain is a residue, and a chain of them is a polypeptide. The backbone is the repeating $\mathrm{-N-C_\alpha-C-}$ sequence, identical in every protein; the side chains are what differ. Twenty side chains are used, and the mean residue mass in an average protein is $110\ \mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}$.

Two structural facts about the peptide bond do all the work later. It is planar, because the amide nitrogen's lone pair delocalises into the carbonyl and gives the link partial double-bond character: rotation about it is stiff and the six atoms of the link lie in a plane. And rotation is therefore free only about the two single bonds either side of each $\mathrm C_\alpha$, whose angles are named $\phi$ and $\psi$. A polypeptide is not a freely jointed chain of atoms; it is a chain of rigid planar plates hinged at two angles per residue, and that is why §4's Kuhn length for an unfolded chain is one to two nanometres rather than a bond length.

One reconciliation is owed immediately, because §4 used a round number here. §4's reductio needed the mass of one residue and took it as "about $100\ \mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}$". The true figures are $110$ for an average protein and, for collagen specifically, $89.7$ — lower because collagen is a third glycine, and glycine at $57.05$ is the lightest residue there is. §4's $100$ sits between the two, and its conclusion — that an entropic network would need a crosslink every tenth of a residue — is unaffected by a $20\%$ change in a quantity it was using to show a factor of ten was impossible.

The Gly-X-Y triple helix, and where §4's 300 nm comes from

Collagen is the one protein in this course whose mechanics the module has computed, and its architecture is unusually rigid and unusually easy to state.

Definition 7.2 (the collagen triple helix). A collagen $\alpha$ chain has the repeating sequence Gly-X-Y: every third residue is glycine, $\mathrm X$ is most often proline and $\mathrm Y$ most often hydroxyproline — proline with an added $\mathrm{-OH}$, made after the chain is built. Each chain forms a left-handed helix of about three residues per turn, and three such chains wind about one another into a right-handed supercoil of pitch $8.6\ \mathrm{nm}$. The rise along the supercoil axis is $0.286\ \mathrm{nm}$ per residue.

The glycine is not decoration and its position is not arbitrary. Three chains packed against a common axis leave, at the point where they touch, room for a hydrogen atom and nothing larger. Glycine's side chain is a hydrogen atom, and it is the only residue of the twenty for which that is true. So the structure demands glycine at every position facing the axis, and the axis is approached once every three residues. That single geometric constraint predicts the sequence, and it makes a composition prediction that can be checked against a bottle of hydrolysed tendon:

$$\text{Gly-X-Y} \;\Longrightarrow\; \text{glycine} = \tfrac13 = 33.3\% \text{ of residues}; \qquad \text{measured: } 33\%.$$

Now §4's contour length, which §4 took from measurement and this section can build. The triple-helical domain of the collagen I $\alpha$ chain is $1014$ residues long, and at $0.286\ \mathrm{nm}$ each:

$$L_{\text{helix}} \;=\; 1014 \times 0.286\ \mathrm{nm} \;=\; 290.0\ \mathrm{nm}, \qquad +\ 10\ \mathrm{nm}\ \text{of telopeptides} \;=\; 300\ \mathrm{nm}.$$

That is §4's number, assembled from a residue count and a rise. The $10\ \mathrm{nm}$ discrepancy is not a fudge: it is the telopeptides, the short non-helical tails at each end, which are exactly the elements §4 identified as the only flexible part of the molecule and the only candidate for an entropic contribution. The two sections agree, and they agree about which $10\ \mathrm{nm}$ is which.

derived here: $L_c = 300\ \mathrm{nm}$ follows from a residue count and a crystallographic rise, with no mechanical measurement among the inputs. The two inputs are a sequence, which is read from the gene, and a rise per residue of $0.286\ \mathrm{nm}$, which comes from X-ray fibre diffraction on the helix. One qualification: fibre diffraction measures the axial repeat of the assembled structure, so the rise is a property of collagen in a fibril rather than of an isolated molecule, and a molecule stretched taut in a trap would read slightly longer. That is the right order of correction for the $10\ \mathrm{nm}$ discrepancy above, so the agreement should be read as good to a few per cent rather than exact.

The glycosaminoglycan, and where §5's fixed charge comes from

§5 derived a swelling pressure of $156\ \mathrm{kPa}$ from a fixed charge density of $0.2\ \mathrm M$ and declined to predict the charge density, saying it is set by how much proteoglycan a cell has secreted. That is right as far as it goes, but the step from how much to how many charges is chemistry and can be done here.

Definition 7.3 (glycosaminoglycan and proteoglycan). A glycosaminoglycan (GAG) is an unbranched polysaccharide of repeating disaccharides, each carrying at least one negative charge. Cartilage's principal GAG is chondroitin sulfate, whose disaccharide bears one carboxyl and one sulfate group and therefore two fixed negative charges, at a molar mass of $458\ \mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}$. Both groups are fully ionised at physiological pH — the sulfate's $\mathrm{p}K_a$ is near $1$ and the carboxyl's near $3$, both far below $7.4$ by §5 Proposition 5.4. Many GAG chains attached to one core protein form a proteoglycan, and in cartilage these are bound to hyaluronan into aggregates too large to diffuse out, which is what makes the charge fixed.

The conversion is now one line. A GAG content of $m$ milligrams per millilitre gives

$$c_F \;=\; \frac{2\,m}{M_{\text{disacc}}} \;=\; \frac{2m}{458}\ \mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}\ \ (m \text{ in } \mathrm{g\,L^{-1}}).$$

Healthy cartilage assays at $40$ to $70\ \mathrm{mg\,mL^{-1}}$ of GAG, which gives $c_F = 0.175$ to $0.306\ \mathrm M$. §5's assumed $0.2\ \mathrm M$ sits inside that, and corresponds to $45.8\ \mathrm{mg\,mL^{-1}}$ — a perfectly ordinary value. So §5's fixed charge is now traceable to a biochemical assay through a stoichiometry, rather than being a number chosen to make the swelling pressure come out right. The chain of inference runs: GAG content by assay $\to$ two charges per disaccharide $\to$ $c_F$ $\to$ Proposition 5.3 $\to$ $156\ \mathrm{kPa}$ $\to$ Module 4's pre-stressed cartilage. Nothing in that chain is fitted to a mechanical measurement.

Side chains, and which residue does §5's buffering

§5's buffer figure attributed the intracellular capacity at pH $7.4$ largely to "protein histidine", and gave histidine a $\mathrm{p}K_a$ of $7.3$. Both claims can now be justified, and one of them needs qualifying.

Of the twenty side chains, seven are ionisable, and their $\mathrm{p}K_a$ values in free solution are: aspartate $3.9$, glutamate $4.3$, histidine $6.0$, cysteine $8.3$, tyrosine $10.1$, lysine $10.5$, arginine $12.5$. By §5 Definition 5.4 a buffer contributes capacity only within about $1.5$ units of its own $\mathrm{p}K_a$, so at pH $7.4$ the field narrows to two: histidine and cysteine. It is worth being exact about this, because the tempting statement — that histidine is the only side chain placed to buffer at physiological pH — is false, and an earlier draft of this section's verification script asserted it and failed.

Histidine dominates the capacity nonetheless, and for reasons of abundance and availability rather than placement. Histidine is about $2.3\%$ of residues and cysteine about $1.4\%$, and most cysteine in a structural protein is locked into disulfide bonds and cannot titrate at all; taking a tenth of it as free, histidine carries roughly $16\times$ the capacity. The honest version of §5's claim is therefore that histidine dominates intracellular buffering because there is more of it available, not because it is uniquely well placed.

The $\mathrm{p}K_a$ shift is the second point and it is a general one. Free histidine titrates at $6.0$; §5 used $7.3$. The difference is environment: a side chain buried against hydrophobic neighbours, or sitting near another charge, has its $\mathrm{p}K_a$ shifted by an amount §5's Definition 5.1 makes plausible — the local relative permittivity in a protein interior is far below water's $73.2$, so charge separation there costs more, and an equilibrium that creates charge is pushed back. Shifts of one to two units are routine and up to four are known. The consequence for this course is a caution: a $\mathrm{p}K_a$ measured on a free amino acid may not be the one operating in a tissue, and §5's $7.3$ is the in-protein value precisely because that is the one that buffers.

The phospholipid bilayer, and an assumption §5 never checked

Definition 7.4 (phospholipid and bilayer). A phospholipid has a charged or polar head group and two hydrocarbon tails. In water the tails associate to avoid contact with it — the hydrophobic effect, which by §3's boxed $\Delta G = \Delta H - T\Delta S$ is driven by the entropy of the water released from ordering around them, not by any bond between the tails. The result is a bilayer: two sheets of lipid tail-to-tail, about $5\ \mathrm{nm}$ thick overall with a hydrocarbon core near $2.5\ \mathrm{nm}$, at roughly $0.65\ \mathrm{nm^2}$ per lipid, so about $3.1\times10^{6}$ lipids per square micrometre of membrane.

A bilayer is therefore a thin sheet of hydrocarbon, of relative permittivity about $2.2$, between two conducting solutions. That is a capacitor, and its capacitance per unit area follows from Definition 5.1's permittivity:

$$C/A \;=\; \frac{\epsilon_0\epsilon_r}{d} \;=\; \frac{(8.854\times10^{-12})(2.2)}{2.5\times10^{-9}} \;=\; 0.78\ \mu\mathrm{F\,cm^{-2}},$$

against a measured membrane capacitance close to $1\ \mu\mathrm{F\,cm^{-2}}$ — a factor of $1.28$, from a calculation whose only inputs are a thickness and a dielectric constant. The remaining discrepancy is honest and locatable: real membranes are not pure hydrocarbon slabs, the head-group region contributes, and the effective thickness is smaller than the core.

Now the assumption. §5 derived the Nernst equation using bulk concentrations on each side of a membrane, and never asked whether holding a potential across that membrane changes those concentrations. If it did, the derivation would be circular. Take a cell of radius $25\ \mu\mathrm m$ holding the resting $-90\ \mathrm{mV}$. The charge separated is $Q = (C/A)\,V\,A$, and dividing by the elementary charge gives the ion count:

Eight parts per million. The membrane potential is held by a charge imbalance so small that the bulk concentrations are unchanged to five decimal places, which is exactly the licence §5 needed and did not stop to obtain. It also explains something the Nernst equation makes look paradoxical: a cell can depolarise and repolarise thousands of times without measurably altering its ion content, because each event moves parts per million. The pumps of §3 are not restoring the potential after every action potential — the potential barely costs anything to restore. They are fighting the slow background leak.

What this section licenses

The parameters this module has been using now have provenance in architecture rather than only in measurement. Collagen's contour length is a residue count times a rise. Cartilage's fixed charge is a GAG assay times a stoichiometry. A membrane's capacitance is a thickness and a dielectric constant, and the charge it holds is negligible against the cell's contents. Collagen's glycine content is predicted by the one geometric constraint that a triple helix imposes. Two of those predictions land within a few per cent, one lands inside a measured range, and one — the capacitance — lands within $30\%$ and says where the rest is.

What this section has not done should be equally clear, because it is the boundary of what a chemistry module can claim. It does not predict a persistence length: that needs the bending stiffness of three wound chains, which is a mechanics calculation. It does not predict how much proteoglycan a chondrocyte secretes, or how much collagen a tendon contains, because those are outcomes of regulation rather than of chemistry. The module's honest position is that chemistry fixes the per-molecule quantities and biology fixes how many, and every tissue property this course computes is a product of one of each.

8. Computational lab: two equilibria with no closed form

Both labs in this section exist because a result earlier in the module was stated with a hypothesis attached, and the interesting case is the one the hypothesis excludes. §6 Proposition 6.2 derived a binding curve and then said, in its own proof, that the ligand must be in excess over the sites — and that in a half-sarcomere holding §1's $64$ free calcium ions "that assumption is badly violated". Lab 8A solves the violated case. §3 and §6 each gave the calcium pump a temperature dependence, of different kinds, and neither asked what the two do together. Lab 8B runs them together, and the answer is not the one the earlier sections lead you to expect.

Neither problem has a closed form. The first is a nonlinear conservation equation in one unknown; the second is a sweep over three temperature-dependent terms that do not cancel. Both are a few dozen lines of standard library Python, and both are printed in full so the numbers quoted below can be reproduced rather than believed.

Lab 8A — binding when the ligand runs out

The set-up is §6's cooperative site with one hypothesis removed. There are $S_{\text{tot}} = 70\ \mu\mathrm M$ of troponin C regulatory sites in a muscle, with $K = 1\ \mu\mathrm M$ and $n_H = 3$. What is observable and controllable in an experiment is the total calcium, not the free concentration, and the two differ because the sites hold some of it. Conservation gives

$$c_{\text{tot}} \;=\; c_{\text{free}} \;+\; S_{\text{tot}}\,\theta\!\left(c_{\text{free}}\right), \qquad \theta(c) = \frac{c^{\,n_H}}{K^{\,n_H} + c^{\,n_H}}.$$

One equation, one unknown, and no closed form for $n_H = 3$ — substituting the Hill expression gives a quartic in $c_{\text{free}}$. But the left side is strictly increasing in $c_{\text{free}}$ (both terms are), so the root is unique and bisection is not merely adequate but guaranteed. That monotonicity is worth noticing rather than assuming: it is what licenses the bracket-and-halve loop in the code below, and it would fail for a system with negative cooperativity.

"""Lab 8A - binding when the ligand runs out.

Section 6 Proposition 6.2 assumed the ligand concentration is fixed, and
said in its own proof that a half-sarcomere holding 64 free calcium ions
violates that badly. This solves the case it excluded: sites and ligand
in comparable amounts, so binding depletes the free pool and the free
concentration must be solved for rather than assumed.

Conservation:   c_tot = c_free + S_tot * theta(c_free)
with            theta(c) = c^n / (K^n + c^n)

That is one nonlinear equation in c_free. There is no closed form for
n = 3, so it is bracketed and bisected.
"""

K_HALF = 1.0e-6          # M, half-activation, section 6
N_HILL = 3.0             # Hill coefficient, section 6
S_TOT = 70.0e-6          # M, troponin C regulatory sites in muscle


def theta(c, k_half=K_HALF, n=N_HILL):
    """Fractional occupancy of the cooperative sites."""
    if c <= 0.0:
        return 0.0
    return c ** n / (k_half ** n + c ** n)


def bound(c, s_tot=S_TOT):
    """Calcium held on the sites, in M, at free concentration c."""
    return s_tot * theta(c)


def free_from_total(c_tot, s_tot=S_TOT, tol=1e-18):
    """Solve c_tot = c_free + bound(c_free) for c_free, by bisection.

    The residual is strictly increasing in c_free, so a sign change
    brackets the unique root.
    """
    def residual(c):
        return c + bound(c, s_tot) - c_tot

    lo, hi = 0.0, max(c_tot, 1e-12)
    while residual(hi) < 0.0:
        hi *= 2.0
    for _ in range(200):
        mid = 0.5 * (lo + hi)
        if residual(mid) < 0.0:
            lo = mid
        else:
            hi = mid
        if hi - lo < tol:
            break
    return 0.5 * (lo + hi)


def sweep(totals):
    """Free concentration and occupancy across a range of total calcium."""
    rows = []
    for c_tot in totals:
        c_free = free_from_total(c_tot)
        rows.append({
            "c_tot": c_tot,
            "c_free": c_free,
            "theta_true": theta(c_free),
            "theta_naive": theta(c_tot),
            "buffered": c_tot - c_free,
        })
    return rows


def main():
    totals = [10 ** (-7.0 + 3.2 * i / 60.0) for i in range(61)]
    rows = sweep(totals)

    print("total Ca    free Ca     occupancy   naive        buffered")
    print("(uM)        (uM)        (true)      (c_tot used) fraction")
    for r in rows[::10]:

              f"{r['theta_true']:9.4f}  {r['theta_naive']:9.4f}  "
              f"{r['buffered']/r['c_tot']:9.3f}")

    print(f"\nhalf-occupancy needs a TOTAL calcium of "
          f"{half['c_tot']*1e6:.2f} uM,")
    print(f"  against a half-activation constant K of {K_HALF*1e6:.1f} uM:")
    print(f"  a factor of {half['c_tot']/K_HALF:.1f} more calcium than the "
          f"fixed-ligand answer.")

    print(f"\nthe fixed-ligand formula overstates occupancy most at "
          f"c_tot = {worst['c_tot']*1e6:.2f} uM:")

Lab 8B — the cooled pump, and which effect wins

§3 costed the reticulum calcium pump at body temperature and found a margin of $-5.24\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ in a working muscle. §6 then gave every rate constant an Arrhenius dependence with $E_a = 70\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$. Cool the limb and both change. The naive expectation, and the one §3's language about a "thermodynamic stall point" invites, is that they change the same way: the pump gets both weaker and slower. Three temperature-dependent terms decide it:

  1. the standard free energy, through $\Delta G^{\circ\prime}(T) = \Delta H^{\circ\prime} - T\Delta S^{\circ\prime}$, which by §3 makes ATP weaker as $T$ falls (because $\Delta S^{\circ\prime} \gt 0$);
  2. the concentration term $R_gT\ln Q$, which is proportional to $T$ and negative, so it also makes ATP weaker as $T$ falls;
  3. the calcium climb $2R_gT\ln(c_{\text{SR}}/c_{\text{cyt}})$, which is proportional to $T$ and positive, so cooling makes the gradient cheaper to hold.

Terms 2 and 3 both scale with $T$ and have opposite signs, so they do not cancel; which wins is an arithmetic question, and $2\ln(10^4) = 18.4$ against $|\ln Q| = 8.47$ suggests the climb should dominate. The lab settles it.

"""Lab 8B - the cooled pump: two temperature effects, opposite signs.

Section 3 costed the calcium pump at one temperature. Section 6 gave
every rate an Arrhenius temperature dependence. Neither asked what
happens to the two TOGETHER when a limb cools, and the answer is not the
obvious one.

Three things depend on temperature here:
  1. the standard free energy, through dG0' = dH0' - T dS0'
  2. the concentration term, through R_g T ln Q
  3. the calcium climb, through 2 R_g T ln(ratio)
and separately
  4. every rate constant, through Arrhenius.

Terms 2 and 3 are both proportional to T and have opposite signs, so
they do not cancel. Run it and see which way the margin goes.
"""

import math

R_G = 8.314462618        # J/(mol K)
T_BODY = 310.15          # K
DH0P = -20.5e3           # J/mol, ATP hydrolysis, section 3
DG0P_298 = -30.5e3       # J/mol at 298.15 K, section 3
DS0P = (DH0P - DG0P_298) / 298.15        # J/(mol K), assuming dCp = 0
Q_WORK = 2.10e-4         # reaction quotient, working muscle, section 3
CA_RATIO = 1.0e4         # SR lumen over cytosol, section 3
N_CA = 2                 # calcium ions per ATP
EA = 70.0e3              # J/mol, from Q_10 = 2.5, section 6


def dg0_prime(t):
    """Transformed standard free energy at temperature t, in J/mol."""
    return DH0P - t * DS0P


def dg_atp(t, q=Q_WORK):
    """Free energy of ATP hydrolysis at temperature t, in J/mol."""
    return dg0_prime(t) + R_G * t * math.log(q)


def dg_climb(t, ratio=CA_RATIO, n=N_CA):
    """Cost of lifting n calcium ions up the gradient, in J/mol."""
    return n * R_G * t * math.log(ratio)


def margin(t):
    """Net free energy of the coupled pump cycle, in J/mol."""
    return dg_atp(t) + dg_climb(t)


def rate_factor(t, ea=EA, t_ref=T_BODY):
    """Every rate constant, relative to its value at t_ref."""
    return math.exp(-ea / R_G * (1.0 / t - 1.0 / t_ref))


def sweep(temps):
    return [{
        "t_c": t - 273.15,
        "dg0": dg0_prime(t) / 1e3,
        "dg_atp": dg_atp(t) / 1e3,
        "climb": dg_climb(t) / 1e3,
        "margin": margin(t) / 1e3,
        "rate": rate_factor(t),
    } for t in temps]


def main():
    temps = [283.15 + 27.0 * i / 54.0 for i in range(55)]
    rows = sweep(temps)

    print(" T     dG0'    dG_ATP   climb   margin   rate")
    print("(C)   (kJ/mol)                          (rel)")
    for r in rows[::9]:
        print(f"{r['t_c']:5.1f} {r['dg0']:8.2f} {r['dg_atp']:8.2f} "
              f"{r['climb']:7.2f} {r['margin']:8.2f} {r['rate']:7.3f}")

    cold = rows[0]
    warm = [r for r in rows if abs(r["t_c"] - 37.0) < 0.3][0]
    print(f"\ncooling from {warm['t_c']:.1f} to {cold['t_c']:.1f} C:")
    print(f"  the margin goes {warm['margin']:+.2f} -> "
          f"{cold['margin']:+.2f} kJ/mol, "
          f"{'MORE' if cold['margin'] < warm['margin'] else 'less'} "
          f"favourable by {abs(cold['margin']-warm['margin']):.2f}")
    print(f"  every rate goes 1.000 -> {cold['rate']:.3f}, a "
          f"{1/cold['rate']:.1f}x slowdown")

    print("\nwhy the margin improves: the climb costs 2 R_g T ln(ratio),")
    print("which is proportional to T, so cooling makes the gradient")
    print(f"cheaper to hold ({warm['climb']:.2f} -> {cold['climb']:.2f}),")
    print("and that beats the weakening of ATP over the same interval")
    print(f"({warm['dg_atp']:.2f} -> {cold['dg_atp']:.2f}).")

    stall = [r for r in rows if r["margin"] >= 0.0]
    print(f"\nthe pump does not stall anywhere in 10-37 C: "
          f"{len(stall)} of {len(rows)} points have margin >= 0.")
    print("So a cold muscle's slow relaxation is KINETIC, not")
    print("thermodynamic. Section 3's stall argument belongs to fatigue")
    print("(rising phosphate and ADP), not to cold.")
    return rows


if __name__ == "__main__":

The margin improves because the climb is a pure $R_gT$ quantity — holding a concentration ratio against thermal motion costs less when there is less thermal motion — and the $4.13\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ that buys beats the $2.81$ lost from ATP. That sharpens something §3 left loose. §3 observed that a fatigued muscle's pump approaches its thermodynamic stall point and offered that as one contributor to slowed relaxation. This lab shows the argument does not transfer to cold: across the whole range from $10$ to $37\ ^\circ\mathrm C$ the margin is negative at every one of the $55$ points computed, so the pump never comes near stalling. Two mechanisms that produce the same symptom — slow relaxation — and only one of them is thermodynamic.

What the labs establish

Two methodological points and two physiological ones.

A hypothesis attached to a formula is not decoration. §6 Proposition 6.2 named its ligand-in-excess condition and Lab 8A shows that violating it moves the answer by a factor of $36$ in the half-point and by $0.93$ in occupancy. Every boxed result in this module carries hypotheses of that kind, and the reader now has one worked demonstration of what ignoring one costs.

Two temperature dependencies of a coupled system need not agree in sign. It is tempting, having established that cooling slows everything, to assume it weakens everything. Lab 8B shows the free-energy margin improving while the rate collapses, because $R_gT$ appears in the climb with the opposite sign to its appearance in the driving reaction. Neither §3 nor §6 alone could have found that; it required running them together, which is what a lab is for.

Physiologically: a muscle's calcium buffering is a first-order effect and not a correction, so free and total calcium must never be conflated. And the slowed relaxation of a cold muscle and of a fatigued muscle have different causes, kinetic in one case and partly thermodynamic in the other, which matters to any model that tries to explain both with one parameter.

9. What this chemistry captures and misses; diagnostics and problems

Eight sections have built a chemical foundation for a mechanics course, and the honest way to close is to say what it can now do, what it cannot, and where it was wrong on the way. Three of its own claims were withdrawn as it went, which is worth stating plainly rather than leaving in the commit history.

What it captures

The module set out to remove the unexplained constants from the course's mechanics, and it removed some of them. A bulk modulus is now a cohesive energy density, and apatite's $143\ \mathrm{GPa}$ follows from a lattice energy and a cell volume with no elastic measurement among the inputs (§2). A tissue's free-energy budget is computable at any composition, and the two pumps of a muscle cell have their stoichiometries capped — two calcium per ATP into the reticulum, one out through the cell surface — by arithmetic rather than by measurement (§3, §5). Elastin's modulus is predicted forwards from crosslink chemistry to within a factor of $1.4$, and the measured value is bracketed by the dry and hydrated calculations (§4). Cartilage's swelling pressure of $156\ \mathrm{kPa}$ descends from a glycosaminoglycan assay through a stoichiometry with no mechanical datum in the chain (§5, §7). Collagen's contour length is a residue count times a rise (§7). And the temperature dependence of everything a muscle does is one activation energy, which turns a $Q_{10}$ into a threefold prediction about a cold limb (§6).

Two structural results are worth more than any single number. The first is the provenance discipline: every gated parameter in this module declares whether it was derived here, imported from another section, or measured — and that discipline caught three errors that no other check would have. The second is the pairing of §3 with §6: a concentration can matter enormously to a thermodynamic quantity and not at all to a kinetic one, and a model that confuses the two will explain fatigue by fuel exhaustion, which is wrong.

What it misses

Five gaps, in order of how much they matter to the rest of the course.

It does not predict a persistence length. §4 used $\ell_p = 14.5\ \mathrm{nm}$ for tropocollagen and §7 could recover the contour length but not the bending stiffness, which needs the mechanics of three wound chains. §5 could only show that the electrostatic contribution is under one per cent at body salt — a bound on the error, not a derivation.

It does not predict how much of anything a cell makes. Fixed charge density, collagen content, myosin isoform, pump density: all are regulatory outcomes. The module's position is that chemistry fixes the per-molecule quantities and biology fixes how many, and every tissue property computed here is a product of one of each. That is a real boundary and not a temporary one.

Mean-field electrostatics is used at the edge of its validity. §5 stated it: at physiological ionic strength only $0.3$ ions sit inside one Debye sphere, so Debye–Hückel is a scaling law here rather than a precise theory. Every Debye length in this course should be read as good to a factor of order one.

Crowding is ignored throughout. Every activity in this module was a concentration, which assumes dilute non-interacting solutes. A cytosol is $20$–$30\%$ macromolecule by volume, where activities depart from concentrations by factors of several. §3 Proposition 3.2 named this and did not fix it.

There is no spatial dimension. Every equilibrium here is uniform. Diffusion appears only as a speed limit in §6, and a real calcium transient has gradients on the scale of the sarcomere. Module 4 §3's consolidation equation is the one place this course does chemistry in space.

Three claims this module withdrew

Rather than leave these in the version history, they belong here, because each is a lesson about how the errors were found.

  1. §0's stiffness ladder was circular. It presented bond stiffnesses that reproduced Module 2 §5's measured moduli, and those stiffnesses had been calibrated from those moduli. §2 ran the arrow forwards properly. Found by: asking where a table value came from.
  2. §0 and §2 attributed collagen's compliance to entropy. It is not entropic: a Gaussian network is short of a fibril by $642\times$ at any real crosslink spacing, and reaching $1\ \mathrm{GPa}$ would need a crosslink inside every amino acid. §4 withdrew the claim and named the real ladder — bond count, helix bending, intermolecular shear, all energetic. Found by: putting numbers into an attribution that had only ever been asserted.
  3. §4's own first correction was also wrong. Having withdrawn the entropic claim, §4 then boxed a ceiling of $108\ \mathrm{MPa}$ evaluated at a crosslink spacing shorter than one Kuhn segment — outside the hypothesis of the very proposition it was using. The box now holds the Gaussian-valid ceiling. Found by: a reviewer checking whether a boxed number was one the theorem produces.

The pattern in all three is the same, and it is the module's most transferable lesson: the claim that fails is not the one you compute, it is the one you assert while computing something adjacent.

Five diagnostics

Answer these before the problems. Each has a one-line answer and each tests whether a distinction in this module actually landed. Work them without looking back.

Diagnostic 1. A reaction has $\Delta G^{\circ} = +12\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$. Can it run forwards in a cell? In one sentence, say what determines the answer.

Answer

Yes. $\Delta G^{\circ}$ is the value at a reference composition no cell occupies; direction is set by $\Delta G = \Delta G^{\circ} + R_gT\ln Q$ (§3 Proposition 3.3), so any $Q$ below $\exp(-12/2.58) = 9.9\times10^{-3}$ makes it run. Holding products low is exactly how a cell drives uphill standard reactions.

Diagnostic 2. A muscle is cooled from $37$ to $25\ ^\circ\mathrm C$. Does the maximum force one crossbridge can exert go up, down, or stay about the same — and does the muscle get weaker?

Answer

The ceiling barely moves: it is $|\Delta G|/N_Ad$, a thermodynamic quantity, and $\Delta G$ changes a few per cent over $12\ \mathrm K$. The muscle nevertheless gets weaker, because every rate falls by a factor of about three (§6). A cold muscle is not a weaker spring; it is a slower engine. Conflating the two is the trap.

Diagnostic 3. Two proteins bind calcium with the same $K_d = 1\ \mu\mathrm M$. One has $k_{\text{on}} = 10^{8}$, the other $10^{5}\ \mathrm{M^{-1}s^{-1}}$. Which is the better calcium sensor for a twitch lasting $100\ \mathrm{ms}$?

Answer

The fast one. Equal $K_d$ means equal equilibrium occupancy, but $K_d = k_{\text{off}}/k_{\text{on}}$ (§6 Proposition 6.1) so the slow binder has $k_{\text{off}} = 0.1\ \mathrm s^{-1}$ and a relaxation time of $10\ \mathrm s$ — a hundred times longer than the twitch. It never reaches equilibrium and never releases in time. $K_d$ alone cannot tell you this.

Diagnostic 4. Cartilage is placed in distilled water instead of saline. Does it swell or shrink, and by what mechanism?

Answer

It swells. By §5 Proposition 5.3 the swelling pressure is $2R_gT[\sqrt{c_{\text{ext}}^2 + (c_F/2)^2} - c_{\text{ext}}]$, which increases as $c_{\text{ext}}$ falls, reaching $R_gTc_F$ in pure water — about $515\ \mathrm{kPa}$ for $c_F = 0.2\ \mathrm M$, more than three times the physiological value. External salt screens the fixed charge; remove it and the tissue takes up water until its collagen network stops it.

Diagnostic 5. A student computes the free energy of a Ca–O bond in bone as $898\,k_BT$ and concludes that calcium cannot leave bone mineral at body temperature. What is wrong?

Answer

The bond energy was computed in vacuum. In water the same pair at the same separation is $12.3\,k_BT$, weaker by the relative permittivity $\epsilon_r = 73.2$ (§5 Definition 5.1) — and that is before hydration of the freed ions, which lowers it further. Dissolution is slow for kinetic and surface reasons, not because the bond is thermally unreachable.

Problems — conceptual (C1–C10)

These test whether a distinction landed, not whether a formula can be recalled. Each should be answerable in a short paragraph.

C1. §2 proved that an affine estimate of a modulus is an upper bound, and that the same theorem makes Module 2 §5's Voigt average an upper bound on a composite. Explain in what sense these are the same theorem, and identify what plays the role of the "internal coordinates" in each case.

Probes: whether Proposition 2.1's Schur-complement argument is understood as a statement about constrained minimisation rather than about crystals.

Solution

Both are the statement that constraining a variational problem raises its minimum. In the crystal, the strain is imposed and the internal coordinates $\mathbf u$ — the atom shifts within the unit cell that the macroscopic strain does not dictate — are free to relax; forcing $\mathbf u = 0$ is the affine assumption, and the relaxed energy cannot exceed it. In the composite, the internal coordinate is the partition of strain between the phases; Voigt assumes both phases share one strain, which is the same "freeze the internal variable" assumption one level of description higher. In both cases the true stiffness is the Schur complement $\mathsf A - \mathsf B\mathsf D^{-1}\mathsf B^{\top}$ and the frozen estimate is $\mathsf A$. Equality requires $\mathsf B = 0$: no coupling between the macroscopic and internal variables, which for a crystal means every atom sits at an inversion centre, and for a composite means the phases have equal moduli.

C2. A colleague proposes measuring the standard free energy of ATP hydrolysis by putting ATP, ADP and phosphate each at $1\ \mathrm M$ and measuring the heat released. Give two reasons this does not give $\Delta G^{\circ\prime}$.

Probes: the distinction between $\Delta G$ and $\Delta H$, and the meaning of the transformed standard state.

Solution

First, heat is $\Delta H$, not $\Delta G$; they differ by $T\Delta S$, which for this reaction is about $10\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ (§3). A calorimeter measures the wrong quantity. Second, $\Delta G^{\circ\prime}$ is a transformed standard state (§3 Definition 3.4): pH and free magnesium are held fixed and each reactant is the sum of its protonation and magnesium-bound forms. Putting the species at $1\ \mathrm M$ each does not fix pH — the reaction releases a proton, and at $1\ \mathrm M$ concentrations the pH would move enormously. The measurement would report a mixture of conditions rather than a defined state.

C3. §4 showed that an entropic spring stiffens on warming while an energetic one softens. A tendon is warmed from $20$ to $37\ ^\circ\mathrm C$ and stiffens by $6\%$. Does this prove its elasticity is entropic? What else would you need to measure?

Probes: whether the discriminator is understood as a sign test with confounds, not a proof.

Solution

No. The sign is consistent with entropic elasticity, and $6\%$ is close to the $5.8\%$ that $f \propto T$ predicts, but a tendon is not a single mechanism: warming also changes water content, alters crosslink and proteoglycan behaviour, and can shift crimp geometry, any of which could stiffen it. To make the test decisive one would (i) check that the stiffening is reversible and instantaneous, since a structural change would show hysteresis and a time constant; (ii) check the magnitude against $T_2/T_1$ quantitatively across a wider range, since a structural mechanism has no reason to be linear in absolute temperature; and (iii) look for the Gough–Joule companion effect, warming on adiabatic stretch. Note also that §4 concluded collagen is not entropic, so a tendon showing a clean entropic signature would be evidence about its elastin and matrix rather than its collagen.

C4. Explain why the reticulum calcium pump can move two calcium ions per ATP while the plasma-membrane pump manages one, given that both face the same $10^{4}$ concentration ratio.

Probes: whether the electrical term of the electrochemical potential is understood as a real cost and not a formality.

Solution

The concentration ratio is the same but the electrochemical work is not. The reticulum membrane holds essentially no potential, because counter-ion channels let potassium and chloride follow the calcium, so the cost is the chemical term alone: $23.75\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ per ion, and two fit inside an ATP's $52.7$. The cell surface holds $-90\ \mathrm{mV}$, and moving a divalent cation out against that adds $zF\Delta\phi = 17.4\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$, taking the total to $42.6$ per ion (§5). Two would cost $85.2$ and no ATP can pay for it. The stoichiometries differ because the electrical term differs, not because the pumps differ in quality.

C5. Bicarbonate is the body's dominant buffer at pH $7.4$ despite having its $\mathrm{p}K_a$ at $6.1$, where §5 Definition 5.4 says its capacity is poor. Resolve this.

Probes: whether the open-system argument is understood as coupling rather than as a special case of buffering.

Solution

Definition 5.4's formula is for a closed system, in which adding acid consumes bicarbonate and accumulates carbon dioxide, and the accumulating product limits the capacity. The lungs hold dissolved $\mathrm{CO_2}$ fixed by adjusting ventilation, so one product never accumulates, and the capacity becomes $\ln 10\,[\mathrm{HCO_3^-}] = 55\ \mathrm{mM}$ per pH unit instead of $2.5$ — a factor of $22$. This is §3 Proposition 3.5's coupling argument applied to an organ rather than to a pump: the buffer is coupled to a process that removes its product, and the removal is what buys the capacity. Note the corollary: the advantage vanishes if ventilation cannot respond, which is why respiratory and metabolic acid–base disturbances behave so differently.

C6. A model of muscle fatigue attributes the loss of force to falling ATP concentration slowing the crossbridge cycle. Using two results from this module, explain why this is wrong, and state what the falling ATP does affect.

Probes: the thermodynamic-versus-kinetic distinction, which is the module's central pairing.

Solution

Two results. From §6 Proposition 6.4, myosin's $K_M$ for ATP is about $50\ \mu\mathrm M$ against a cellular $5\ \mathrm{mM}$, so the enzyme is $99\%$ saturated and the rate changes by $0.09\%$ across a whole bout — ATP concentration does not control the speed. From §3, the free energy depends on $\ln([\mathrm{ADP}][\mathrm{P_i}]/[\mathrm{ATP}])$ and falls $15.7\%$ over the same bout, so what the composition change does affect is the thermodynamic budget, and hence the ceiling on force per crossbridge (from $10.4$ to $8.8\ \mathrm{pN}$). Even that is not the cause of fatigue, because measured head forces of $3$–$6\ \mathrm{pN}$ sit under both ceilings. The dominant mechanism is phosphate acting on the crossbridge cycle itself, which is a kinetic and mechanistic effect of a product, not a fuel shortage.

C7. §5 notes that only $0.3$ ions sit inside one Debye sphere at physiological ionic strength. Explain why this is a problem for Debye–Hückel theory, and say whether it makes the $0.79\ \mathrm{nm}$ screening length useless.

Probes: whether a mean-field theory's validity condition is understood, and whether the student can hold "approximate" and "useful" together.

Solution

The derivation replaces the discrete ions by a smooth charge density and then linearises the Boltzmann factor. Both steps assume many ions within the region over which the potential varies, so that an average is meaningful; with $0.3$ ions per Debye sphere there is typically no ion at all within a screening length, and the "cloud" is a fiction on that scale. It does not make the length useless. What survives is the scaling — $\lambda_D \propto I^{-1/2}$, which is well confirmed — and the order of magnitude, which is what every use in this course relies on: that electrostatics has a range of roughly a nanometre in a body fluid. What does not survive is any claim to two-figure accuracy, or any use at a highly charged surface where the linearisation also fails and divalent counter-ions condense.

C8. §7 derives collagen's contour length from its residue count but says it cannot derive the persistence length. Explain what makes the second harder, and why §5's screening result does not close the gap.

Probes: the difference between a geometric and a mechanical property, and what a bound is.

Solution

Contour length is geometry: a count of residues times a rise per residue, both read from structure. Persistence length is $\ell_p = \kappa/k_BT$, a mechanical property requiring the bending rigidity of three chains wound about each other and hydrogen-bonded to one another — which depends on how load redistributes among the strands as the assembly bends, a calculation of the kind §2 Proposition 2.1 shows requires the full internal-coordinate problem. §5's result is a bound on one contribution: it shows the electrostatic part of $\kappa$ is under one per cent at body salt, so the remaining $99\%$ is intrinsic and unexplained. Ruling out one term is not deriving the sum.

C9. A reviewer objects that §4's refutation of entropic collagen is invalid, because a semiflexible network crosslinked inside its persistence length is not bounded by $3nk_BT$. Assess the objection.

Probes: whether the scope of Proposition 4.3 is understood, and whether the student can defend a result by checking its hypothesis against the case at hand.

Solution

The objection is correct in general and does not apply here, and §4 says so. A semiflexible network with crosslink spacing $\ell_c \lt \ell_p$ has a modulus scaling as $\kappa^2/(k_BT\xi^2\ell_c^3)$ and can be orders of magnitude stiffer than $3nk_BT$. But collagen is not in that regime: a mature fibril carries one or two load-bearing crosslinks per $300\ \mathrm{nm}$ molecule, so $\ell_c \approx 150$–$300\ \mathrm{nm}$ against $\ell_p = 14.5\ \mathrm{nm}$, and $\ell_c \gg \ell_p$ is the flexible regime Proposition 4.3 covers. There is also an independent check: the semiflexible modulus carries $k_BT$ in the denominator, so such a network softens on warming and §4's thermoelastic discriminator would classify it as energetic, not entropic. The bound and the measurement agree.

C10. This module withdrew three of its own claims. For each, name the check that caught it, and say which of those checks an automated gate could have performed.

Probes: whether the reader has absorbed the module's methodological lesson, which is the one most likely to transfer.

Solution

(i) §0's circular ladder was caught by asking where a table value came from — a provenance question. An automated gate can check that a declaration exists, and one does; it cannot check that the declaration is true, because that requires knowing the history of a number. (ii) The entropic-collagen attribution was caught by computing a quantity that had only been asserted. No gate could have done this: the claim was well-formed, internally consistent, and wrong, and only substituting numbers exposed it. (iii) §4's own over-correction — boxing a ceiling evaluated outside its proposition's hypothesis — was caught by a reviewer asking whether a boxed number is one the theorem produces. A gate could in principle check that a boxed result's inputs lie inside stated hypotheses, but no such gate exists here, and writing one would require the hypotheses to be machine-readable. The general lesson: gates catch broken content reliably and wrong content never.

Problems — derivational (D1–D10)

D1. Derive the rocksalt corollary $K = (n-1)\alpha_M|z_1z_2|C_e/(18r_0^4)$ from §2 Proposition 2.2, stating where the Madelung constant and the packing factor $\gamma_s = 2$ enter.

Probes: whether the cancellation of $\gamma_s$ in Proposition 2.2 is understood well enough to be undone for a special case.

Solution

Proposition 2.2 gives $K = n|U_{\text{latt}}|/(9V_f)$ with $|U_{\text{latt}}| = (\mathcal A/r_0)(1 - 1/n)$ from the equilibrium condition. For rocksalt, summing $\pm C_e/r$ over the lattice gives $\mathcal A = \alpha_M|z_1z_2|C_e$ with $\alpha_M = 1.7476$, and one formula unit occupies $V_f = 2r_0^3$, so $\gamma_s = 2$. Substituting, $$K = \frac{n}{9\cdot 2r_0^3}\cdot\frac{\alpha_M|z_1z_2|C_e}{r_0}\left(1-\frac1n\right) = \frac{(n-1)\alpha_M|z_1z_2|C_e}{18\,r_0^{4}}.$$ The Madelung constant enters through $\mathcal A$, i.e. through the lattice sum that replaces a single pair's Coulomb energy; the packing factor enters through $V_f$, and the $n$ cancels against the $(1-1/n)$ to leave $(n-1)$. Note that $\gamma_s$ cancelled in the general result and reappears here only because we chose to express the answer in $r_0$ rather than in $V_f$.

D2. Starting from §3 Proposition 3.3, derive the van 't Hoff relation $\mathrm d\ln K_{\text{eq}}/\mathrm d(1/T) = -\Delta H^{\circ}/R_g$, stating the approximation required.

Probes: whether Propositions 3.3 and 3.4 can be combined with $\Delta G = \Delta H - T\Delta S$.

Solution

From Proposition 3.4, $\ln K_{\text{eq}} = -\Delta G^{\circ}/(R_gT)$. Substituting the boxed $\Delta G^{\circ} = \Delta H^{\circ} - T\Delta S^{\circ}$, $$\ln K_{\text{eq}} = -\frac{\Delta H^{\circ}}{R_g}\cdot\frac1T + \frac{\Delta S^{\circ}}{R_g}.$$ Differentiating with respect to $1/T$ gives $-\Delta H^{\circ}/R_g$ directly, provided $\Delta H^{\circ}$ and $\Delta S^{\circ}$ are treated as temperature-independent — which requires the reaction's heat-capacity change $\Delta C_p$ to be negligible, the same approximation §3 used to move $\Delta G^{\circ\prime}$ from $298$ to $310\ \mathrm K$. The result is a plot recipe: $\ln K_{\text{eq}}$ against $1/T$ is a straight line of slope $-\Delta H^{\circ}/R_g$, which is how reaction enthalpies are measured without a calorimeter.

D3. Show that for a freely jointed chain the exact force–extension law $x/L_c = \coth\beta - 1/\beta$, with $\beta = fb/k_BT$, reduces to §4 Proposition 4.2 at small force, and find the leading correction.

Probes: whether the Gaussian result is recognised as a limit rather than a separate model.

Solution

Expand the Langevin function for small $\beta$: $\coth\beta = 1/\beta + \beta/3 - \beta^3/45 + \dots$, so $x/L_c = \beta/3 - \beta^3/45 + \dots$. To leading order $x/L_c = \beta/3 = fb/(3k_BT)$, and with $L_c = Nb$ this gives $f = 3k_BTx/(Nb^2)$ — exactly Proposition 4.2. The leading correction is the cubic term: inverting to the same order, $\beta = 3(x/L_c) + \tfrac{9}{5}(x/L_c)^3 + \dots$, so $$f = \frac{3k_BT}{Nb^2}x\left[1 + \tfrac35\left(\frac{x}{L_c}\right)^{2} + \dots\right].$$ The correction is $3\%$ at $x/L_c = 0.22$ and $15\%$ at $0.5$, which is consistent with §4's figure showing the Gaussian law good only to $x/L_c \approx 0.17$ against the worm-like chain (a stiffer comparison than the freely jointed one).

D4. Derive the Debye length for a solution containing a $2{:}1$ salt at concentration $c$, and show it is a factor $\sqrt3$ shorter than for a $1{:}1$ salt at the same $c$.

Probes: whether ionic strength's $z^2$ weighting is understood as the reason valence matters more than concentration.

Solution

A $2{:}1$ salt such as $\mathrm{CaCl_2}$ at concentration $c$ gives $c$ of $\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}$ and $2c$ of $\mathrm{Cl^-}$. By §5 Definition 5.2, $I = \tfrac12[c(2)^2 + 2c(1)^2] = \tfrac12[4c + 2c] = 3c$. A $1{:}1$ salt at the same $c$ gives $I = \tfrac12[c + c] = c$. Since $\lambda_D \propto I^{-1/2}$ by Proposition 5.1, the ratio of lengths is $\sqrt{c/3c} = 1/\sqrt3 = 0.577$. So a divalent salt screens three times as strongly per mole, which is why divalent counter-ions dominate the behaviour of a charged tissue even at low concentration, and why replacing sodium with calcium in a cartilage bath shrinks it.

D5. Derive the Donnan potential $\phi_D$ for the system of §5 Proposition 5.3, and evaluate it for $c_F = 0.2\ \mathrm M$ against $c_{\text{ext}} = 0.15\ \mathrm M$.

Probes: whether the proof's intermediate quantity can be recovered, and whether its sign is understood.

Solution

The proof of Proposition 5.3 used $c_+^{\text{in}} = c_{\text{ext}}e^{-F\phi_D/R_gT}$. Solving for the potential, $$\phi_D = -\frac{R_gT}{F}\ln\frac{c_+^{\text{in}}}{c_{\text{ext}}}.$$ With $c_+^{\text{in}} = 0.2803\ \mathrm M$ from the proposition and $c_{\text{ext}} = 0.15$, the ratio is $1.869$, so $\phi_D = -26.73\ \mathrm{mV}\times\ln(1.869) = -16.7\ \mathrm{mV}$. The interior is negative relative to the bath, which is what one expects: the immobile negative charge attracts cations and repels anions, and the resulting potential is the field that holds the excess cations in. Note this is a genuine measurable potential, not a bookkeeping device, and it is the reason a microelectrode pushed into cartilage reads a few tens of millivolts negative.

D6. Derive §5 Definition 5.4's buffer capacity $\beta = \ln 10\,C_{\text{tot}}K_a[\mathrm H^+]/(K_a+[\mathrm H^+])^2$, and show it is maximal at $\mathrm{pH} = \mathrm{p}K_a$.

Probes: whether a definition stated without proof can be reconstructed, and whether the maximum is found rather than asserted.

Solution

Let $C_{\text{tot}} = [\mathrm{HA}] + [\mathrm A^-]$ and $h = [\mathrm H^+]$. From $K_a = h[\mathrm A^-]/[\mathrm{HA}]$, the base fraction is $[\mathrm A^-] = C_{\text{tot}}K_a/(K_a + h)$. Adding strong base converts acid to base one for one, so $C_b = [\mathrm A^-]$ up to a constant, and $$\beta = \frac{\mathrm dC_b}{\mathrm{dpH}} = \frac{\mathrm d[\mathrm A^-]}{\mathrm dh}\cdot\frac{\mathrm dh}{\mathrm{dpH}}.$$ Now $\mathrm d[\mathrm A^-]/\mathrm dh = -C_{\text{tot}}K_a/(K_a+h)^2$, and since $\mathrm{pH} = -\log_{10}h$ we have $h = 10^{-\mathrm{pH}}$ and $\mathrm dh/\mathrm{dpH} = -h\ln 10$. Multiplying, $$\beta = \ln 10\;C_{\text{tot}}\frac{K_ah}{(K_a+h)^2}.$$ For the maximum, differentiate the fraction with respect to $h$: $\mathrm d/\mathrm dh[h/(K_a+h)^2] = (K_a - h)/(K_a+h)^3$, which vanishes at $h = K_a$, i.e. $\mathrm{pH} = \mathrm{p}K_a$, and is positive below and negative above, so it is a maximum. There $\beta = \ln 10\,C_{\text{tot}}/4 = 0.576\,C_{\text{tot}}$.

D7. Show that a Hill coefficient $n_H$ narrows the $10\%$-to-$90\%$ transition to $2\log_{10}(9)/n_H$ decades of ligand, and state what this implies for the largest $n_H$ that could be useful in a system whose signal spans two decades.

Probes: whether Definition 6.2's steepness claim can be derived, and whether its practical limit is seen.

Solution

Set $\theta = c^{n_H}/(K^{n_H}+c^{n_H})$. Then $\theta = 0.1$ requires $c^{n_H} = K^{n_H}/9$, i.e. $c = K\,9^{-1/n_H}$, and $\theta = 0.9$ requires $c = K\,9^{1/n_H}$. The ratio is $9^{2/n_H}$, whose base-ten logarithm is $2\log_{10}(9)/n_H = 1.908/n_H$ decades. For a signal spanning two decades, an $n_H$ much above about $2$ makes the transition narrower than the resolution of the signal itself: at $n_H = 10$ the switch flips within $0.19$ decades, so the system becomes an all-or-nothing threshold detector and loses any ability to grade its response. Steepness beyond the signal's own precision buys nothing and costs robustness, which is consistent with the observed $n_H \approx 3$ for thin filaments — enough to switch cleanly across two decades, not enough to make the switch hair-trigger.

D8. Derive the Michaelis–Menten result under the rapid-equilibrium assumption instead of the steady-state one, and say when the two differ.

Probes: whether the role of the steady-state approximation in Proposition 6.4 is understood.

Solution

Rapid equilibrium assumes binding equilibrates much faster than catalysis, $k_{-1} \gg k_2$, so $[\mathrm{ES}]$ is set by the dissociation constant alone: $[\mathrm E]c/[\mathrm{ES}] = K_d = k_{-1}/k_1$. Conservation then gives $[\mathrm{ES}] = [\mathrm E]_{\text{tot}}c/(K_d+c)$ and $v = k_2[\mathrm{ES}] = v_{\max}c/(K_d+c)$ — the same hyperbola, but with $K_d$ where Proposition 6.4 has $K_M = (k_{-1}+k_2)/k_1$. The two coincide exactly when $k_2 \ll k_{-1}$, and $K_M$ exceeds $K_d$ by $k_2/k_1$ otherwise. They differ most for a highly efficient enzyme, where $k_2$ approaches or exceeds $k_{-1}$: then $K_M$ is dominated by catalysis rather than by affinity, and reading it as a binding constant is wrong. This is why $K_M$ and $K_d$ must be kept distinct even though they occupy the same place in the formula.

D9. A network of Gaussian chains is stretched biaxially, $\lambda_1 = \lambda_2 = \lambda$, with $\lambda_3 = \lambda^{-2}$ by incompressibility. Derive the strain-energy density and the small-strain modulus, and compare with §4 Proposition 4.3's uniaxial result.

Probes: whether Proposition 4.3's derivation is understood well enough to be re-run under a different deformation.

Solution

Proposition 4.3's proof gives $W = \tfrac12nk_BT(\lambda_1^2+\lambda_2^2+\lambda_3^2-3)$ for any homogeneous deformation. Substituting the biaxial state, $$W = \tfrac12nk_BT\left(2\lambda^2 + \lambda^{-4} - 3\right).$$ The equibiaxial nominal stress is $\sigma = \tfrac12\,\mathrm dW/\mathrm d\lambda = nk_BT(\lambda - \lambda^{-5})$ per direction. Setting $\lambda = 1+\varepsilon$ and expanding, $\lambda - \lambda^{-5} \approx 6\varepsilon$, so $\sigma \approx 6nk_BT\varepsilon$. The biaxial modulus is therefore $6nk_BT$, twice the uniaxial $3nk_BT$ — which is the expected result for an incompressible isotropic solid, since the biaxial modulus is $E/(1-\nu)$ and $\nu = \tfrac12$ gives $2E$. The strain-energy function is the same; only the constraint differs, and the check that the two agree through $\nu = \tfrac12$ confirms the network is behaving as an isotropic incompressible continuum.

D10. Show that the charge separated across a membrane to hold a potential $V$ is $Q = CV$ and that the fraction of a spherical cell's internal potassium it represents scales as $1/r$. Comment on what this means for small cells.

Probes: whether §7's parts-per-million result is understood as a scaling rather than a coincidence.

Solution

$Q = CV$ is the definition of capacitance, with $C = (C/A)\cdot 4\pi r^2$ for a sphere. The ions separated are $n_{\text{sep}} = Q/e = (C/A)V4\pi r^2/e$. The internal potassium is $n_K = c_KN_A\cdot\tfrac43\pi r^3$. The ratio is $$\frac{n_{\text{sep}}}{n_K} = \frac{3(C/A)V}{e\,c_KN_A\,r} \;\propto\; \frac1r.$$ For $r = 25\ \mu\mathrm m$ this is $8.0$ parts per million (§7). The scaling says the effect grows as cells shrink, because charge lives on the surface and content in the volume. At $r = 0.25\ \mu\mathrm m$ — a small bacterium, or a synaptic vesicle — the fraction is $800$ parts per million, and at $r = 25\ \mathrm{nm}$ it approaches a per cent. So the licence §5 obtained for treating bulk concentrations as constant is a licence about large cells, and it must be re-checked for any small compartment. A synaptic vesicle's interior genuinely does change composition when its membrane potential changes.

K2 — integrate a fatiguing bout. Take a muscle starting at §3's resting composition ($[\mathrm{ATP}] = 5.5\ \mathrm{mM}$, $[\mathrm{ADP}] = 13\ \mu\mathrm M$, $[\mathrm{P_i}] = 2\ \mathrm{mM}$) and let phosphate accumulate at $0.22\ \mathrm{mM\,s^{-1}}$ for $60\ \mathrm s$, with free ADP tracking phosphate as $[\mathrm{ADP}] \propto [\mathrm{P_i}]^{1.35}$ through the creatine kinase equilibrium and $[\mathrm{ATP}]$ falling only from $5.5$ to $5.0\ \mathrm{mM}$. Integrate the trajectory and find the time at which the crossbridge force ceiling of §3 falls below $9\ \mathrm{pN}$.

Probes: whether §3's ceiling can be computed along a trajectory rather than at two endpoints, and whether the student notices the ceiling is a function of composition and therefore of time.

Solution

Euler integration at $\mathrm dt = 0.05\ \mathrm s$, recomputing $\Delta G = \Delta G^{\circ\prime} + R_gT\ln([\mathrm{ADP}][\mathrm{P_i}]/[\mathrm{ATP}])$ and then $\bar F_{\max} = |\Delta G|/(N_Ad)$ at every step:

  • phosphate rises $2.0 \to 15.2\ \mathrm{mM}$ over the bout;
  • the ceiling falls $10.38 \to 8.30\ \mathrm{pN}$;
  • it crosses $9\ \mathrm{pN}$ at $t = \mathbf{26.2\ s}$, when $[\mathrm{P_i}] = 7.8\ \mathrm{mM}$.

Three things the endpoints alone do not show. The fall is front-loaded: half the total drop happens in the first $16\ \mathrm s$, because $\Delta G$ depends on $\ln[\mathrm{P_i}]$ and the early rise from $2$ to $4\ \mathrm{mM}$ is a bigger logarithmic step than the later rise from $12$ to $15$. So a model that interpolates the ceiling linearly in time is wrong at exactly the moment fatigue is developing fastest. Second, the trajectory ends at $8.30\ \mathrm{pN}$, which is §1's original figure — §1's round $50\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ turns out to describe the muscle at the end of a hard minute, which is a more specific claim than §1 made. Third, the crossing time depends on the assumed phosphate rate, and that rate is the least well constrained input; halving it doubles the crossing time, so $26\ \mathrm s$ should be read as an order rather than a prediction.

  • a $10\%$ contribution at $I = 11.4\ \mathrm{mM}$;

Note the scaling that makes those three numbers one fact: $\ell_{\text{OSF}} \propto \lambda_D^2 \propto 1/I$, so a tenfold dilution is a tenfold increase in electrostatic stiffening, exactly. The threshold is therefore sharp in a useful way — there is no broad crossover to worry about.

The practical consequence is a warning about the literature. Single-molecule stretching experiments are frequently run in dilute buffer, often at or below $10\ \mathrm{mM}$ ionic strength, to keep the trap's optics and the molecule's handles behaving. At $11.4\ \mathrm{mM}$ the reported persistence length is already $10\%$ electrostatic, and at $1\ \mathrm{mM}$ it is half electrostatic — so the number reported is a property of the buffer as much as of the protein. That is a quantitative candidate explanation for why the reported $\ell_p$ for tropocollagen spans $10$ to $180\ \mathrm{nm}$: some of that spread is not disagreement about collagen but disagreement about salt.

K5 — optimise the buffer, and find the body has not. Take §5's three intracellular buffers — bicarbonate ($\mathrm{p}K_a\ 6.10$, $24\ \mathrm{mM}$), phosphate ($6.80$, $1\ \mathrm{mM}$) and protein histidine ($7.30$, $8\ \mathrm{mM}$). Probes: whether the student can find an optimum and then resist the conclusion that biology must be at it.

Solution

Summing Definition 5.4 over the three buffers and scanning pH in steps of $0.001$:

  • the maximum is at $\mathrm{pH} = \mathbf{6.18}$, where $\beta_{\text{tot}} = 15.27\ \mathrm{mM}$ per pH unit;
  • at $\mathrm{pH}\ 7.40$ the total is $7.43$, a $51\%$ penalty.

So the body holds its pH more than a unit away from where its own buffers work best, and gives up half its closed-system capacity to do it. The optimum sits near $6.18$ because bicarbonate has by far the largest concentration and its $\mathrm{p}K_a$ is $6.10$; the calculation is dominated by it.

Two things resolve the discrepancy, and the first is the important one. Buffer capacity is not the objective being optimised. pH $7.4$ is set by what enzymes, ion channels and protein charge states require; buffering is a constraint the body must satisfy at that pH, not a quantity it is free to maximise. Reading an optimum out of a model and inferring that biology should be at it is the error the problem is designed to elicit.

Second, the $51\%$ penalty is largely illusory, because the closed-system formula is the wrong one for bicarbonate. As §5 established, the lungs hold $\mathrm{CO_2}$ fixed and bicarbonate's real capacity at pH $7.4$ is $55\ \mathrm{mM}$ per pH unit — seven times the closed-system total computed here. The body recovers the loss not by moving its pH but by coupling one buffer to an organ. A student who computes the optimum and stops has found a real number and drawn a false conclusion from it.

K6 — fit an activation energy and propagate the error. You are given four measured rates, relative to $37\ ^\circ\mathrm C$, at $10$, $20$, $30$ and $37\ ^\circ\mathrm C$, each carrying a few per cent of experimental scatter. Fit $E_a$ by least squares on an Arrhenius plot ($\ln k$ against $1/T$), report the implied $Q_{10}$, and compute how far the fitted $E_a$ moves the predicted cooling factor at $25\ ^\circ\mathrm C$ relative to the true value of $70\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$.

Probes: whether an inverse problem is solved properly rather than by taking two points, and whether the sensitivity of the conclusion to the fit is quantified.

Solution

With scatter factors $1.06$, $0.95$, $1.04$, $0.97$ applied to the true rates, the least-squares slope on $\ln k$ against $1/T$ gives

  • $E_a = \mathbf{68.6\ kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ against a true $70.0$ — a $2.0\%$ error;
  • an implied $Q_{10} = 2.45$ against a true $2.5$;
  • a predicted cooling factor at $25\ ^\circ\mathrm C$ of $0.343$ against a true $0.335$ — a $2.2\%$ error.

The important finding is how insensitive the conclusion is. Individual rate measurements carrying up to $6\%$ scatter produce a cooling prediction good to $2\%$, because the fit uses four points spanning $27\ \mathrm K$ and the exponential's slope is well constrained by a wide lever arm. Contrast the two-point estimate a $Q_{10}$ usually is: taking only the $20$ and $30\ ^\circ\mathrm C$ points, with their $-5\%$ and $+4\%$ errors pulling in opposite directions, gives an $E_a$ in error by about $9\%$ and a cooling factor in error by $10\%$.

The lesson is that a $Q_{10}$ quoted from two temperatures is a much weaker number than one fitted across a range, and the difference matters here because §6's entire cold-muscle prediction rests on it. It is also why §6's provenance marker calls $Q_{10}$ a fitted steepness averaged over a mechanism rather than a barrier height.

K7 — integrate a twitch, with buffering. Solve the coupled system for free cytosolic calcium during one twitch: release from the reticulum as $J_{\text{in}} = (R/\tau)e^{-t/\tau}$ with $R = 135\ \mu\mathrm M$ and $\tau = 4\ \mathrm{ms}$, removal by a pump obeying Michaelis–Menten with $v_{\max} = 6\ \mathrm{mM\,s^{-1}}$ and $K_M = 0.5\ \mu\mathrm M$, and buffering by $70\ \mu\mathrm M$ of cooperative sites ($K = 1\ \mu\mathrm M$, $n_H = 3$). Reported values span roughly $3$ to $10\ \mathrm{mM\,s^{-1}}$ across fibre types, fast fibres being the faster; the choice here mainly sets how quickly the transient decays and barely moves the peak, which is release-dominated. The $K_M$ of $0.5\ \mu\mathrm M$ is better constrained and matters more, because it sits an order below the peak and therefore keeps the pump saturated through most of the transient.

Probes: whether an ODE with a state-dependent buffering factor can be set up and integrated, and whether the answer is checked against §1's ladder.

Solution

The buffering enters as a factor on the time derivative. Writing $\theta(c)$ for the Hill occupancy, total calcium is $c + S_{\text{tot}}\theta(c)$, so $$\frac{\mathrm dc}{\mathrm dt} = \frac{J_{\text{in}} - J_{\text{out}}}{1 + S_{\text{tot}}\,\mathrm d\theta/\mathrm dc},$$ and the denominator is the instantaneous buffering factor, which is itself a function of $c$. Integrating at $\mathrm dt = 20\ \mu\mathrm s$:

  • peak free calcium $\mathbf{10.08\ \mu M}$, reached a few milliseconds in;
  • peak occupancy $0.999$ — the filament is fully activated;
  • with the sites removed the peak would be $70.7\ \mu\mathrm M$, so buffering reduces it by a factor of $\mathbf{7.0}$.

The peak agrees with §1's ladder, which puts activated cytosolic calcium at $10\ \mu\mathrm M$ — and that agreement is a check on the model, not an input to it: the release amplitude was chosen and the peak came out. Two further readings. The buffering factor is not constant: it is large when $c$ is near $K$ and falls towards $1$ once the sites saturate, so a model using a fixed buffer ratio will get the rising phase and the falling phase wrong in opposite directions. And the sites reach $0.999$ occupancy while holding only about $70\ \mu\mathrm M$ of calcium out of the $135$ released, so more than half the released calcium is free or already pumped at the peak — the sites are saturated well before the transient is over, which is why the twitch has a plateau in activation but not in calcium.

K9 — invert the switch specification. A thin filament must be under $1\%$ activated at §1's resting calcium of $0.1\ \mu\mathrm M$ and over $95\%$ activated at $10\ \mu\mathrm M$, with half-activation at $1\ \mu\mathrm M$. Solution

Scanning $n_H$ upward until both conditions hold:

  • $n_H = \mathbf{2.00}$ is the smallest that works, giving $0.99\%$ at rest and $99.0\%$ activated;
  • for comparison, $n_H = 1$ gives $9.1\%$ at rest and $90.9\%$ activated — failing both;
  • the observed $n_H \approx 3$ gives $0.10\%$ and $99.9\%$.

So $n_H = 2$ is sufficient and the filament has about $3$. The margin buys tolerance rather than performance. At $n_H = 2$ the specification is met exactly at the stated calcium levels, so any drift — resting calcium creeping up, or peak calcium falling short in a fatigued fibre — breaks it. Note what this problem cannot conclude. Finding that $n_H = 2$ suffices does not explain why the filament has $3$: cooperativity arises from a physical mechanism — tropomyosin shifting along the filament and exposing neighbouring sites — and its strength is whatever that mechanism delivers, not a design parameter tuned to a specification. The calculation bounds what is needed; it says nothing about what is available. Reading it the other way is the same error as K5's.

K10 — the twitch energy budget, and where it goes. A muscle fibre bundle performs one twitch. Solution
  • crossbridge ATP: $1.5\times10^{17}$; pump ATP: $1.8\times10^{17}/2 = 0.9\times10^{17}$;
  • total $2.4\times10^{17}$ ATP, of which the pump is $37.5\%$;
  • energy at $52.74\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$: $21.0\ \mathrm{mJ}$;
  • work delivered: $1.5\times10^{17}\times5\ \mathrm{pN}\times10\ \mathrm{nm} = 7.50\ \mathrm{mJ}$;
  • efficiency $\mathbf{35.7\%}$.

Two readings. First, more than a third of the ATP a twitch costs is spent on putting calcium back, not on generating force — which is why a muscle held isometrically at length still consumes energy at a substantial rate, and why the reticulum pump is the largest single ATP consumer in a resting muscle. Any model that accounts only for crossbridge ATP understates the metabolic cost by more than half again.

Second, the $35.7\%$ is an efficiency of free-energy conversion at the crossbridge and pump level and is not the whole-muscle efficiency, which is measured nearer $20$–$25\%$. The difference is everything this budget omits: the cost of the action potential, basal metabolism, the pumps maintaining sodium and potassium gradients, and the losses in oxidative phosphorylation upstream of ATP. Quoting $35.7\%$ as "muscle efficiency" would be wrong, and the discrepancy is a useful check that the budget is incomplete by roughly the amount one would expect.

Note also the consistency check available: $5\ \mathrm{pN}$ over $10\ \mathrm{nm}$ is $50\ \mathrm{zJ}$, which against §1's $8.30\times10^{-20}\ \mathrm J$ per ATP is $60\%$ of one quantum — inside §3's ceiling, as it must be, and consistent with the $36$–$72\%$ range §1 quoted from optical-trap measurements.
