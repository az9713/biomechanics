0. Motivation — a femur that carries tonnes yet snaps in a fall

The same femur carries gait forces of two to three times body weight, about a million cycles a year, with a large margin in compression; yet in an elderly person a sideways fall from standing height can break it. Two puzzles sit here. First: how does a tube of bone carry such loads without yielding? Second: why does the safe everyday bone become fragile, and why does the fracture appear in bending/impact rather than in the much larger compression of standing?

Bone outlines: NIH BioArt upper-leg illustration is public domain (NIAID/NIH; Ryan Kissinger). The drawn outlines use assumed adult dimensions (neck-shaft angle ≈125°, head diameter ≈46 mm scaled). See anatomy_kit/ATTRIBUTIONS.md.

The models here sit on two rungs of the course's level ladder. §1 to §6 are Level 0 (scalar stress and strain estimates) built on Level 1 static loads: every load is a fixed force or moment, and every result is an elastic stress compared with a strength. §7 and §8 are a scalar version of Level 10, a mechanobiological adaptation law, with one state variable $Z$ in place of a tissue field. Nothing here is dynamic: the fall of §6 is an energy balance, not an impact simulation.

The answers are the content of this module. We take the muscle and joint forces derived in Module 1 as the loads, run them through beam theory to get internal stress, compare against bone's strength to get a safety factor, and then let the bone respond: the remodeling feedback that builds bone where strain is high and removes it where strain is low. That feedback, run forward in time, explains both training-induced strengthening and the bone loss of disuse and aging that sets up the fall-fracture in the first place.

1. Stress, strain, and the elastic law

SI units throughout: stress in pascals (Pa $=\mathrm{N\,m^{-2}}$; bone strengths run to $10^8\ \mathrm{Pa}=100\ \mathrm{MPa}$), strain dimensionless (often quoted in microstrain, $1\ \mu\varepsilon=10^{-6}$).

Definition 1.1 (stress). Stress $\sigma$ is internal force per unit area on a surface inside the material, $\sigma=F/A$ for a force $F$ normal to area $A$. Normal stress is tensile (pulling apart, $\sigma\gt 0$) or compressive ($\sigma\lt 0$); a force parallel to the surface gives shear stress $\tau$.
Definition 1.2 (strain). Strain $\varepsilon$ is fractional deformation: for a bar of length $L$ stretched by $\Delta L$, $\varepsilon=\Delta L/L$. It is dimensionless. Throughout the module $L$ means the length of whichever member is under discussion: the bar here, the twisted shaft of §4, the cantilever of K10.
Definition 1.3 (Young's modulus, Hooke's law). In the small-strain elastic regime, stress is proportional to strain: $$\sigma = E\,\varepsilon,\tag{1.1}$$ where $E$ (Pa) is Young's modulus, the material's axial stiffness. For cortical (dense, outer) bone $E\approx 17\ \mathrm{GPa}$ along the long axis; trabecular (spongy, internal) bone is far softer, $E\approx 0.1$–$2\ \mathrm{GPa}$. Bone is anisotropic ($E$ depends on direction) and heterogeneous ($E$ varies with position); we use an effective longitudinal value and revisit its origin in §5.

With (1.1) as the constitutive law, the rest of the module asks where in a loaded bone $\sigma$ is largest, and how that compares with the stress bone can bear.

2. Axial loading: why compression is almost free

Model the femoral midshaft as a hollow circular tube (the medullary canal, the marrow-filled cavity along the shaft, is the hole), outer radius $R$, inner radius $r$, cross-sectional area $A=\pi(R^2-r^2)$. Under a purely axial compressive force $F$ (Definition 1.1): $$\sigma_\text{axial}=\frac{F}{A},\qquad \varepsilon=\frac{\sigma}{E}.\tag{2.1}$$

Definition 2.1 (safety factor). For a load that produces a peak stress $\sigma_\max$ in a material of strength $\sigma_f$ (the stress at which it fails in that sense of loading), the safety factor is $n=\sigma_f/\sigma_\max$. A factor of $2$ means the load could double before failure.
Worked number. Take the single-leg-stance femoral load as $F=2.5\,Mg$ with $M=70$ kg the reference human of Module 1: $F\approx2.5\times70\times9.81\approx1717\ \mathrm N$. The factor $2.5$ is an assumption here (Appendix); it is larger than $1$ because the hip abductors must pull down on the pelvis to balance the trunk over one leg, and their pull adds to the weight on the femoral head. Module 3 derives the factor from that balance. With $R=14\ \mathrm{mm}$, $r=7\ \mathrm{mm}$, $A=4.62\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm m^2$, so $\sigma_\text{axial}=1717/4.62\times10^{-4}\approx3.7\ \mathrm{MPa}$. Against the compressive strength of cortical bone, $\sigma_c\approx170\ \mathrm{MPa}$ (Appendix), Definition 2.1 gives $n\approx45$. Pure compression is not what breaks bones: the bone could carry $\sigma_cA\approx78\ \mathrm{kN}$, about eight tonnes-force, before crushing.

3. Bending: the dominant danger, and why bone is hollow

Body weight rarely acts along the bone axis. The joint reaction force, of the kind Module 1 derives at the elbow (Proposition 5.2), here the assumed hip load $F$ of §2, enters the femoral head, which sits off the shaft axis at the end of the femoral neck (Fig. 1), so the shaft carries a bending moment $M=F\,e$ with $e$ the perpendicular offset of the force line from the shaft axis. This is not the joint torque of Module 1, which is the muscle moment about the joint; it is the contact force times its lever about the shaft. Muscle pulls and the inertial loads of running add to it. The module uses three activity levels for $M$, all listed in the Appendix as assumptions: the habitual gait value that sets the lab's $1250\ \mu\varepsilon$ ($M_\text{ref}=E\,\varepsilon\,Z=43$ N m, 21 MPa), a running-level $50$ MPa (K6), and a vigorous-activity $M=200$ N m ($99$ MPa) used below for the safety-factor comparison. Bending, not compression, produces the largest stresses. We derive the Euler–Bernoulli result.

Definition 3.1 (neutral axis, second moment of area, section modulus). Under pure bending each cross-section rotates rigidly about some line in its own plane on which the fibre strain is zero; that line is the neutral axis. (Theorem 3.2 shows it passes through the section's centroid.) Let $y$ be the signed distance of a point of the section from the neutral axis. The second moment of area about that axis is $I=\int_A y^2\,dA$, in $\mathrm m^4$; it measures how far the material sits from the axis. The section modulus is $Z=I/c$, where $c$ is the distance from the axis to the outermost fibre. Example: for the hollow circle of §2, $I=\tfrac{\pi}{4}(R^4-r^4)=2.83\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm m^4$ and $Z=I/R=2.02\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm m^3$ (D3 does the integral).
Theorem 3.2 (Euler–Bernoulli bending stress). For a beam in pure bending with moment $M$, the axial stress varies linearly across the section: $$\boxed{\;\sigma(y)=\frac{M\,y}{I},\qquad \sigma_\max=\frac{M\,c}{I}=\frac{M}{Z}.\;}\tag{3.1}$$
Assume plane cross-sections remain plane and rotate by curvature $\kappa$. Measure $y$ from an as-yet-unknown zero-strain line; a fibre at $y$ then has strain $\varepsilon(y)=\kappa y$ (geometry of rotation), and by Hooke's law (1.1), $\sigma(y)=E\kappa y$ — linear in $y$. The neutral axis is the centroidal axis. Pure bending applies a moment but no net axial force, so the stresses must sum to zero force: $$\int_A \sigma\,dA = E\kappa\int_A y\,dA = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; \int_A y\,dA = 0.$$ Since $\kappa\ne0$, the first moment of area about the zero-strain line vanishes — which is the definition of the centroid. So the neutral axis passes through the section's centroid; for the symmetric hollow circle that is its centre. With $y$ now measured from the centroid, the internal stresses must reproduce the applied moment about that axis: $$M=\int_A y\,\sigma(y)\,dA = E\kappa\int_A y^2\,dA = E\kappa\,I,$$ using Definition 3.1. Hence $E\kappa=M/I$, and substituting back into $\sigma=E\kappa y$ gives $\sigma(y)=My/I$. The extreme fibre at $y=c$ carries $\sigma_\max=Mc/I=M/Z$. ∎

For the hollow circular section, $I=\tfrac{\pi}{4}(R^4-r^4)$ and $c=R$. Because $I$ weights area by $y^2$, moving material outward (hollowing the centre, which sits near the neutral axis where it does little) buys bending strength per unit mass.

Proposition 3.3 (hollow beats solid). A hollow tube and a solid rod of the same cross-sectional area (hence same mass per length) have section moduli in ratio $Z_\text{hollow}/Z_\text{solid}\approx 1.44$ for the femoral proportions $R/r=2$. The hollow bone is $44\%$ stronger in bending at no extra mass. That is the engineering reason long bones are tubes.
Let the tube have outer radius $R$, inner radius $r$, and let the solid rod have radius $a$. Equal cross-sectional area (equal mass per length) requires $\pi a^2=\pi(R^2-r^2)$, i.e. $$a^2=R^2-r^2.$$ The section moduli $Z=I/c$ are, using $I=\tfrac{\pi}{4}(R^4-r^4)$, $c=R$ for the tube and $I=\tfrac{\pi}{4}a^4$, $c=a$ for the rod: $$Z_\text{hollow}=\frac{\pi}{4}\,\frac{R^4-r^4}{R},\qquad Z_\text{solid}=\frac{\pi}{4}\,a^3=\frac{\pi}{4}\,(R^2-r^2)^{3/2}.$$ Introduce the bore ratio $\beta=r/R$. Then $R^4-r^4=R^4(1-\beta^4)$ and $(R^2-r^2)^{3/2}=R^3(1-\beta^2)^{3/2}$, so $$\frac{Z_\text{hollow}}{Z_\text{solid}}=\frac{R^3(1-\beta^4)}{R^3(1-\beta^2)^{3/2}}=\frac{1-\beta^4}{(1-\beta^2)^{3/2}}=(1+\beta^2)\,(1-\beta^2)^{-1/2},$$ where the last step factors $1-\beta^4=(1+\beta^2)(1-\beta^2)$. For the femoral proportions $R/r=2$, $\beta=\tfrac12$: the ratio is $(1+\tfrac14)(1-\tfrac14)^{-1/2}=\tfrac{5}{4}\big/\sqrt{\tfrac34}=1.443$. The factor $(1+\beta^2)(1-\beta^2)^{-1/2}$ exceeds $1$ for every $0\lt\beta\lt1$ and grows without bound as $\beta\to1$: the thinner the wall (larger bore) at fixed area, the greater the bending advantage. ∎

Which strength sets the limit? Bending loads one face in tension and the opposite face in equal-magnitude compression (Fig. 2). But cortical bone is not equally strong in the two senses: it bears $\sim170\ \mathrm{MPa}$ in compression (§2) yet only $\sim120$–$150\ \mathrm{MPa}$ in tension — a strength anisotropy layered on top of the stiffness anisotropy of §5. The two faces reach $\sigma_\max$ together, so the tensile face is the one that yields first. Bending failure is governed by the tensile strength. That is why the yield line drawn below sits at the tensile value $\sim130\ \mathrm{MPa}$, not the larger compressive $170\ \mathrm{MPa}$, and why the safety factor is $1.3$ (tensile), not $1.7$ (compressive). This same tension-first rule reappears in torsion (§4), where the crack opens on the surface of maximum tensile stress.

4. Torsion: shear, the angle of twist, and the spiral fracture

A planted foot while the trunk rotates (a ski catching an edge, a tackle, a stumble with a fixed shoe) applies a torque $T$ about the bone's long axis. Bending stretched fibres along the axis; torsion instead shears them around it. The analysis runs parallel to bending, with shear strain replacing axial strain and the polar second moment of area replacing $I$.

Definition 4.1 (rate of twist, shear modulus). Let the shaft twist so each cross-section rotates rigidly about the axis, the rotation angle $\varphi(z)$ increasing along the axis $z$. The rate of twist is $\vartheta=d\varphi/dz$ (rad m$^{-1}$); the script form keeps it distinct from the plane angle $\theta$ of D7. A material fibre at radius $\rho$ from the axis is then sheared through angle $\gamma(\rho)=\rho\,\vartheta$ (the arc $\rho\,d\varphi$ over the length $dz$). Shear stress follows the elastic shear law $\tau=G\,\gamma$, where $G$ (Pa) is the shear modulus — the stiffness against shape change, the shear analogue of Young's modulus $E$. For cortical bone $G\approx 3.3\ \mathrm{GPa}$ (roughly $E/5$).
Proposition 4.1 (torsion formula and angle of twist). For a circular shaft under torque $T$, the shear stress grows linearly with radius, and the twist accumulates linearly with length: $$\boxed{\;\tau(\rho)=\frac{T\rho}{J},\qquad \tau_\max=\frac{T R}{J},\qquad \varphi=\frac{T L}{G\,J},\qquad J=\frac{\pi}{2}(R^4-r^4).\;}\tag{4.1}$$ $J=\int_A\rho^2\,dA$ is the polar second moment of area; for a circle $J=2I$ (it sums $I$ about two perpendicular diameters).
By Definition 4.1, $\tau(\rho)=G\gamma=G\rho\vartheta$ — linear in $\rho$, as bending stress was linear in $y$. The internal shear stresses must reproduce the applied torque about the axis (each element contributes force $\tau\,dA$ at moment arm $\rho$): $$T=\int_A \rho\,\tau(\rho)\,dA = G\vartheta\int_A \rho^2\,dA = G\vartheta\,J,$$ using $J=\int_A\rho^2\,dA$. For the hollow circle, $\int_A\rho^2\,dA=\int_r^R \rho^2\,(2\pi\rho\,d\rho)=\tfrac{\pi}{2}(R^4-r^4)$. Hence the rate of twist is $\vartheta=T/(GJ)$; substituting into $\tau=G\rho\vartheta$ gives $\tau(\rho)=T\rho/J$, maximal at the surface $\rho=R$. Integrating $\vartheta$ over a shaft of length $L$ (constant $T$, $J$) gives the total angle of twist $\varphi=\vartheta L=TL/(GJ)$. ∎
Lemma 4.2 (pure shear resolves into tension and compression at 45°). Consider a small square element of the bone surface whose faces carry shear stress $\tau$ and no normal stress (a state of pure shear). On the plane inclined at $45^\circ$ to the faces the stress is purely normal and equals $+\tau$ (tension); on the plane at $-45^\circ$ it is purely normal and equals $-\tau$ (compression). These are the element's principal stresses, the largest and smallest normal stresses over all plane orientations, $\sigma_1=+\tau$ and $\sigma_2=-\tau$.
Cut the square element along a diagonal and keep the triangular half whose two short faces, of length $a$ each (per unit depth), carry the shear $\tau$; the diagonal face has length $a\sqrt2$. On the two short faces the shear forces are $\tau a$ each, directed along the faces. By the equal-and-opposite pairing of shear on perpendicular faces, their vector sum is perpendicular to the diagonal and has magnitude $\tau a\sqrt2$. Equilibrium of the wedge requires the diagonal face to carry an equal and opposite force $\tau a\sqrt2$, also perpendicular to the diagonal, hence a normal stress of magnitude $\tau a\sqrt2/(a\sqrt2)=\tau$ and no shear. Whether it is tension or compression depends on which diagonal was cut: on one the two face forces pull the wedge apart (tension, $\sigma_1=+\tau$), on the other they push it together (compression, $\sigma_2=-\tau$). For a general orientation $\theta$ the same wedge balance gives a normal stress $\tau\sin2\theta$, which is extremal at $\theta=\pm45^\circ$ (D7 carries out this step), so no other plane carries a larger normal stress. ∎

Why the fracture spirals at 45°. The torsion formula gives a state of pure shear at the bone surface: on the axial and circumferential planes there is shear $\tau$ but no normal stress. Rotate that surface element by $45^\circ$, however, and the shear resolves into pure normal stresses — a principal tension $\sigma_1=+\tau$ along one $45^\circ$ diagonal and an equal principal compression $\sigma_2=-\tau$ along the other (Lemma 4.2). Because bone is brittle and — as §3 established — weakest in tension, the crack opens on the plane perpendicular to $\sigma_1$. That plane wraps the shaft as a $45^\circ$ helix: the classic spiral fracture (Fig. 5). A ductile metal, failing instead on the plane of maximum shear, breaks flat across the axis — bone does not.

Worked numbers. For the femoral tube ($R=14$ mm, $r=7$ mm), $J=\tfrac{\pi}{2}(R^4-r^4)=5.66\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm m^4$. An everyday pivoting torque $T\approx 30\ \mathrm{N\,m}$ gives $\tau_\max=TR/J\approx 7.4\ \mathrm{MPa}$ and, over a $0.40\ \mathrm m$ shaft, an angle of twist $\varphi=TL/(GJ)\approx 3.7^\circ$ — small and safe. A violent torsional load $T\approx 150\ \mathrm{N\,m}$ raises $\tau_\max$ to $\approx 37\ \mathrm{MPa}$. Setting $\tau_\max$ to bone's torsional (shear) strength $\sim 65\ \mathrm{MPa}$ gives a failure torque $T_f=\tau_f J/R\approx 260\ \mathrm{N\,m}$ for this idealized stout tube; femora with thinner cortices are assumed to fail nearer $140$ to $180\ \mathrm{N\,m}$ (Appendix), so a hard rotational fall of $150$ N m has a safety factor of $1.75$ against the idealized tube, and between $0.9$ and $1.2$ against that assumed real-femur range, so whether such a fall spirals the bone depends on the individual femur.

5. Where $E$ comes from: the mineral–collagen composite

The modulus $E\approx17\ \mathrm{GPa}$ used above is not fundamental — it emerges from bone's two-phase nanostructure. Type-I collagen fibrils (compliant, $E\sim1$ GPa, but tough) are reinforced by plate-shaped crystals of hydroxyapatite, $\mathrm{Ca_{10}(PO_4)_6(OH)_2}$ (stiff, $E\sim100$ GPa, but brittle). The result is a classic stiff-filler/ductile-matrix composite: its modulus lies between the two, far below the volume-fraction average because the stiff phase is discontinuous (quantified below and in K8), while its toughness exceeds either phase because cracks are deflected and bridged at interfaces.

Validating the 17 GPa. Two elementary bounds check that a mineral–collagen mix can plausibly give $E\approx17\ \mathrm{GPa}$. The Voigt (isostrain, phases in parallel) bound is the volume-weighted average $E_V=v_m E_m+(1-v_m)E_c$; the Reuss (isostress, phases in series) bound is the harmonic average $E_R=\big(v_m/E_m+(1-v_m)/E_c\big)^{-1}$. With mineral $E_m\approx100\ \mathrm{GPa}$, collagen $E_c\approx1\ \mathrm{GPa}$, and $v_m\approx0.5$, these bracket $E_R\approx2.0\ \mathrm{GPa}\le E\le E_V\approx50\ \mathrm{GPa}$. The measured $17\ \mathrm{GPa}$ sits inside — nearer the Reuss floor, because the stiff mineral is discontinuous platelets, not continuous fibres, so it cannot fully share load in parallel. (The bounds are derived in §10, D9; the volume fraction is swept in K8.)

Mineralization is the controlled deposition of hydroxyapatite within the collagen scaffold; too little (osteomalacia, rickets) lowers $E$ and strength, too much (some treatments) raises stiffness but embrittles. Anisotropy ($E$ largest along the loaded axis) is itself an adaptation — the structural echo of the feedback loop in §7.

6. Failure: yield, fatigue, fracture, and the falling body

Three failure routes matter for daily life.

(i) Monotonic overload. If $\sigma_\max$ from (3.1) exceeds the tensile yield strength $\sigma_Y=130$ MPa (Appendix; cortical bone has little ductility, so its ultimate strength is only slightly higher and this module does not distinguish them), the bone yields and then breaks. By Definition 2.1 the safety factor is $n=\sigma_Y/\sigma_\max$; §3 showed $n\sim1.3$ in vigorous bending — bone runs a deliberately lean margin, trading mass for mobility.

(ii) Stress concentration & fatigue. A geometric notch (a screw hole, a cortical defect) raises local stress by a factor $K_t$, $\sigma_\text{local}=K_t\,\sigma_\text{nominal}$. Repeated loading drives fatigue: microcracks nucleate and grow, captured empirically by an S–N (stress vs. cycles-to-failure) curve $N_f\propto(\Delta\sigma)^{-m}$ with $m\approx5$–$8$ for bone. Living bone normally repairs this microdamage (§7); a stress fracture is fatigue outpacing repair.

(iii) Fracture mechanics. A crack of length $a_\text{crack}$ fails when the stress intensity $K=Y_g\,\sigma\sqrt{\pi a_\text{crack}}$, with $Y_g$ a dimensionless factor of order $1$ set by the crack and specimen geometry, reaches the toughness $K_{Ic}\ (\sim2$–$6\ \mathrm{MPa\sqrt m}$ for bone), so the tolerable flaw size shrinks as load rises.

Routes (ii) and (iii) are stated here as named empirical laws with their parameters in the Appendix; the module builds neither. Route (i) and the fall model below are the ones it derives.

A fall-impact fracture model

Why a fall, not standing, breaks the hip: a sideways fall delivers a large force over a few centimetres of soft-tissue compression. Model the impact as energy capture by an effective spring (soft tissue plus pelvis) of stiffness $k_s$ (the subscript $s$ keeps it distinct from the remodeling gain $k$ of §7; the symbol $E$ stays reserved for Young's modulus). An effective mass $m_\text{eff}$, the part of the body whose momentum the hip must stop, falls a height $h$ before the hip meets the floor.

Proposition 6.1 (peak force of a spring-arrested fall). Let a mass $m_\text{eff}$ fall from rest a height $h$ onto a linear spring of stiffness $k_s$, with $g$ the gravitational acceleration. The peak spring force is $$F_\text{peak}=m_\text{eff}g\left(1+\sqrt{1+\frac{2k_sh}{m_\text{eff}g}}\right)\approx\boxed{\;\sqrt{2\,k_s\,m_\text{eff}\,g\,h}\;}\tag{6.1}$$ where the boxed form holds when the compression $x=F_\text{peak}/k_s$ is small compared with $h$.
Take the spring's rest point as the zero of height. The mass starts at height $h$ with no kinetic energy and stops, momentarily, at maximum compression $x$ below the rest point. Between those two states gravity does work $m_\text{eff}g(h+x)$ and the spring stores $\tfrac12k_sx^2$; no other force does work, so $\tfrac12k_sx^2=m_\text{eff}g(h+x)$. This quadratic in $x$ has the positive root $x=\dfrac{m_\text{eff}g}{k_s}\left(1+\sqrt{1+\dfrac{2k_sh}{m_\text{eff}g}}\right)$, and $F_\text{peak}=k_sx$ gives the exact form. When $x\ll h$ the term $m_\text{eff}gx$ is negligible against $m_\text{eff}gh$, the balance reduces to $\tfrac12k_sx^2=m_\text{eff}gh$, and $F_\text{peak}=k_s\sqrt{2m_\text{eff}gh/k_s}=\sqrt{2k_sm_\text{eff}gh}$, the boxed form. The neglected term is exactly the weight's work over the compression stroke. ∎
Worked number. With the Appendix values $m_\text{eff}=28\ \mathrm{kg}$, $h=0.5\ \mathrm m$, $k_s=5\times10^{4}\ \mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}$: the drop releases $m_\text{eff}gh\approx137\ \mathrm J$, the boxed form gives $F_\text{peak}\approx3.7\ \mathrm{kN}$ at a compression $x=0.074$ m, and the exact form gives $3.99\ \mathrm{kN}$ at $x=0.080$ m, because $m_\text{eff}gx$ adds 15 percent to the energy and $x/h=0.15$ is not small. This module keeps the boxed form and its $3.7$ kN as the working value; the 8 percent it undercounts is the first correction. Against the assumed strengths of the Appendix for the proximal femur (the upper end of the thigh bone: head, neck, and trochanters), $\sim4\ \mathrm{kN}$ healthy and $\sim2\ \mathrm{kN}$ osteoporotic, the healthy hip sits at the edge of its margin and the osteoporotic hip is well past it. The same fall is marginal or catastrophic depending on a strength the body itself sets over years (§7).

7. Adaptation: Wolff's law as a strain feedback loop

"Wolff's law" — bone adapts to the loads it carries — becomes quantitative as the mechanostat (Frost): bone tissue senses its own peak strain and adds or removes material to keep that strain in a target band. The mechanism is cellular:

  • Osteocytes, the bone cells sealed inside the mineralized matrix that act as its sensor network, detect strain-driven flow of interstitial fluid through the lacunar–canalicular system (the microscopic fluid channels housing the osteocytes and their processes) and convert it to chemical signals (mechanotransduction).
  • High strain suppresses sclerostin (a protein the osteocytes secrete that inhibits bone formation), releasing the brake on osteoblasts (the cells that lay down new bone) $\to$ bone formation. Low strain (disuse) raises the ratio of RANKL to OPG (two signalling proteins: RANKL recruits bone-removing cells, OPG blocks that recruitment), activating osteoclasts (the cells that dissolve bone) $\to$ bone resorption.
  • Between the two thresholds lies a "lazy zone" where formation and resorption balance and net mass is steady.
Model 7.1 (remodeling ODE). Let the section modulus $Z$ stand for "how much bone, well placed" (it sets bending strength via (3.1)). Peak strain under habitual moment $M$ is $\varepsilon(Z)=M/(Z\,E)$. The mechanostat is modelled (a stated constitutive law, not a derived theorem) as $$\frac{dZ}{dt}= \begin{cases} k\,Z\,\bigl(\varepsilon-\varepsilon_\text{hi}\bigr), & \varepsilon\gt \varepsilon_\text{hi}\ \text{(formation)}\\[2pt] 0, & \varepsilon_\text{lo}\le\varepsilon\le\varepsilon_\text{hi}\ \text{(lazy zone)}\\[2pt] k\,Z\,\bigl(\varepsilon-\varepsilon_\text{lo}\bigr), & \varepsilon\lt \varepsilon_\text{lo}\ \text{(resorption)} \end{cases}\tag{7.1}$$ The consequences of (7.1) are Lemma 7.2.
Lemma 7.2 (equilibrium and stability of the remodeling law). Under a constant habitual moment $M$, the law (7.1) has a continuum of rest states: every $Z$ with $M/(E\varepsilon_\text{hi})\le Z\le M/(E\varepsilon_\text{lo})$ is an equilibrium (the lazy zone). A bone starting below that interval grows to $Z_\text{eq}=M/(E\varepsilon_\text{hi})$; a bone starting above it shrinks to $Z_\text{eq}=M/(E\varepsilon_\text{lo})$. Both approaches are exponential, with time constants $1/(k\varepsilon_\text{hi})$ and $1/(k\varepsilon_\text{lo})$.
Substitute $\varepsilon=M/(ZE)$ into the formation branch: $\dfrac{dZ}{dt}=kZ\Big(\dfrac{M}{ZE}-\varepsilon_\text{hi}\Big)=k\Big(\dfrac{M}{E}-\varepsilon_\text{hi}Z\Big)$, which is linear in $Z$ with the single zero $Z_\text{eq}=M/(E\varepsilon_\text{hi})$. Write $\delta=Z-Z_\text{eq}$; then $\dot\delta=-k\varepsilon_\text{hi}\delta$, so $\delta(t)=\delta(0)\,e^{-k\varepsilon_\text{hi}t}$ decays with time constant $1/(k\varepsilon_\text{hi})$. The branch applies while $\varepsilon\gt\varepsilon_\text{hi}$, that is while $Z\lt Z_\text{eq}$, so a bone below the band grows toward $Z_\text{eq}$ and stops there, because inside the band the rate is zero. The resorption branch is the same computation with $\varepsilon_\text{lo}$ in place of $\varepsilon_\text{hi}$, approached from above. Inside the band $dZ/dt=0$ identically, so every such $Z$ is a rest state. With the Appendix values the time constants are $8.3$ and $12.5$ days. ∎

Two things follow. The bone chases a target strain, not a target size: double the load and the equilibrium doubles. And the destination is one edge of the band, never its middle, so where a bone rests depends on its history; a bone whose load only ever rose sits at $\varepsilon_\text{hi}$. The gain $k$ sets only the speed. K2 shows what this history dependence does to an aging prediction.

Sensitivity analysis

The equilibrium $Z_\text{eq}=M/(E\varepsilon^*)$ is inversely proportional to the strain setpoint $\varepsilon^*$ and proportional to load $M$: a $10\%$ rise in habitual load yields a $10\%$ stronger bone at steady state, while a rise in the setpoint driven by drugs or aging $\varepsilon^*$ (the bone "tolerating" more strain before building) lowers $Z_\text{eq}$ — a mechanism for age-related fragility independent of load. The gain $k$ sets only the speed of approach, not the destination: doubling $k$ halves the adaptation time but leaves $Z_\text{eq}$ unchanged.

Five diagnostic questions

  1. Why is the safety factor against compression ($\sim45$) so much larger than against bending ($\sim1.3$), and what does that imply about which loads bones are "designed" for?
    Answer

    Compression spreads a force over the whole area $A$ ($\sigma=F/A$), while bending concentrates it at the outer fibre through the much smaller section modulus $Z$ ($\sigma=M/Z$); a modest offset turns a small force into a large moment. Evolution tunes bone to the load that actually threatens it — bending and impact — so it runs a lean $\sim1.3$ margin there and a wastefully large one in pure compression, which it never approaches.

  2. Two bones have equal mass per length; one is a solid rod, one a tube. Explain via $I=\int y^2\,dA$ why the tube is stronger in bending but identical in pure axial loading.
    Answer

    Axial stress is $F/A$, and the two share the same $A$ (same mass), so they are identical in compression. Bending weights area by $y^2$: moving material outward to form the tube wall places it at larger $y$, raising $I$ and hence $Z=I/c$. Equal mass, more $I$ — the tube wins in bending only (Prop 3.3, factor $1.44$ at $R/r=2$).

  3. In the mechanostat, why does a bone reach a steady size even though the feedback is on strain? What sets the final size?
    Answer

    Adding bone lowers strain ($\varepsilon=M/(ZE)$ falls as $Z$ grows), so growth is self-limiting: it stops when strain re-enters the lazy zone. The steady size is $Z_\text{eq}=M/(E\varepsilon^*)$ — set by the load $M$ and the strain setpoint $\varepsilon^*$, not by any target size. The bone chases a target strain.

  4. Why does a fall fracture the hip, when standing — a far longer-lasting load — does not? Use (6.1) and the energy/force distinction.
    Answer

    Standing applies a static force ($\sim2.5\times$ body weight, well inside the margin). A fall converts gravitational energy $m_\text{eff}gh$ into force over a few centimetres of soft-tissue compression; by (6.1) the peak force $\sqrt{2k_sm_\text{eff}gh}\approx3.7$ kN is dynamic and momentary but exceeds the osteoporotic strength. For a single overload the duration is irrelevant; the peak force is what breaks bone (repeated loading is the separate fatigue route of §6).

  5. Astronauts lose bone in microgravity despite exercising. Which model parameter changes, and why does exercise only partly compensate?
    Answer

    Microgravity removes the gravitational component of habitual load $M$, dropping peak strain below $\varepsilon_\text{lo}$ into the resorption branch, so $Z_\text{eq}=M/(E\varepsilon_\text{lo})$ falls. Resistive exercise restores load only during brief sessions and along limited directions, so the peak daily strain that drives the mechanostat is only partly recovered — net loss persists.

Conceptual (C1–C10) ↑ contents

C1. Two femoral cross-sections carry the same mass per length: the real tube ($R/r=2$) and a solid rod of equal area. Rank them for (a) axial stiffness, (b) bending strength, and (c) resistance to local buckling of the wall, that is, the sideways collapse of a thin compressed wall before the material itself yields (not treated in this module; reason from the geometry alone).

Probes: the $A$-vs-$I$ split — why the same mass gives equal axial but unequal bending performance, and the cost of going hollow.

Show solution

(a) Axial: identical. Axial stiffness is $EA$ and both share the same area $A$, so $\sigma=F/A$ and $\varepsilon$ are the same. (b) Bending: the tube wins. Bending weights area by $y^2$ (Def 3.1), and the tube's material sits at larger $y$, so $I$ and $Z=I/c$ are larger — by the factor $1.44$ of Prop 3.3. (c) Buckling: the solid rod wins. The tube's advantage comes from a thin wall, and thin walls buckle locally (a mode the solid rod lacks). Hollowing therefore trades a little buckling margin for bending efficiency; real cortices keep a wall thick enough that material yielding, not wall buckling, governs.

C2. On the bent femoral section, where is the bending stress largest and where is it zero? Explain why drilling out the central medullary bore costs almost none of the bending strength.

Probes: the linear stress field $\sigma=My/I$ and the $y^2$ weighting that makes central material nearly free to remove.

Show solution

Stress varies linearly with distance from the neutral (centroidal) axis, $\sigma(y)=My/I$: zero at the axis, maximal $\pm Mc/I$ at the outer fibres. Material near the centre sits at small $y$ and contributes to $I=\int y^2\,dA$ only in proportion to $y^2\to0$. Removing the central bore therefore subtracts almost nothing from $I$ (hence from $Z$) while removing real mass — and creating room for marrow. That is why long bones are tubes.

C3. A sideways fall bends the femoral neck (the short angled segment between the head and the shaft, Fig. 1), loading one cortex in tension and the opposite in compression to equal magnitude $\sigma_\max$. Given compressive strength $\sim170$ MPa and tensile $\sim130$ MPa, on which face does the crack begin, and why?

Probes: strength anisotropy — why "tension governs bending" even though the two faces see the same stress magnitude.

Show solution

The crack starts on the tensile face. Both faces reach the same $\sigma_\max$ simultaneously, but they are compared against different limits: the tensile face against $\sim130$ MPa, the compressive against $\sim170$ MPa. The tensile face reaches its (smaller) limit first, so failure initiates there and the crack runs across the section. This is why §3 draws the yield line at the tensile value and why the honest bending safety factor uses $\sim130$ MPa, not $170$.

C4. A planted ski twists the tibia (the shin bone) to failure. Explain why the fracture spirals along a $45^\circ$ helix, whereas a steel rod under the same torsion would shear flat across its axis.

Probes: pure shear $\to$ principal stresses at $45^\circ$, and the brittle-vs-ductile failure-plane selection.

Show solution

Torsion puts the surface in pure shear $\tau$ (Prop 4.1). On planes at $45^\circ$ this shear resolves into a principal tension $\sigma_1=+\tau$ and compression $\sigma_2=-\tau$ (Lemma 4.2). Bone is brittle and weakest in tension, so it cracks on the plane perpendicular to $\sigma_1$ — a $45^\circ$ helix, the spiral fracture. Ductile steel instead yields on the plane of maximum shear (the transverse plane), giving a flat break. The material's failure mode, not the loading, chooses the plane.

C5. A healed surgical screw hole leaves a cortex (the dense outer wall of the bone) whose nominal stress is only $40$ MPa (safety factor $\sim3$), yet it can still initiate a fracture. Explain, defining the stress-concentration factor $K_t$, and give the fatigue consequence.

Probes: geometric stress concentration $\sigma_\text{local}=K_t\sigma_\text{nom}$ and its role in fatigue crack nucleation.

Show solution

A hole forces the stress trajectories to crowd around it, raising the local stress to $\sigma_\text{local}=K_t\,\sigma_\text{nom}$ with $K_t\approx2$–$3$ for a circular hole. At $\sigma_\text{nom}=40$ MPa the wall sees $\sim100$ MPa locally — a safety factor that collapses from $\sim3$ to $\sim1.3$. Under cyclic load, this hot-spot is where fatigue microcracks nucleate first (the $\Delta\sigma^{-m}$ law of §6ii), so activity must stay limited until remodeling refills the defect.

C6. A runner who doubles weekly mileage abruptly develops a tibial stress fracture; the same total mileage reached gradually instead strengthens the bone. Explain why the rate of load increase, not the total, governs the outcome.

Probes: the competition of two timescales — per-cycle fatigue damage vs. weeks-slow remodeling repair/adaptation.

Show solution

Two processes race. Microdamage accumulates every loading cycle (fast, load-proportional); remodeling both repairs damage and adds bone, but over weeks to months (slow). An abrupt jump makes the damage rate exceed the repair rate, so cracks outgrow repair and coalesce into a stress fracture. A gradual ramp keeps peak strain inside the adaptive window, letting remodeling raise $Z$ (and lower strain) ahead of the damage — the same total load now strengthens. Rate is the control variable; the threshold rate is computed in K4.

C7. Two people load their femora to the same habitual peak strain, but one has twice the body mass (twice the bending moment $M$). Predict their steady section moduli. Then predict the effect of a drug that raises the strain setpoint $\varepsilon^*$.

Probes: $Z_\text{eq}=M/(E\varepsilon^*)$ — the bone chases a target strain, so size tracks load and inversely tracks the setpoint.

Show solution

At equilibrium $Z_\text{eq}=M/(E\varepsilon^*)$. Same $\varepsilon^*$, double $M$ $\Rightarrow$ double $Z$: the heavier person carries twice the bone at the same strain. A drug that raises $\varepsilon^*$ (bone tolerates more strain before building) lowers $Z_\text{eq}$ at fixed load — thinner, more fragile bone independent of activity level. Conversely an anti-resorptive (a drug that slows osteoclast bone removal) that lowers $\varepsilon^*$ raises $Z_\text{eq}$; K2 compares this against simply loading more.

C8. Hydroxyapatite alone is stiff but brittle; collagen alone is compliant and weak. Bone is both stiff and tough. Explain how the two-phase nanostructure achieves a toughness exceeding either phase — and why stiffness, unlike toughness, cannot exceed a simple mixture bound.

Probes: composite mechanics — stiffness is bounded by rule-of-mixtures (Voigt/Reuss) but toughness is an interface/architecture property.

Show solution

Stiff mineral platelets carry load and set $E$; the compliant collagen matrix and the mineral–collagen interfaces deflect, bridge, and blunt advancing cracks, dissipating energy over a large process zone. Toughness thus arises from architecture (crack-tip shielding), so it can beat both phases. Stiffness cannot: it is a weighted average of the phase moduli and is rigorously bracketed by the Voigt (parallel) and Reuss (series) bounds (derived in D9), which $17$ GPa obeys.

C9. Explain why an elephant cannot have leg bones that are a gazelle's scaled up geometrically. Identify which stress grows with body size and by what power.

Probes: Galileo's square–cube scaling — bending stress rises as $\propto L$ under geometric similarity, forcing disproportionate stoutness.

Show solution

Under geometric similarity (every length $\times\lambda$): body weight $\propto$ volume $\propto\lambda^3$; the bending moment that weight applies over a limb $\propto\lambda^3\cdot\lambda=\lambda^4$; the section modulus $Z\propto d^3\propto\lambda^3$. Hence peak bending stress $\sigma\propto M/Z\propto\lambda^4/\lambda^3=\lambda$ — it grows linearly with size. A gazelle scaled to elephant size would exceed bone strength, so large animals must depart from geometric similarity toward stouter bones; K6 compares the two rules that do this (elastic similarity, $d\propto L^{3/2}$, and stress similarity, $d\propto L^2$) and shows what each buys.

C10. Bone's modulus and strength are largest along its habitual loading axis, and the trabeculae inside the femoral head fan out along the joint-load lines. Explain why such directional material is efficient, and how it is a product of the §7 feedback.

Probes: anisotropy as optimized material placement — the trabecular architecture as a frozen map of principal-stress directions.

Show solution

Aligning stiff material with the dominant stress direction maximizes strength and stiffness per unit mass: material placed off-axis carries little of the load. The mechanostat senses strain and deposits (and orients) tissue where strain is high, so over time the trabeculae grow along the trajectories of principal stress. The fan of struts in the femoral head is therefore the frozen record of the habitual joint-load directions — Wolff's law made visible.

Derivational (D1–D10) ↑ contents

D1. For a rectangular cross-section of width $b$ and height $h_\text{sec}$ (the subscript keeps it distinct from the fall height $h$ of §6), derive the second moment of area $I$ about the centroidal axis by integrating $I=\int y^2\,dA$, and give the peak bending stress $\sigma_\max$ in terms of $M,b,h_\text{sec}$.

Probes: the definition $I=\int y^2\,dA$ made concrete, and the section modulus $Z=I/c$.

Show solution

Take a horizontal strip at height $y$, thickness $dy$: $dA=b\,dy$. Then $$I=\int_{-h_\text{sec}/2}^{h_\text{sec}/2} y^2\,b\,dy = b\Big[\tfrac{y^3}{3}\Big]_{-h_\text{sec}/2}^{h_\text{sec}/2} = b\cdot\frac{2}{3}\Big(\frac{h_\text{sec}}{2}\Big)^3=\frac{bh_\text{sec}^3}{12}.$$ The outer fibre is at $c=h_\text{sec}/2$, so $Z=I/c=\dfrac{bh_\text{sec}^3/12}{h_\text{sec}/2}=\dfrac{bh_\text{sec}^2}{6}$ and $\sigma_\max=\dfrac{M}{Z}=\dfrac{6M}{bh_\text{sec}^2}$. The $h_\text{sec}^2$ in $Z$ is why beam depth buys strength faster than width.

D2. Prove that under pure bending (a moment with no net axial force) the neutral axis passes through the centroid of the cross-section.

Probes: the zero-axial-force condition $\int\sigma\,dA=0$ and its identification with the first moment of area.

Show solution

Plane sections rotate, so measured from the zero-strain line the stress is $\sigma(y)=E\kappa y$ (linear). Pure bending has no resultant axial force: $$\int_A \sigma\,dA = E\kappa\int_A y\,dA = 0.$$ Since $\kappa\ne0$, $\int_A y\,dA=0$. But $\int_A y\,dA=0$ is exactly the statement that the reference line passes through the centroid (the first moment of area vanishes there). Hence the neutral axis is the centroidal axis. $\;\blacksquare$

D3. Derive the polar second moment of area $J=\int_A\rho^2\,dA$ for a hollow circular section (outer $R$, inner $r$), and hence $I$, using a ring element.

Probes: integrating with the annular element $dA=2\pi\rho\,d\rho$, and the identity $J=2I$ for a circle.

Show solution

Use a thin ring of radius $\rho$, width $d\rho$: $dA=2\pi\rho\,d\rho$. Then $$J=\int_r^R \rho^2\,(2\pi\rho\,d\rho)=2\pi\Big[\tfrac{\rho^4}{4}\Big]_r^R=\frac{\pi}{2}(R^4-r^4).$$ By symmetry $I_x=I_y$ for a circle, and $J=I_x+I_y=2I$ (since $\rho^2=x^2+y^2$), so $I=J/2=\dfrac{\pi}{4}(R^4-r^4)$ — the value used throughout §3–§4.

D4. A hollow tube (outer $R$, inner $r$, bore ratio $\beta=r/R$) and a solid rod of equal cross-sectional area are compared in bending. Derive the section-modulus ratio and evaluate it at $\beta=\tfrac12$.

Probes: the equal-mass constraint $a^2=R^2-r^2$ and the closed form $(1+\beta^2)/\sqrt{1-\beta^2}$ behind Prop 3.3.

Show solution

Equal area gives $\pi a^2=\pi(R^2-r^2)$, so $a^2=R^2-r^2$. With $Z_\text{hollow}=\tfrac{\pi}{4}(R^4-r^4)/R$ and $Z_\text{solid}=\tfrac{\pi}{4}a^3=\tfrac{\pi}{4}(R^2-r^2)^{3/2}$, $$\frac{Z_\text{hollow}}{Z_\text{solid}}=\frac{R^4-r^4}{R\,(R^2-r^2)^{3/2}}=\frac{1-\beta^4}{(1-\beta^2)^{3/2}}=\frac{(1+\beta^2)(1-\beta^2)}{(1-\beta^2)^{3/2}}=\frac{1+\beta^2}{\sqrt{1-\beta^2}}.$$ At $\beta=\tfrac12$: $\dfrac{1+\tfrac14}{\sqrt{1-\tfrac14}}=\dfrac{5/4}{\sqrt{3}/2}=1.443$. It exceeds $1$ for all $0\lt\beta\lt1$ and diverges as $\beta\to1$ — thinner walls, more advantage (until buckling intervenes, C1).

D5. From $\sigma=My/I$ and Hooke's law derive the curvature–moment relation $\kappa=M/(EI)$. Then, for two geometrically similar bones scaled by $\lambda$, show how $EI$, the self-weight bending moment, the curvature, and the peak stress each scale — and interpret.

Probes: bending stiffness $EI$ and dimensional scaling — the bridge from beam theory to allometry (C9, K6).

Show solution

Plane-section geometry gives $\sigma=E\kappa y$; equating to $\sigma=My/I$ yields $\boxed{\kappa=M/(EI)}$, with $EI$ the bending stiffness. Scale every length by $\lambda$: $I\propto d^4\propto\lambda^4$, so $EI\propto\lambda^4$; the self-weight $\propto$ volume $\propto\lambda^3$ acts over a moment arm $\propto\lambda$, so $M\propto\lambda^4$. Hence the curvature $\kappa=M/(EI)\propto\lambda^0$ is the same for all sizes, but the shape is not: a beam of length $L$ at curvature $\kappa$ sags by $\delta\sim\kappa L^2$, so the relative sag $\delta/L\sim\kappa L\propto\lambda$ grows with size. The peak stress $\sigma_\max=Mc/I\propto\lambda^4\cdot\lambda/\lambda^4=\lambda$ grows the same way. Both the relative deflection and the stress of a geometrically scaled bone rise linearly with size; a bone four times longer (the human-to-elephant factor is $\lambda=(5000/70)^{1/3}=4.1$) would sag four times more for its length and carry four times the stress. Two other rules remove one problem each: elastic similarity ($d\propto L^{3/2}$) keeps $\delta/L$ fixed, and stress similarity ($d\propto L^2$) keeps $\sigma$ fixed. K6 works out what each rule does to stress across the mammalian size range.

D6. For a circular shaft, use $J=2I$ to compare torsion and bending. Show that under an applied moment of equal magnitude the surface shear stress is half the bending stress, while the angular compliance (twist per unit moment vs. curvature per unit moment) is larger by $E/2G$. Interpret for bone ($E/G\approx5$).

Probes: the parallel structure of $\sigma=My/I$ and $\tau=T\rho/J$, and the role of $G$ vs $E$.

Show solution

Surface stresses at equal moment magnitude $M=T$: $\sigma_\max=MR/I$, $\tau_\max=TR/J=TR/(2I)=\tfrac12\sigma_\max$ — torsion generates half the surface stress of bending, because a circle's material is twice as effective about the polar axis. Compliances: curvature $\kappa=M/(EI)$, rate of twist $\vartheta=T/(GJ)=T/(2GI)$; at $M=T$ their ratio is $\vartheta/\kappa=EI/(2GI)=E/(2G)$. For bone $E/G\approx5$, so $\vartheta/\kappa\approx2.5$: a bone twists far more compliantly than it bends, which is why torsional loads produce large angular deflections and the tension-driven spiral failure of §4.

D7. The wedge balance of Lemma 4.2, carried out for a general cut angle $\theta$ and a general plane stress state, gives the transformation formula $\sigma_n(\theta)=\tfrac{\sigma_x+\sigma_y}{2}+\tfrac{\sigma_x-\sigma_y}{2}\cos2\theta+\tau_{xy}\sin2\theta$, prove that a state of pure shear ($\sigma_x=\sigma_y=0,\ \tau_{xy}=\tau$) has principal stresses $\pm\tau$ oriented at $\pm45^\circ$.

Probes: the wedge balance of Lemma 4.2 underlying the $45^\circ$ spiral fracture (§4, C4).

Show solution

With $\sigma_x=\sigma_y=0$ and $\tau_{xy}=\tau$, the transformation reduces to $\sigma_n(\theta)=\tau\sin2\theta$. This is extremal where $\dfrac{d\sigma_n}{d\theta}=2\tau\cos2\theta=0$, i.e. $2\theta=\pm90^\circ\Rightarrow\theta=\pm45^\circ$. There $\sigma_n(45^\circ)=+\tau$ (principal tension $\sigma_1$) and $\sigma_n(-45^\circ)=-\tau$ (principal compression $\sigma_2$). The principal directions are the $45^\circ$ diagonals — the tension diagonal is where a brittle, tension-weak material cracks, giving the helix. $\;\blacksquare$

D8. Reproduce Lemma 7.2 for the formation branch $dZ/dt=kZ(\varepsilon-\varepsilon_\text{hi})$ with $\varepsilon=M/(ZE)$: find the equilibrium, prove it is stable by linearizing, and identify the time constant.

Probes: fixed-point analysis of the remodeling ODE — why the bone converges to a strain-set size (§7).

Show solution

Substitute $\varepsilon=M/(ZE)$: $\dfrac{dZ}{dt}=kZ\Big(\dfrac{M}{ZE}-\varepsilon_\text{hi}\Big)=k\Big(\dfrac{M}{E}-\varepsilon_\text{hi}Z\Big)$ — linear in $Z$. Setting it to zero gives $Z_\text{eq}=\dfrac{M}{E\varepsilon_\text{hi}}$. Linearizing, $\dfrac{d}{dZ}\Big[k\big(\tfrac{M}{E}-\varepsilon_\text{hi}Z\big)\Big]=-k\varepsilon_\text{hi}\lt0$, so a perturbation $\delta=Z-Z_\text{eq}$ obeys $\dot\delta=-k\varepsilon_\text{hi}\,\delta$ and decays: the equilibrium is stable, with time constant $\tau_Z=1/(k\varepsilon_\text{hi})=8.3$ days (the subscript keeps it apart from the shear stress $\tau$ of §4). The gain $k$ sets only the speed; $M$ and $\varepsilon_\text{hi}$ set the destination.

D9. Derive the Voigt (isostrain) and Reuss (isostress) bounds for the modulus of a two-phase composite with mineral fraction $v_m$, and evaluate the bracket for bone ($E_m=100$, $E_c=1$ GPa, $v_m=0.5$).

Probes: the parallel/series models that bracket $E$ and validate the $17$ GPa of §5.

Show solution

Voigt (parallel, equal strain $\varepsilon$): each phase carries $\sigma_i=E_i\varepsilon$; the area-averaged stress is $\sigma=v_mE_m\varepsilon+(1-v_m)E_c\varepsilon$, so $E_V=v_mE_m+(1-v_m)E_c$. Reuss (series, equal stress $\sigma$): strains add in proportion, $\varepsilon=v_m\sigma/E_m+(1-v_m)\sigma/E_c$, so $\dfrac1{E_R}=\dfrac{v_m}{E_m}+\dfrac{1-v_m}{E_c}$. Any real microstructure lies between: $E_R\le E\le E_V$. At $v_m=0.5$: $E_V=50.5$ GPa, $E_R=2.0$ GPa. The measured $17$ GPa sits inside, near the Reuss floor — consistent with discontinuous mineral platelets rather than continuous fibres. (Swept in K8.)

D10. Model a fall as an effective mass $m_\text{eff}$ dropping height $h$ onto a linear soft-tissue spring of stiffness $k_s$. Derive the peak impact force $F_\text{peak}=\sqrt{2k_sm_\text{eff}gh}$, and show it dwarfs the static weight.

Probes: energy $\to$ force conversion and why an impulsive load, not a large mass, breaks the hip (§6, K7).

Show solution

Gravitational energy released in the drop equals the spring's stored energy at peak compression $x$: $m_\text{eff}gh=\tfrac12k_sx^2$, so $x=\sqrt{2m_\text{eff}gh/k_s}$. The peak force is $F_\text{peak}=k_sx=\boxed{\sqrt{2k_sm_\text{eff}gh}}$ (the small-compression form of Proposition 6.1; the exact balance adds the weight's work $m_\text{eff}gx$ and raises the force by 8 percent at these values). Its ratio to the static weight is $\dfrac{F_\text{peak}}{m_\text{eff}g}=\sqrt{\dfrac{2k_sh}{m_\text{eff}g}}$; with $k_s=5\times10^4$, $h=0.5$, $m_\text{eff}=28$ this is $\approx13$ — the fall delivers $\sim13\times$ the body's static weight. Note $F_\text{peak}\propto\sqrt{k_s}$: a softer landing (hip pad, flexed muscles) lowers the force, the design lever exploited in K7.

K4 (critical ramp rate from the damage-repair race). Define fatigue damage by Miner's rule: each loading cycle at stress $\sigma$ adds $1/N_f(\sigma)$ to a damage variable $D$, where $N_f(\sigma)=N_\text{hab}(\sigma/\sigma_\text{hab})^{-m}$ is the S-N law of §6 with $m=6$, and failure occurs at $D=1$. import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import brentq
E, Z0, k = 17e9, 2.02e-6, 80.0
eps_lo, eps_hi = 1000e-6, 1500e-6
M_ref = 1250e-6*Z0*E
m_exp, n0, tau_r, D_hab = 6.0, 5000.0, 14.0, 0.25
sig_hab = 1250e-6*E
Nf_hab = n0*tau_r/D_hab               # Basquin anchor: habitual D = 0.25


def Nf(sig):
    return Nf_hab*(sig/sig_hab)**(-m_exp)


def rate(Z, M):
    e = M/(Z*E)
    if e > eps_hi:
        return k*Z*(e - eps_hi)
    if e < eps_lo:
        return k*Z*(e - eps_lo)
    return 0.0


def max_damage(ramp_days, load=1.6, days=400, dt=0.25):
    Z, D, Dmax = Z0, D_hab, D_hab
    for i in range(int(days/dt) + 1):
        frac = min(i*dt/ramp_days, 1.0) if ramp_days > 0 else 1.0
        M = M_ref*(1 + (load - 1)*frac)
        D += (n0/Nf(M/Z) - D/tau_r)*dt   # Miner increment minus repair
        Dmax = max(Dmax, D)
        Z = max(Z + rate(Z, M)*dt, 1e-9)
    return Dmax

Write the bone's length as $L$ and diameter as $d$, with body mass $M_b\propto Ld^2$.     d = np.diff(x)
    return np.concatenate([[0], np.cumsum((f[:-1] + f[1:])/2*d)])
