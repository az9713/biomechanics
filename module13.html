0. Motivation - the day as applied mechanics

An ordinary morning is a sequence of mechanical problems the body solves without a thought. You rise from bed and from a chair; you carry a full kettle; you climb the stairs and come back down; you lift a box, turn a corner, catch your balance on a rug that slips, open a stubborn jar, push a heavy door, and walk up the hill to the bus. Each of these is a movement this course has already given the tools to analyze - and the point of this module is to use them, turning the machinery of Modules 1-12 on the concrete acts of daily life (Fig. 1) and reading off the forces, torques, powers, and stresses the body actually meets.

Doing this pays three ways. First, it makes the abstractions concrete: the low-back torque of Module 1 becomes the spinal compression that decides whether lifting a box is safe; the inverted pendulum of Modules 7-8 becomes the chair rise an aging parent finds hard; the friction cone of Module 11 becomes the grip that opens a jar. Second, it reveals margins: how close each everyday act runs to the limits of strength, balance, or tissue tolerance - and therefore why a small loss of capacity turns an easy task into an impossible or dangerous one. Third, it names failure modes: for each case, exactly which parameter (strength, range of motion, reaction time, balance margin, tissue strength) has to fall, and by how much, before the movement fails - which is the mechanical basis for aging, injury, and the training that pushes the margins back out.

This module is deliberately not a new theory but a disciplined application. Each case runs the same short pipeline - idealize, draw the free-body diagram, write the governing equation, put in human numbers, simulate, and find the failure mode - a template we set up once in Section 1 and then apply to nine everyday movements across six sections. The recurring lesson is how often ordinary tasks run near a limit: lifting a modest box loads the spine past its recommended tolerance, rising from a low chair demands nearly all an older adult's knee strength, and catching a stumble spends most of the balance budget of Module 10. Daily life is not comfortably inside the body's envelope; daily life runs near the edge of the envelope, which is why the envelope shrinking with age or injury is felt as a sudden loss rather than a gradual one.

The thesis of this module. Every ordinary movement is a solvable mechanics problem, and solving it exposes how near the body runs to its limits. A single template - idealize to links and joints, balance moments or momenta, estimate with human numbers, simulate, and locate the failure mode - turns rising, climbing, lifting, turning, gripping, and walking a slope into quantitative statements about joint torque, spinal load, balance margin, and metabolic power. The margins are thin, and reading them is the mechanics of independence: what keeps a person able to rise, carry, and catch themselves is a strength, range, and balance reserve that this module measures case by case.

Where these models sit. Every case in this module is a Level 1 model in the course's sense: one or two rigid segments, a single equilibrium or conservation law, closed-form arithmetic, and parameters taken from the reference human. Sections 2, 4, 6 and 7 are quasi-static, so they drop inertial terms entirely; Sections 3 and 5 are Level 1 dynamic, using work-energy and impulse-momentum without integrating an equation of motion. Only K8 steps to Level 2, integrating a prescribed trajectory to recover the inertial correction the quasi-static estimates omit, and it finds that correction to be sixteen percent at an ordinary rise speed. Nothing here is inverse dynamics (Module 8), a muscle model (Module 5), or a controller (Module 12); the point of a Level 1 pass is that it already puts a number on every task of the day, and names which of them run close to a limit. Where a case needs more, the section says which module supplies it.

What each case draws on: rising and stairs use the joint torques and power of Modules 1, 5, and 8; lifting uses the low-back moment of Module 1 and the tissue tolerance of Modules 2 and 16; turning and stumble recovery use the balance margin of Module 10; jar and door use the grip and endpoint force of Module 11; and every case is coordinated by the controller of Module 12 and powered by the muscles of Module 5.

1. The case-study method

Before the cases, we fix the template that unifies them. It is a five-step reduction from a real movement to a number, and every later section is an instance of it.

Definition 1 (the case-study pipeline). To analyze a daily movement: (1) Idealize - replace the body with the minimal set of rigid links, joints, and point masses that carries the essential mechanics. (2) Free-body diagram - identify every external force (gravity, ground reaction, contact, load) and the internal joint torques. (3) Govern - write the balance law: static moment balance $\sum M=0$ for a slow (quasi-static) task, or the momentum/energy law for a fast (dynamic) one. (4) Estimate - insert human-scale masses, lengths, and moment arms and read off the number. (5) Test - simulate if the task is dynamic, and locate the failure mode: which parameter, reduced by how much, makes the movement fail.

Two governing laws recur, and choosing between them is the first judgement each case demands (Fig. 2). When the movement is slow enough that accelerations are negligible - holding a lift, pressing a door - the body is in quasi-static equilibrium and the relevant law is moment balance about a joint.

Quasi-static moment balance. About any joint, the muscle torque equals the sum of external moments: $$\tau_{\rm muscle}=F_{\rm muscle}\,d_{\rm muscle}=\sum_i W_i\,d_i,$$ where each external weight $W_i$ acts at horizontal moment arm $d_i$ from the joint and the muscle acts at its (usually short) moment arm $d_{\rm muscle}$. The short muscle arm is why internal forces dwarf external loads (the low back of Section 4).

When the movement is fast - rising briskly, climbing at cadence, catching a stumble - accelerations matter and the relevant laws are work-energy and impulse-momentum: the work done by joint torques changes the body's mechanical energy, and the impulse of the ground reaction changes its momentum.

Work, power, and impulse. A joint torque $\tau$ turning at angular velocity $\omega$ delivers power $P=\tau\,\omega$ and, over an angle, work $U=\int\tau\,d\theta$; positive work is concentric (lifting the body), negative is eccentric (braking it, as in stair descent). A ground-reaction impulse $\int\mathbf F\,dt$ equals the change in the body's momentum $m\,\Delta\mathbf v$ (the turn and stumble of Section 5).

The third recurring quantity is a margin: the ratio of what the body can supply to what the task demands. A strength margin is the maximum voluntary joint torque over the demanded torque; a tissue margin is the tissue's tolerance over the applied stress (Modules 2, 16). A movement is possible when every margin exceeds one and safe with room to spare, and aging or injury shrink the numerator, the capacity, while chair height, load distance, and speed set the denominator. The balance margin is the one exception to the ratio form: Module 10 defines it as a length, the distance of the extrapolated centre of mass inside the base of support, so its threshold is zero rather than one, and a movement is possible while it stays positive. Each case below reports its binding margin in whichever of the two forms the quantity has. With the template fixed, we turn it on the first movement of the day.

2. Rising from a chair

Standing up from a chair is the movement that most often separates independent living from assisted care, because it runs closest to the limit of a common capacity - knee extensor strength. The movement has two phases: a flexion-momentum phase, where the trunk pitches forward to bring the centre of mass over the feet and build forward velocity, and an extension phase, where the legs straighten to lift the body. The mechanically critical instant is seat-off, when the thighs leave the seat and the entire body weight, until then partly supported by the chair, passes through the feet.

Idealize the body at seat-off as two segments: the shanks, fixed at the ankles, and everything above the knees - thighs, pelvis, trunk, head and arms, the HAT (head, arms, trunk) plus the thighs - pivoting about the knees. Take that upper mass as $m_{\rm up}=0.8\,m$: this is an assumption, not a regression. Module 1's Appendix puts the head-arms-trunk fraction at $0.60\,m$, also assumed, and two thighs add roughly a further $0.20\,m$, so $0.8\,m$ is the sum with the thighs counted in full. Counting them in full slightly overstates the moment, because a thigh's own centre of mass sits nearer the knee pivot than the trunk's does and so acts at a shorter arm than the common $d$ this model gives every part of the upper body. The overstatement is conservative for a strength margin and it is the only fraction this section needs; K1 shows how the whole conclusion moves if it is wrong, since $d_{\max}=\tau_{\max}/(m_{\rm up}g)$ is inversely proportional to it. Its centre of mass sits a horizontal distance $d$ behind the knee joint, and gravity's moment there must be balanced by the knee extensors (the quadriceps, through the patellar tendon of Module 6).

Proposition 2.1 (peak knee torque at seat-off). In the quasi-static limit, the knee extensor torque required at seat-off is $$\boxed{\;\tau_{\rm knee}=m_{\rm up}\,g\,d,\;}$$ where $m_{\rm up}$ is the body mass above the knees and $d$ the horizontal distance from the knee to that mass's centre of mass. For $m_{\rm up}=0.8\times70=56\ \mathrm{kg}$, $g=9.81\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$, $d=0.20\ \mathrm{m}$, this is $\tau_{\rm knee}\approx110\ \mathrm{N\,m}$, or $1.57\ \mathrm{N\,m/kg}$ of body mass.
Take moments about the knee joint for the upper body treated as a rigid segment pivoting there (the shanks are fixed, so the knee is the working joint). The only external moment is gravity acting at the upper body's centre of mass, at horizontal distance $d$ from the knee, giving a flexing moment $m_{\rm up}g\,d$. In the quasi-static limit (slow rise, negligible angular acceleration) the net moment is zero, so the knee extensors must supply an equal and opposite extensor moment $\tau_{\rm knee}=m_{\rm up}g\,d$. Substituting the values gives $56\times9.81\times0.20\approx110\ \mathrm{N\,m}$. ∎

That $1.57\ \mathrm{N\,m/kg}$ (Fig. 3) is most of what a healthy adult can produce - peak voluntary knee extensor torque is about $2$-$3\ \mathrm{N\,m/kg}$ in the young and falls toward $1$-$1.5\ \mathrm{N\,m/kg}$ in the old. The strength margin for a chair rise is therefore small, and two everyday facts follow directly from the formula. A lower or softer chair increases $d$ (the knee starts more flexed and the COM sits further behind the knee), raising the required torque - the reason a low sofa is far harder to leave than a tall stool. And the momentum strategy is not a stylistic quirk: pitching the trunk forward before seat-off does two mechanical things, and D7 separates them. It moves the centre of mass toward the feet, shrinking $d$ in $\tau_{\rm knee}=m_{\rm up}gd$, which is a first-order reduction; and it gives the body upward momentum $m_{\rm up}v_0$ that the extensors then do not have to create, which D7 computes at about six percent of the extension impulse. The often-quoted third effect, transferring the trunk's horizontal momentum into the rise, needs a coupled two-segment model this module does not build, and is not claimed here. When even that is not enough, people recruit the arms - pushing on the armrests adds an external upward force at the hands, subtracting directly from the knee's share.

Failure mode. Sit-to-stand fails when the demanded torque exceeds the available: a strength margin below one. Aging lowers the numerator, the available torque (sarcopenia - the age-related loss of muscle mass and force, Module 14) while low chairs raise the demand, so the same person who rises easily at 40 may, at 80, be unable to leave a low armchair without pushing off - and the clinical five-times-sit-to-stand test times exactly this margin. The mechanical fixes all target the formula: a higher, firmer chair (smaller $d$), armrests (an added external force), and strength training (a larger available torque).

3. Stairs: ascent and descent

Stairs are the clearest case of work against gravity, because every step changes the body's height by a fixed amount. Ascending, the legs do positive work to raise the centre of mass; descending, they do negative work to lower it under control. The two are not mirror images - one is powered, the other braked - and the asymmetry is why descent, not ascent, is the greater fall risk.

Proposition 3.1 (work and power on stairs). Raising the body by one step of height $h$ requires mechanical work against gravity $$\boxed{\;U_{\rm step}=m\,g\,h,\qquad P=m\,g\,h\,f=m\,g\,v_{\rm vert},\;}$$ where $f$ is the step frequency and $v_{\rm vert}=hf$ the average vertical speed. For $m=70\ \mathrm{kg}$, $h=0.17\ \mathrm{m}$: $U_{\rm step}\approx117\ \mathrm{J}$ per step; at $f=1.8\ \mathrm{steps/s}$ ($v_{\rm vert}\approx0.31\ \mathrm{m/s}$), $P\approx210\ \mathrm{W}$ of gravitational power.
By the work-energy theorem, the mechanical work done against gravity to raise the centre of mass by $h$ (with negligible net change in kinetic energy step to step, since the body returns to the same speed each cycle) equals the gain in gravitational potential energy, $U_{\rm step}=mgh$. Power is work per unit time; at step frequency $f$ the body climbs $f$ steps per second, so $P=U_{\rm step}f=mghf$, and since the vertical speed is $v_{\rm vert}=hf$, $P=mg\,v_{\rm vert}$. Substituting gives $70\times9.81\times0.17\approx117\ \mathrm{J}$ and $117\times1.8\approx210\ \mathrm{W}$. ∎

This gravitational power (Fig. 4) is the minimum mechanical output; the muscles supply more, because they also swing the legs and are less than perfectly efficient (Module 8's metabolic cost). Inverse dynamics (Module 8) distributes the work across the joints: on ascent the hip and knee extensors do most of the positive work at push-off, the ankle contributing a final propulsive burst. The $\approx210\ \mathrm{W}$ figure - sustained - is why a long staircase is aerobically taxing: it is roughly the sustained output of a brisk cycling effort, and it sits at $84\%$ of the $250\ \mathrm{W}$ ceiling this module assumes (Lab B), which is why a long staircase cannot be climbed at that cadence indefinitely.

Descent inverts the sign. To lower the body one step, the legs do negative work $-mgh$: the knee extensors contract while lengthening (eccentric action, Module 5), absorbing the potential energy the body gives up rather than adding to it. This is metabolically cheaper than ascent but mechanically more demanding of control - an eccentric brake must dissipate $117\ \mathrm{J}$ per step smoothly, and if the knee gives way the energy converts to an impact instead. That is the mechanical reason a descent failure becomes a fall rather than a stop; the epidemiology is not derived here. It is also why knee pain (osteoarthritis, Module 4) is felt most on the way down: eccentric loading concentrates high contact stress on the patellofemoral joint (where the kneecap runs in its groove on the thigh bone) at a flexed knee.

Failure mode. Ascent fails on power - insufficient extensor strength or aerobic capacity to sustain $mgv_{\rm vert}$ - and people slow their cadence to keep within it. Descent fails on control - insufficient eccentric strength or too slow a reflex to arrest a buckling knee - and the failure is a fall, not merely a stop. The training implications differ: ascent wants strength and endurance, descent wants eccentric control and quadriceps capacity, which is why rehabilitation after knee injury drills the controlled step-down specifically.

4. Lifting and carrying: the low back

Lifting a box off the floor is the movement that most reliably injures, and the reason is the lever ratio. The muscles that extend the trunk - the erector spinae, running just behind the spine - act at a moment arm of only about $5\ \mathrm{cm}$ from the intervertebral discs, while the load in the hands and the weight of the trunk itself act at moment arms several times larger. To balance a modest external moment, the back muscles must pull with a large force, and that force presses the vertebrae together: the spine pays for the lever disadvantage in compression.

Idealize the lifter, bent forward, as a lever pivoting at the L5/S1 disc (the lumbosacral junction, the most loaded level). Three forces create moments about it: the upper-body weight $W_{\rm tr}$ at horizontal moment arm $d_{\rm tr}$, the load $W_L$ in the hands at moment arm $d_L$, and the erector spinae force $F_m$ at the short arm $d_m$. Moment balance fixes $F_m$; force balance then gives the disc compression.

Proposition 4.1 (spinal compression in a lift). Balancing moments about L5/S1, the erector spinae force and the resulting disc compression are $$\boxed{\;F_m=\frac{W_{\rm tr}d_{\rm tr}+W_L d_L}{d_m},\qquad F_{\rm comp}=F_m+(W_{\rm tr}+W_L)\cos\phi,\;}$$ with $\phi$ the trunk's inclination from vertical and $d_{\rm tr}=\ell_t\sin\phi$, where $\ell_t$ is the fixed along-trunk distance from the disc to the upper body's centre of mass. Posture therefore enters through $\phi$ alone. Taking Module 1's values for the same lift (upper body $m_{\rm ub}=0.60M=42\ \mathrm{kg}$, so $W_{\rm tr}=412\ \mathrm{N}$; $\ell_t=0.346\ \mathrm{m}$; $d_m=0.05\ \mathrm{m}$), a $20\ \mathrm{kg}$ box at $d_L=0.40\ \mathrm{m}$ lifted from a $\phi=60^\circ$ stoop gives $d_{\rm tr}=0.30\ \mathrm{m}$, $F_m\approx4040\ \mathrm{N}$ and $F_{\rm comp}\approx4350\ \mathrm{N}$, reproducing Module 1's low-back analysis exactly. The same box lifted from a $\phi=20^\circ$ squat with the load pulled to $d_L=0.25\ \mathrm{m}$ gives $F_{\rm comp}\approx2530\ \mathrm{N}$. The NIOSH (US National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health) action limit is $3400\ \mathrm{N}$: the stoop is well over it, the squat well under.
Take moments about the L5/S1 disc. The external flexing moments are $W_{\rm tr}d_{\rm tr}$ (trunk weight) and $W_L d_L$ (load); the erector spinae supplies the balancing extensor moment $F_m d_m$. Quasi-static balance $\sum M=0$ gives $F_m d_m=W_{\rm tr}d_{\rm tr}+W_L d_L$, hence $F_m=(W_{\rm tr}d_{\rm tr}+W_L d_L)/d_m$. The trunk's centre of mass sits a fixed distance $\ell_t$ along the trunk from the disc, so tipping the trunk $\phi$ from vertical puts it a horizontal distance $d_{\rm tr}=\ell_t\sin\phi$ ahead: standing up shortens that arm as $\sin\phi$, which is the whole of the squat's postural benefit. For the compression, resolve forces along the spine's long axis: the muscle force $F_m$ acts nearly parallel to the spine, and the upper body plus load contribute their axial component $(W_{\rm tr}+W_L)\cos\phi$, so the disc reaction is $F_{\rm comp}=F_m+(W_{\rm tr}+W_L)\cos\phi$. Substituting the stoop, $W_{\rm tr}=412\ \mathrm{N}$, $W_L=196\ \mathrm{N}$, $d_{\rm tr}=0.346\sin60^\circ=0.300\ \mathrm{m}$: $F_m=(412\times0.300+196.2\times0.40)/0.05=(123.6+78.5)/0.05=4042\ \mathrm{N}$, and $F_{\rm comp}=4042+608\times\cos60^\circ=4346\ \mathrm{N}$. To three significant figures these are $4040\ \mathrm{N}$ and $4350\ \mathrm{N}$, the stoop value of Module 1 and of Lab C. Substituting the squat, $d_{\rm tr}=0.346\sin20^\circ=0.118\ \mathrm{m}$ and $d_L=0.25\ \mathrm{m}$: $F_m\approx1960\ \mathrm{N}$ and $F_{\rm comp}\approx2530\ \mathrm{N}$. Both moves matter, and the derivation says which is which: standing up shrinks $d_{\rm tr}$ as $\sin\phi$, pulling the load in shrinks $d_L$ directly. ∎

Four and a third kilonewtons (Fig. 5) is $6.3$ times body weight, pressed through one disc, to lift a box a fifth of body weight. The amplification is the lever ratio and nothing else: the load's own contribution to the muscle force is $d_L/d_m=8$ times its weight, the trunk's is $d_{\rm tr}/d_m=6$ times its weight, and the two sum to $F_m/W_L=20.6$ times the box's weight. Two everyday rules fall straight out of the formula, and the formula also ranks them. Keep the load close: $F_m$ is linear in $d_L$, and pulling the box from $0.40$ to $0.25\ \mathrm{m}$ at the same stoop cuts the compression from $4350$ to $3760\ \mathrm{N}$, a $14\%$ saving. Stand up: the trunk arm shrinks as $\sin\phi$, and coming from $60^\circ$ to $20^\circ$ with the box still at $0.40\ \mathrm{m}$ cuts it to $3120\ \mathrm{N}$, a $28\%$ saving. Posture buys twice what reach buys, and doing both, the squat lift, gives $2530\ \mathrm{N}$, a $42\%$ cut and the difference between clearly hazardous and comfortably safe. K3 sweeps the two together.

Carrying shifts the geometry sideways. A bag in one hand adds a load at a lateral moment arm about the spine, which the contralateral trunk side-flexors must balance - so a one-sided carry both compresses and side-bends the spine, and people instinctively lean away from the bag to bring the combined centre of mass back over the pelvis, trading a lateral moment for a small compression increase. Splitting the load into two bags, one per hand, cancels the lateral moments and is mechanically far kinder.

Failure mode. Lifting fails when disc compression (or shear) exceeds tissue tolerance: acute failure is disc herniation or, in osteoporotic bone (Module 2), a vertebral compression fracture; chronic overload is the mechanics behind much low-back pain. Tolerance falls with age as discs dehydrate and bone thins, while the demand is set by load, distance, and posture - which is why safe-lifting guidance is entirely about shrinking $d_L$ and $\phi$, and why a young back tolerating a careless stoop lift is running the same $4350\ \mathrm{N}$ that fractures an old one.

5. Turning and catching a stumble

Straight-line walking (Module 8) is only part of getting about; real locomotion is a sequence of turns, and turns - together with the stumbles that interrupt them - are where the balance machinery of Module 10 is spent hardest. Both are momentum problems: a turn redirects the body's momentum, a stumble recovery arrests an unplanned one, and both are governed by impulse.

To turn while walking, the horizontal velocity must change direction. Redirecting a speed $v$ through a turn angle $\beta$ changes the velocity vector even at constant speed, and that change of momentum must be delivered as a sideways impulse by the ground.

Proposition 5.1 (impulse to turn). Redirecting the horizontal velocity through angle $\beta$ at constant speed $v$ requires a ground-reaction impulse of magnitude $$\boxed{\;J=m\,|\Delta\mathbf v|=2\,m\,v\,\sin(\beta/2),\;}$$ directed toward the inside of the turn. For $m=70\ \mathrm{kg}$, $v=1.3\ \mathrm{m/s}$, $\beta=60^\circ$: $J\approx91\ \mathrm{N\,s}$.
The horizontal momentum before the turn is $m\mathbf v_1$ and after is $m\mathbf v_2$, with $|\mathbf v_1|=|\mathbf v_2|=v$ and the angle between them $\beta$. By the impulse-momentum theorem the ground-reaction impulse equals the momentum change, $J=m|\mathbf v_2-\mathbf v_1|$. For two equal-length vectors separated by angle $\beta$, $|\mathbf v_2-\mathbf v_1|=2v\sin(\beta/2)$ (the base of the isosceles triangle of sides $v$). Hence $J=2mv\sin(\beta/2)$, directed along $\mathbf v_2-\mathbf v_1$, i.e. toward the inside of the turn. Substituting, $2\times70\times1.3\times\sin30^\circ=91\ \mathrm{N\,s}$. ∎

That impulse (Fig. 6) is delivered over a step, and while it is being delivered the body's extrapolated centre of mass (XcoM, Module 10) swings - crucially - toward the inside of the turn and must be kept over a base of support that is itself being repositioned by the stepping foot. A turn is thus a coupled problem: generate the lateral impulse and keep the XcoM captured, all while the base is in motion. This is the mechanical reason turning is harder than walking straight - it demands lateral balance control at the moment the base is least stable - and the reason balance rehabilitation drills turning steps specifically.

A stumble is the uninvited version of the same physics. A trip decelerates the swinging foot and pitches the trunk forward, throwing the XcoM toward and past the toe - exactly Module 10's condition for a mandatory change of support. Recovery is a reactive step that must plant a new base ahead of the racing XcoM, and its required length is set by the capturability result of Module 10.

Stumble recovery (imported from Module 10). After a trip, a fixed-support (ankle/hip) response suffices only while the extrapolated COM stays within the foot; once $\xi=x_{\rm com}+\dot x_{\rm com}/\omega_0$ (with $\omega_0=\sqrt{g/\ell}$ the pendulum rate and $\ell$ the COM height; Module 10) passes the toe, a recovery step of length at least the XcoM's forward excursion is mandatory. Early in swing the body uses an elevating strategy (lift the tripped foot over the obstacle and reach forward); late in swing a lowering strategy (plant the foot down fast and take a compensatory step with the other leg). The choice is timing-critical, and a delay of tens of milliseconds - the reflex slowing of Module 10 and aging - is the difference between a caught stumble and a fall.

Failure mode. Turning fails when lateral balance control cannot keep the XcoM captured over the moving base - a lateral balance-margin shortfall - and stumble recovery fails when the reactive step is too short, too slow, or too weak to plant ahead of the XcoM. Both degrade with the same Module 10 parameters (reflex delay, step speed and length, torque capacity), which is why turning and tripping, not straight walking, dominate real-world falls.

6. Opening a jar, pushing a door

The hand's daily tasks are torque problems delivered through the manipulator of Module 11. Two are archetypal: unscrewing a jar lid, limited by grip friction, and pushing a door, limited by reach geometry. Both convert a hand action into a torque about an axis, and both reveal why the everyday design of lids and handles is really applied mechanics.

A jar lid is held by friction (and often a vacuum seal) that resists rotation with some torque $\tau_{\rm resist}$. To open it the hand grips the lid rim (radius $r$) and twists; friction between skin and lid converts the inward grip force $F_{\rm grip}$ into a tangential force $\mu_{\rm lid} F_{\rm grip}$ around the rim, and that tangential force at radius $r$ produces the opening torque.

Proposition 6.1 (grip-limited jar torque). A hand gripping a lid of radius $r$ with total effective normal force $F_{\rm grip}$ and skin-lid friction $\mu_{\rm lid}$ can apply an opening torque up to $$\boxed{\;\tau_{\rm open}=\mu_{\rm lid}\,F_{\rm grip}\,r,\;}$$ so opening a lid that resists with $\tau_{\rm resist}$ requires $F_{\rm grip}\ge\tau_{\rm resist}/(\mu_{\rm lid} r)$. Skin on a lid runs from about $\mu_{\rm lid}=0.5$ (dry skin on smooth steel) to about $1.0$ (a rubber pad); take the mid-range $0.7$ for the worked number and let K6 work the ends. For $\tau_{\rm resist}=2\ \mathrm{N\,m}$, $r=0.035\ \mathrm{m}$, $\mu_{\rm lid}=0.7$: a tangential force of $\approx57\ \mathrm{N}$ around the rim, needing a grip force of $\approx82\ \mathrm{N}$.
The friction available at the grip is $F_t\le\mu_{\rm lid} F_{\rm grip}$ (Coulomb's law, Module 11), acting tangent to the lid rim. A tangential force $F_t$ at radius $r$ exerts a torque $\tau=F_t\,r$ about the lid axis; maximizing $F_t$ gives $\tau_{\rm open}=\mu_{\rm lid} F_{\rm grip}r$. Opening requires $\tau_{\rm open}\ge\tau_{\rm resist}$, i.e. $\mu_{\rm lid} F_{\rm grip}r\ge\tau_{\rm resist}$, so $F_{\rm grip}\ge\tau_{\rm resist}/(\mu_{\rm lid} r)=2/(0.7\times0.035)\approx82\ \mathrm{N}$; the tangential force is $\mu_{\rm lid} F_{\rm grip}\approx57\ \mathrm{N}$, equivalently $\tau_{\rm resist}/r$. ∎

The formula names every jar-opening aid (Fig. 7). A rubber pad or a dry hand raises $\mu_{\rm lid}$; a wider lid or a jar wrench raises $r$; both cut the grip force needed in inverse proportion. The task is grip-limited, and grip strength is among the first capacities to fall with age and the worst hit by hand arthritis - so a stuck jar is a threshold task: at the mid-range $\mu_{\rm lid}=0.7$ a $150\ \mathrm{N}$ grip delivers $3.7\ \mathrm{N\,m}$ and opens the lid easily, while a $60\ \mathrm{N}$ grip delivers $1.5\ \mathrm{N\,m}$ and does not. On dry skin at $\mu_{\rm lid}=0.5$ that same $60\ \mathrm{N}$ grip delivers only $1.05\ \mathrm{N\,m}$ (K6), which is why the aid, not the effort, is what changes the outcome. It is also why grip strength is measured in frailty assessment, though the module derives only the jar, not the correlation.

Pushing a door is the complementary case - not grip-limited but geometry-limited. A door resists opening with a torque about its hinge (a latch, a seal, a self-closer), and the hand's force $F_{\rm hand}$ at distance $d_{\rm hinge}$ from the hinge supplies the opening torque $F_{\rm hand}d_{\rm hinge}$; balance requires $F_{\rm hand}=\tau_{\rm door}/d_{\rm hinge}$ - a one-line lever balance beside the jar's friction subtlety, needing no separate proposition. (The letter $d$ alone is reserved for the chair-rise arm of Section 2.) For a self-closer of $\tau_{\rm door}\approx20\ \mathrm{N\,m}$ and a handle at $d_{\rm hinge}=0.8\ \mathrm{m}$, a modest $\approx25\ \mathrm{N}$ suffices - which is exactly why handles are placed far from the hinge, and why a handle near the hinge (or a door pushed near its edge) feels immovable. The arm delivers $F_{\rm hand}$ through the endpoint-force relation $\boldsymbol\tau=J^{\mathsf T}\mathbf F$ of Module 11, bracing best with a near-straight arm along the push - the force-ellipsoid lesson made domestic.

7. Walking up and down a slope

A slope changes what gravity does over a stride. On level ground the net work against gravity over a stride is zero (the COM returns to its height); on a grade, every stride nets a height change, and the component of gravity along the path either resists progress (uphill) or drives it (downhill). The mechanics is a one-line resolution of the weight vector, but its consequences - why uphill is aerobically hard and downhill is hard on the knees - are opposite.

Proposition 7.1 (power on a slope). Walking at speed $v$ along a slope of inclination $\theta$, the extra mechanical power against gravity beyond level walking is $$\boxed{\;P_{\rm slope}=m\,g\,v\,\sin\theta,\;}$$ positive (supplied by the muscles) uphill and negative (absorbed, braked) downhill. For $m=70\ \mathrm{kg}$, $v=1.0\ \mathrm{m/s}$: $P_{\rm slope}\approx60,\,119,\,178\ \mathrm{W}$ at $\theta=5^\circ,10^\circ,15^\circ$ ($9\%,18\%,27\%$ grade).
Resolve the weight $mg$ into components parallel and perpendicular to the slope. The parallel component is $mg\sin\theta$, directed down the slope. Walking up the slope at speed $v$ moves the body against this component at rate $v$, so the power the muscles must add is force times velocity, $P_{\rm slope}=mg\sin\theta\cdot v$. Equivalently, the vertical speed is $v\sin\theta$ and the gravitational power is $mg\times v\sin\theta$, the same result. Downhill the parallel component aids motion, so the muscles must absorb $mg\sin\theta\cdot v$ eccentrically to hold speed constant. Substituting $\theta=10^\circ$: $70\times9.81\times1.0\times\sin10^\circ\approx119\ \mathrm{W}$. ∎

Uphill (Fig. 8), the extra $60$-$180\ \mathrm{W}$ is added on top of level walking's cost, which is why a moderate hill quickly raises heart rate and why people slow down to hold power within their aerobic limit - the same power ceiling that caps stair ascent (Section 3). Downhill flips the demand from supply to absorb: the legs must dissipate $mg\sin\theta\,v$ as eccentric muscle work step after step, concentrating high forces on the quadriceps and the patellofemoral joint at a flexed knee - the reason a long descent leaves the quadriceps sore for days: eccentric loading is the regime that produces delayed muscle soreness (Module 5), and it aggravates knee osteoarthritis (Module 4) far more than the climb did. Steep enough, and eccentric braking cannot keep up: the walker must switchback, sidestep, or descend slowly, converting an absorption problem back into a controllable one.

Failure mode. Uphill fails on aerobic/strength power - the same ceiling as stairs; downhill fails on eccentric control and joint tolerance, the same as stair descent. Slopes are simply stairs with the risers spread out, and they inherit both failure modes.

Lab D - the recovery step a stumble demands

Physical question. After a trip, how long a recovery step is needed as a function of how fast the body is pitching forward? Model. Module 10's extrapolated centre of mass; the step must reach the XcoM. Parameters. COM height $\ell=1.0\ \mathrm{m}$ ($\omega_0=\sqrt{g/\ell}$), forward COM velocity $\dot x$ from $0.3$ to $1.5\ \mathrm{m/s}$, initial COM offset $x_0=0.05\ \mathrm{m}$.       % (x0 + 1.0/w0, max_step))
print('max recoverable speed with no delay: %.2f m/s'
      % (w0*(max_step - x0)))
plt.plot(xdot, xi, label='required step (XcoM)')
plt.axhline(max_step, ls='--', label='max reachable')
plt.xlabel('forward COM speed (m/s)')
plt.ylabel('recovery step length (m)')
plt.legend()
plt.show()

Interpretation. The required step grows linearly with forward speed: at $\dot x=1.0\ \mathrm{m/s}$ the XcoM is $\approx0.37\ \mathrm{m}$ ahead, within a $0.60\ \mathrm{m}$ reachable step, but past $\dot x\approx1.72\ \mathrm{m/s}$ the demand exceeds any single step and a fall (or a second step) is unavoidable. 10. Problems, diagnostics, and limitations

Jump to: Conceptual · Derivational · Computational · Diagnostics · Limitations · Repayment

Thirty problems - ten conceptual, ten derivational, ten computational - plus five diagnostics. Each carries a Probes note and a collapsible worked solution; computational solutions quote numbers the code produces.

Conceptual ↑ contents

C1 - the low soft chair. Why is a low, soft armchair so much harder to leave than a tall, firm stool, for the same person? Probes: the moment arm $d$ in the chair-rise torque; Section 2.
Solution
The knee torque needed at seat-off is $\tau=m_{\rm up}gd$, linear in the horizontal knee-to-COM distance $d$. A low seat starts the knees more flexed and the COM further behind and below them, enlarging $d$; a soft cushion sinks the pelvis, enlarging it further. Since available knee torque is fixed, a larger $d$ can push the demand past it - the mechanical reason a low sofa defeats someone who rises easily from a kitchen stool. Raising and firming the seat (smaller $d$) is the first, cheapest intervention.
C2 - down is worse than up. Stair falls happen far more on descent than ascent. Why, in terms of what the muscles must do? Probes: eccentric control versus power; Section 3.
Solution
Ascent requires positive work - the muscles add energy to raise the body - and its failure is benign: run out of power and you simply slow or stop. Descent requires negative work - the knee extensors lengthen under load (eccentric) to absorb $mgh$ per step - and its failure is catastrophic: if the eccentric brake gives way, the absorbed energy becomes an impact and a fall. Control, not power, is binding on the way down, and control degrades with reflex slowing and weak quadriceps, which is the mechanical reason a descent failure becomes a fall rather than a stop.
C3 - keep it close. Safe-lifting advice says hold the load close to the body. Why is this the single most effective rule? Probes: the $d_L$ lever in spinal compression; Section 4.
Solution
Spinal compression is dominated by the erector spinae force $F_m=(W_{\rm tr}d_{\rm tr}+W_Ld_L)/d_m$, and the muscle arm $d_m\approx5\ \mathrm{cm}$ is tiny, so every newton-metre of extra external moment costs $1/d_m=20\ \mathrm{N}$ of muscle force. For a $20\ \mathrm{kg}$ box that is $W_L/d_m=3924\ \mathrm{N}$ per metre of reach, or $39\ \mathrm{N}$ for every centimetre the load drifts away from the body. Halving the horizontal load distance $d_L$ halves the load's whole contribution to the muscle force and, with it, most of the compression. The one change that competes with it is standing up, which shrinks the other arm as $d_{\rm tr}=\ell_t\sin\phi$; K3 sweeps the two together and finds posture the larger lever at the geometry of Section 4. Neither is a matter of strength: the compression is set by geometry, and $d_L$ is the piece of geometry the lifter controls with the least effort.
C4 - squat versus stoop. Why does squatting to lift load the spine less than stooping with straight legs? Probes: both moment arms in the compression formula; Section 4.
Solution
A stoop lift (bend at the waist, straight legs) maximizes both external moment arms: the trunk pitches far forward (large $d_{\rm tr}$) and the load hangs far from the spine (large $d_L$). A squat lift (bend the knees, keep the trunk upright) minimizes both: the trunk stays near vertical (small $d_{\rm tr}$) and the load can be tucked close (small $d_L$). Since compression $\propto W_{\rm tr}d_{\rm tr}+W_Ld_L$, the squat's smaller arms give a lower compression. Section 4's numbers for a $20\ \mathrm{kg}$ box are $\approx2530\ \mathrm{N}$ squatting ($\phi=20^\circ$, $d_L=0.25\ \mathrm{m}$) against $\approx4350\ \mathrm{N}$ stooping ($60^\circ$, $0.40\ \mathrm{m}$) - a $42\%$ cut, and the whole of it is geometry. The formula also ranks the two moves: the trunk arm collapses as $\sin\phi$ while the load arm falls only in proportion to itself, so standing up buys roughly twice what pulling the box in buys (K3).
C5 - the lean away. Why do you instinctively lean away from a heavy bag carried in one hand? Probes: the lateral spinal moment of an asymmetric carry; Section 4.
Solution
A bag in one hand hangs at a lateral moment arm from the spine, creating a side-bending moment the trunk muscles must balance. Leaning the trunk away shifts the body's own weight to the opposite side, creating an equal and opposite moment that cancels the bag's - bringing the combined centre of mass back over the pelvis and the base of support. The lean trades a large muscular side-bending effort for a small increase in symmetric compression; splitting the load into two bags removes the lateral moment entirely and is mechanically kinder.
C6 - falls happen at corners. Why do turns and trips, rather than straight walking, account for most falls? Probes: dynamic balance and the moving base; Section 5, Module 10.
Solution
Straight walking keeps the extrapolated COM tracking forward over a base repositioned in the direction of travel - a well-practised, stable pattern. A turn demands a lateral impulse ($2mv\sin(\beta/2)$) while the XcoM swings sideways and the base is being repositioned in a new direction, taxing lateral balance exactly when the base is least stable. A trip throws the XcoM past the foot, demanding a fast, accurate reactive step (Module 10). Both draw heavily on the balance margin and are timing-critical, so both, not steady walking, are where balance fails.
C7 - the rubber jar-opener. Why does a thin rubber pad let you open a jar you could not open bare-handed? Probes: friction in the grip-limited torque; Section 6.
Solution
The opening torque is $\tau_{\rm open}=\mu_{\rm lid} F_{\rm grip}r$, limited by the skin-lid friction coefficient $\mu_{\rm lid}$. Skin on a smooth or damp lid has low $\mu_{\rm lid}$, so most of your grip force cannot be converted into turning force. A rubber pad roughly doubles $\mu_{\rm lid}$, doubling the achievable torque for the same grip - often enough to cross the threshold from "won't budge" to "opens." It adds no strength; it converts more of the strength you already have into torque.
C8 - handles far from hinges. Why is a door handle placed at the edge farthest from the hinge, and why does pushing near the hinge feel impossible? Probes: the moment arm about the hinge; Section 6.
Solution
Opening a door is a torque about the hinge: $F_{\rm hand}d_{\rm hinge}=\tau_{\rm door}$, so the hand force needed is $\tau_{\rm door}/d_{\rm hinge}$, inversely proportional to the distance $d_{\rm hinge}$ from the hinge. At the far edge $d_{\rm hinge}$ is largest and the force smallest (a gentle $\approx25\ \mathrm{N}$); pushing near the hinge makes $d_{\rm hinge}$ tiny, so the required force grows without bound as $d_{\rm hinge}\to0$; a door pushed on its hinge line cannot be opened at all. Placing the handle far from the hinge is the free mechanical advantage designers hand the user.
C9 - lungs up, knees down. Why does walking uphill tax the lungs while walking downhill taxes the knees? Probes: positive versus negative work on a slope; Section 7.
Solution
Uphill, the muscles do positive work against gravity's along-slope component, adding energy at rate $mgv\sin\theta$ - a metabolic (aerobic) demand that raises heart rate and breathing. Downhill, that component aids motion, so the muscles must absorb the same power eccentrically to hold speed constant; eccentric braking is metabolically cheap but concentrates high forces on the quadriceps and the knee/patellofemoral joint at a flexed knee. Same $mgv\sin\theta$, opposite sign, opposite organ system: supply uphill, absorb downhill.
C10 - one weakness, many failures. Why does a single deficit like weak quadriceps impair rising, stair-climbing, and downhill walking all at once? Probes: shared reserves across cases; Section 8.
Solution
Different daily tasks draw on the same small set of reserves. Knee extensor strength is the binding margin for the chair rise ($m_{\rm up}gd$), for stair ascent power, and for the eccentric control of stair and slope descent - so one deficit in that reserve shows up simultaneously across every task that spends it. This is why frailty presents as a general loss of capability rather than a list of unrelated failures, and why training the shared reserve (leg strength) restores several tasks at once - the mechanical basis of targeted exercise.

Derivational ↑ contents

D1 - chair-rise torque. Derive the quasi-static knee extensor torque at seat-off. Probes: moment balance about the knee; Section 2.
Solution
Treat the body above the knees as a rigid segment pivoting at the knee (shanks fixed). Gravity acts at its centre of mass, horizontal distance $d$ from the knee, giving a flexing moment $m_{\rm up}gd$. Quasi-static balance $\sum M=0$ requires the knee extensors to supply an equal extensor moment, so $\tau_{\rm knee}=m_{\rm up}gd$ (Proposition 2.1).
D2 - stair work and power. Derive the work per step and the climbing power. Probes: the work-energy theorem; Section 3.
Solution
Each step raises the COM by $h$; with kinetic energy unchanged step to step, the work equals the potential-energy gain $U_{\rm step}=mgh$. At step frequency $f$, power is $P=U_{\rm step}f=mghf=mgv_{\rm vert}$ with $v_{\rm vert}=hf$ (Proposition 3.1).
D3 - spinal compression. Derive the erector spinae force and disc compression when lifting. Probes: moment and force balance about L5/S1; Section 4.
Solution
Moment balance about L5/S1: $F_md_m=W_{\rm tr}d_{\rm tr}+W_Ld_L$, so $F_m=(W_{\rm tr}d_{\rm tr}+W_Ld_L)/d_m$. The trunk arm is not free: the upper body's centre of mass sits a fixed distance $\ell_t$ along the trunk from the disc, so at a trunk inclination $\phi$ from vertical it stands a horizontal distance $d_{\rm tr}=\ell_t\sin\phi$ ahead, and posture enters the formula through that one factor. Force balance along the spine adds the axial weight component: $F_{\rm comp}=F_m+(W_{\rm tr}+W_L)\cos\phi$ (Proposition 4.1). The short $d_m$ makes $F_m$, and hence the compression, large.
D4 - turning impulse. Derive the ground impulse needed to redirect the walk through angle $\beta$. Probes: impulse-momentum for a vector velocity change; Section 5.
Solution
Momentum changes from $m\mathbf v_1$ to $m\mathbf v_2$ with equal speeds $v$ and included angle $\beta$; the impulse is $J=m|\mathbf v_2-\mathbf v_1|$. Two equal vectors at angle $\beta$ differ by $2v\sin(\beta/2)$, so $J=2mv\sin(\beta/2)$, directed inward (Proposition 5.1).
D5 - jar torque. Derive the maximum opening torque a grip can apply and the grip force to beat a resisting torque. Probes: friction converted to torque; Section 6.
Solution
Friction gives tangential force $\le\mu_{\rm lid} F_{\rm grip}$ at the lid rim (radius $r$), so torque $\le\mu_{\rm lid} F_{\rm grip}r$. Opening needs $\mu_{\rm lid} F_{\rm grip}r\ge\tau_{\rm resist}$, i.e. $F_{\rm grip}\ge\tau_{\rm resist}/(\mu_{\rm lid} r)$ (Proposition 6.1).
D6 - slope power. Derive the extra power of walking on a grade. Probes: resolving weight along the slope; Section 7.
Solution
The along-slope weight component is $mg\sin\theta$; walking up at speed $v$ works against it at rate $P=mg\sin\theta\,v$ (equivalently $mg\times v\sin\theta$, the vertical power). Downhill the sign flips and the muscles absorb it eccentrically (Proposition 7.1).
D7 - the momentum strategy. Show why pitching the trunk forward before rising reduces the extension-phase knee/hip torque. Probes: momentum transfer at seat-off; Section 2.
Solution

Work in the vertical direction and treat the body above the knees as a point mass $m_{\rm up}$ that must be raised through $H$ and arrive at rest. Let the extension phase begin at seat-off with upward velocity $v_0$ and last a time $T_e$. Vertical impulse-momentum over the extension phase gives $\int_0^{T_e}\!\big(F_z-m_{\rm up}g\big)\,dt=m_{\rm up}(0-v_0)=-m_{\rm up}v_0$, so the extra ground impulse the legs must supply beyond holding the weight is reduced by exactly $m_{\rm up}v_0$: the momentum the flexion phase already put into the body is momentum the extensors do not have to create. Equivalently in energy, the rise needs $m_{\rm up}gH$ of work and the flexion phase has already delivered $\tfrac12m_{\rm up}v_0^2$ of it, so the extensors supply $m_{\rm up}gH-\tfrac12m_{\rm up}v_0^2$. Take $m_{\rm up}=56\ \mathrm{kg}$, $H=0.40\ \mathrm{m}$, and assume a modest seat-off speed $v_0=0.25\ \mathrm{m/s}$ over an extension lasting $T_e=0.4\ \mathrm{s}$ (both assumptions, Appendix): that is $220-1.8=218\ \mathrm{J}$ against $220\ \mathrm{J}$, so the energy saving is under one percent, while the saving in impulse is $m_{\rm up}v_0=14\ \mathrm{N\,s}$ against the $m_{\rm up}gT_e\approx220\ \mathrm{N\,s}$ of the extension, about six percent. So the flexion phase buys a small direct reduction, and the larger part of the strategy is geometric: pitching the trunk forward moves the centre of mass toward the feet and shrinks $d$ in $\tau=m_{\rm up}gd$, which is a first-order effect on torque. Quantifying the transfer of horizontal trunk momentum into the rise needs the coupled two-segment model; this module does not build it, and Section 2's claim should be read as the geometric effect plus this six percent, not more.

D8 - the safe-lift envelope. From the compression limit, derive the maximum safe load as a function of its distance. Probes: inverting the compression formula; Section 4.
Solution
Set $F_{\rm comp}=F_{\rm lim}$ and solve $ (W_{\rm tr}d_{\rm tr}+W_Ld_L)/d_m+(W_{\rm tr}+W_L)\cos\phi=F_{\rm lim}$ for $W_L$: $W_L\big(d_L/d_m+\cos\phi\big)=F_{\rm lim}-W_{\rm tr}d_{\rm tr}/d_m-W_{\rm tr}\cos\phi$, so $W_L^{\max}=\dfrac{F_{\rm lim}-W_{\rm tr}(d_{\rm tr}/d_m+\cos\phi)}{d_L/d_m+\cos\phi}$. The safe load falls roughly as $1/d_L$ at fixed posture, and as $1/\sin\phi$ in the trunk term: with $F_{\rm lim}=3400\ \mathrm{N}$ it is $\approx35\ \mathrm{kg}$ squatting ($\phi=20^\circ$, $d_L=0.25\ \mathrm{m}$) and only $\approx8.7\ \mathrm{kg}$ stooping ($\phi=60^\circ$, $d_L=0.40\ \mathrm{m}$), the same $8.7\ \mathrm{kg}$ Module 1 derives for this lift. K3 sweeps the two arms jointly.
D9 - the recovery step. Derive the minimum recovery-step length after a trip from the extrapolated COM. Probes: capturability; Section 5, Module 10.
Solution
The free inverted pendulum diverges along $\xi=x_{\rm com}+\dot x_{\rm com}/\omega_0$ with $\omega_0=\sqrt{g/\ell}$ (Module 10). A recovery step arrests the fall only if the new base of support reaches the extrapolated COM, so the minimum step length is $\xi=x_0+\dot x/\omega_0$. It grows linearly with forward speed and, with a reaction delay $\Delta t$, to $\xi\,e^{\omega_0\Delta t}$.
D10 - the carry lean. Derive the trunk lean that recentres the COM over the pelvis when carrying a one-hand load. Probes: cancelling a lateral moment; Section 4.
Solution
A bag of weight $W_b$ at lateral arm $d_b$ makes a moment $W_bd_b$ about the spine. Leaning the trunk shifts the body weight $W_{\rm tr}$ to the far side by $x_{\rm lean}$, an opposing moment $W_{\rm tr}x_{\rm lean}$. Setting them equal, $x_{\rm lean}=W_bd_b/W_{\rm tr}$: a $10\ \mathrm{kg}$ bag at $0.30\ \mathrm{m}$ makes a $29.4\ \mathrm{N\,m}$ moment and needs a $\approx0.071\ \mathrm{m}$ COM shift against the upper-body weight $W_{\rm tr}=412\ \mathrm{N}$ of Section 4. Two symmetric bags cancel the lateral moment and need no lean.

K1 - the chair an older adult can leave (threshold + inverse). Sweep the knee-to-COM distance $d$ and find the highest chair (smallest $d$) that a person with a given peak knee torque can rise from; compare a typical older adult ($1.5\ \mathrm{N\,m/kg}$) and a frail one ($1.0\ \mathrm{N\,m/kg}$). Probes: the strength-margin threshold; Section 2.
Solution
Setting $m_{\rm up}gd=\tau_{\max}$ and inverting, the largest tolerable $d$ is $d_{\max}=\tau_{\max}/(m_{\rm up}g)$. For $m_{\rm up}=56\ \mathrm{kg}$: a typical older adult ($\tau_{\max}=105\ \mathrm{N\,m}$) rises up to $d=0.19\ \mathrm{m}$ (most chairs), but a frail adult ($70\ \mathrm{N\,m}$) only up to $d=0.13\ \mathrm{m}$ - a high, firm chair. The inverse gives the design target: to keep the frail adult independent, seats must hold $d\le0.13\ \mathrm{m}$, i.e. be tall and firm. The threshold, not the average, is what independence turns on.

K2 - the cadence ceiling (inverse + sensitivity). Find the maximum sustainable stair cadence for a given power ceiling, and how it changes with body mass. Probes: inverting the power law; Section 3.
Solution

From $P=mghf$, the maximum cadence at a ceiling $P$ is $f_{\max}=P/(mgh)$. At $P=250\ \mathrm{W}$ and $h=0.17\ \mathrm{m}$ this is $2.14\ \mathrm{steps/s}$ at $70\ \mathrm{kg}$ but only $1.67\ \mathrm{steps/s}$ at $90\ \mathrm{kg}$: a $29\%$ mass increase costs $22\%$ of cadence, which is why load carriage and obesity slow stair climbing measurably. The sensitivity is $\partial f_{\max}/\partial m=-P/(m^2gh)$, proportional to the ceiling itself: at $70\ \mathrm{kg}$ it is $0.018\ \mathrm{steps\,s^{-1}kg^{-1}}$ for a $150\ \mathrm{W}$ ceiling and $0.049$ for a $400\ \mathrm{W}$ one. A fit climber therefore loses more absolute cadence per added kilogram than a frail one, while both lose the same $1\%$ of cadence per $1\%$ of mass, since the relative sensitivity $\mathrm{d}\ln f_{\max}/\mathrm{d}\ln m=-1$ is independent of $P$. That distinction is the point: the fractional cost of carrying weight upstairs is a property of the geometry, not of the climber.

    print("m = %5.1f kg -> f_max = %.3f steps/s" % (mb, f))
print("70 -> 90 kg: mass +%.1f%%, cadence %.1f%%"
      % (100*(90-70)/70, 100*(f_max[2]-f_max[1])/f_max[1]))
for Pc in (150.0, 250.0, 400.0):
    print("ceiling %5.1f W -> |df_max/dm| at 70 kg = %.4f steps/s per kg"
          % (Pc, Pc/(70.0**2*g*h)))
K3 - the safe-lift envelope (two-parameter sweep). Sweep trunk angle $\phi$ and load distance $d_L$ jointly and compute the largest load that keeps spinal compression under the $3400\ \mathrm{N}$ limit on each. Which single move buys more, standing up or pulling the box in, and where do the two together change the answer by a factor rather than a fraction? Probes: a two-parameter safety boundary that separates posture from reach; Section 4.
Solution

Inverting Proposition 4.1 at $F_{\rm comp}=F_{\rm lim}$ gives $W_L^{\max}=\big[F_{\rm lim}-W_{\rm tr}(\ell_t\sin\phi/d_m+\cos\phi)\big]\big/\big(d_L/d_m+\cos\phi\big)$, a surface over the two arms rather than a curve over one. Across the grid the safe load runs from $35.0\ \mathrm{kg}$ at the squat corner ($\phi=20^\circ$, $d_L=0.25\ \mathrm{m}$) to $6.4\ \mathrm{kg}$ at the deep-stoop corner ($60^\circ$, $0.55\ \mathrm{m}$). Taken one at a time from the $60^\circ$, $0.40\ \mathrm{m}$ stoop, standing to $20^\circ$ raises the safe load from $8.7$ to $23.2\ \mathrm{kg}$ and pulling the box in to $0.25\ \mathrm{m}$ raises it to $13.4\ \mathrm{kg}$: posture buys roughly twice what reach buys, because the trunk's own $412\ \mathrm{N}$ acts at an arm that collapses as $\sin\phi$ while the load's arm falls only in proportion to itself. Together they give $35.0\ \mathrm{kg}$, four times the stoop value. That is the quantitative form of "squat, don't stoop": not a fractional improvement but a change of what the lift is, and it is why safe-lifting guidance leads with posture and not with strength.

import numpy as np

g, m, d_m, F_lim = 9.81, 70.0, 0.05, 3400.0
W_tr = 0.60*m*g
ell_t = 0.30/np.sin(np.radians(60))       # along-trunk COM distance


def safe_load(dL, phi_deg):

    return (F_lim - W_tr*(d_tr/d_m + c))/(dL/d_m + c)

K4 - the recoverable envelope (2-parameter sweep). Compute the maximum forward speed from which a trip can be recovered in one step, as a function of reaction delay. Probes: how delay shrinks the balance envelope; Section 5, Module 10.
Solution
With a maximum step $L=0.60\ \mathrm{m}$, the recoverable speed satisfies $(x_0+\dot x/\omega_0)e^{\omega_0\Delta t}\le L$, so $\dot x_{\max}=\omega_0(Le^{-\omega_0\Delta t}-x_0)$. For $\omega_0=3.13\ \mathrm{rad/s}$: no delay gives $\dot x_{\max}=1.72\ \mathrm{m/s}$, but a $100\ \mathrm{ms}$ reaction delay drops it to $1.22\ \mathrm{m/s}$ - a $29\%$ loss of the recoverable envelope from a tenth-second slowing. This quantifies why reflex slowing (Module 10, aging) so sharply raises trip-fall risk: the envelope shrinks exponentially in delay.
import numpy as np

g, ell, x0, L = 9.81, 1.0, 0.05, 0.60
w0 = np.sqrt(g/ell)
for dt in (0.0, 0.05, 0.10, 0.15, 0.20):
    v = w0*(L*np.exp(-w0*dt) - x0)
    print("delay %4.0f ms -> max recoverable speed %.3f m/s" % (1e3*dt, v))
v0 = w0*(L - x0)
v1 = w0*(L*np.exp(-w0*0.10) - x0)
print("a 100 ms delay costs %.1f%% of the envelope" % (100*(v0 - v1)/v0))
K5 - the sharpest safe turn (friction limit). Find the maximum turn angle achievable in one step before the foot must slip, as a function of walking speed. Probes: the friction-limited lateral impulse; Section 5.
Solution
The lateral impulse $2mv\sin(\beta/2)$ delivered over a step of duration $t_{\rm step}$ needs an average lateral force $2mv\sin(\beta/2)/t_{\rm step}\le\mu_{\rm shoe} mg$ (no slip), where $\mu_{\rm shoe}$ is the shoe-floor friction coefficient - a different contact from the jar's $\mu_{\rm lid}$. Solving, $\sin(\beta/2)\le\mu_{\rm shoe} g\,t_{\rm step}/(2v)$. For $\mu_{\rm shoe}=0.7$ (a shoe on dry indoor flooring; assumed), $t_{\rm step}=0.35\ \mathrm{s}$: at $v=1.0\ \mathrm{m/s}$ any turn is possible ($\le180^\circ$), at $v=1.3\ \mathrm{m/s}$ up to $\beta\approx135^\circ$, but at $v=2.0\ \mathrm{m/s}$ only $\beta\approx74^\circ$ before the foot slips. Fast turns must be gentle - the mechanics behind slowing to round a sharp corner, and behind slip-turn falls on low-friction floors.

K6 - which aid opens the jar? (regime comparison). For a weak grip ($60\ \mathrm{N}$), compute the opening torque bare-handed, with a rubber pad, and with a jar wrench, and see which crosses a $2\ \mathrm{N\,m}$ threshold. Probes: the two levers $\mu_{\rm lid}$ and $r$; Section 6.
Solution

Opening torque is $\mu_{\rm lid} F_{\rm grip}r$. With $F_{\rm grip}=60\ \mathrm{N}$: bare hand ($\mu_{\rm lid}=0.5$, dry skin on smooth steel, $r=0.035\ \mathrm{m}$) gives $1.05\ \mathrm{N\,m}$ - fails the $2\ \mathrm{N\,m}$ lid; a rubber pad ($\mu_{\rm lid}=1.0$) gives $2.10\ \mathrm{N\,m}$ - just opens; a jar wrench ($\mu_{\rm lid}=0.7$, $r=0.060\ \mathrm{m}$) gives $2.52\ \mathrm{N\,m}$ - opens comfortably. K7 - how fast can you climb the hill? (inverse + regime). Given a sustained-power ceiling, compute the maximum walking speed up slopes of increasing grade, and contrast the downhill braking demand. Probes: inverting the slope-power law; Section 7.
Solution
The gravity power budget $mgv\sin\theta\le P$ gives $v_{\max}=P/(mg\sin\theta)$ for the climbing share. K8 - does a brisk rise cost more torque? (dynamic simulation + regime). Simulate a chair rise with a minimum-jerk COM path and compare the peak knee torque of a slow (quasi-static) and a brisk rise. Probes: the inertial cost of speed; Section 2.
Solution
Driving the COM up by $H=0.40\ \mathrm{m}$ on a minimum-jerk path in $T=1.2\ \mathrm{s}$ adds a vertical acceleration $a_z(t)$, so the knee torque becomes $m_{\rm up}(g+a_z)d$. K9 - splitting a carried load (optimisation). A total load is split between the two hands, each share hanging at lateral distance $d_b$ from the spine. Sweep the split fraction and find the distribution that minimises the lateral spinal moment - and hence the trunk lean, and the extra compression that lean itself creates, needed to cancel it. Probes: optimising a load distribution; Section 4.
Solution
With a fraction $\alpha$ of the total $10\ \mathrm{kg}$ load in one hand and $1-\alpha$ in the other (each at $\pm d_b=0.30\ \mathrm{m}$), the net lateral moment about the spine is $|2\alpha-1|\,M_Lg\,d_b$, and the trunk lean needed to cancel it, $x_{\rm lean}=|2\alpha-1|M_Lg\,d_b/W_{\rm tr}$, grows with the imbalance - itself adding symmetric compression. Both the residual side-flexor effort and the lean's compression penalty increase with $|2\alpha-1|$, so sweeping $\alpha$ traces a V with its minimum at the even split $\alpha=0.5$ (net moment $0$, no lean); the one-handed extreme $\alpha=1$ is the worst case at $29.4\ \mathrm{N\,m}$ of lateral moment, a $0.071\ \mathrm{m}$ lean, and $589\ \mathrm{N}$ of side-flexor force at the $5\ \mathrm{cm}$ arm. (The lean is $0.071\ \mathrm{m}$, not the $0.09\ \mathrm{m}$ a lighter trunk would give, because $W_{\rm tr}=412\ \mathrm{N}$ is Module 1's upper body, Section 4.) The even two-bag split is thus the optimum along a continuum, not merely better than one bag - and any deviation costs in proportion to the imbalance.

K10 - which task fails first? (multi-margin sweep). Scale a young adult's two leg reserves - peak knee extensor torque $2.5\ \mathrm{N\,m/kg}$ and sustained leg power $250\ \mathrm{W}$ - down by a common factor, and compute the factor at which each everyday task's margin reaches one. Which task is the first casualty, and does the answer depend on the chair? Probes: shared reserves and the ordering of thresholds; Section 8.
Solution

Assume the two reserves decline together by a common factor $s$; that is a modelling assumption, not a result, and it is what lets a torque-limited task and a power-limited one be ranked on one axis. Each task fails when $s$ falls to its demand divided by the young reserve. The low chair goes first at $s=0.88$, stair climbing at habitual cadence next at $0.84$, the standard chair at $0.63$, and only near half strength do the gentle slope ($0.48$) and the high firm chair ($0.47$) fail. Two things follow. The first casualty is not "rising from a chair" but rising from a particular chair: the same person with the same legs keeps the high stool for another forty percent of decline after the low sofa is lost, which is why Section 2's advice is about the furniture and not only about the quadriceps. And the two tasks at the top of the list, the low chair and the stairs, are separated by four points of reserve, so a person who has just started avoiding the sofa is already close to slowing on the stairs - the shared-reserve effect of Section 8 made into a schedule.

Five diagnostics ↑ contents

  1. Units of compression. Check that $(W\,d)/d_m$ has units of force.
    Solution
    $W\,d$ is $\mathrm{N\cdot m}$ (a moment); dividing by $d_m$ in $\mathrm{m}$ gives $\mathrm{N}$ - a force, as required.

    Toward the inside of the turn (to your left): the impulse $2mv\sin(\beta/2)$ points along $\mathbf v_2-\mathbf v_1$, inward.

    Solution
    Positive (concentric) ascending - the muscles add energy; negative (eccentric) descending - they absorb it.

    Solution
    Absorbed: gravity aids the descent, so the muscles must brake it eccentrically at that rate.
