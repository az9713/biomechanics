0. Motivation - the number you never measured

Every module of this course has been written in the currency of joint torque. Module 1 balanced the moment a biceps must supply to hold a weight; Module 5 turned neural drive into muscle force and then into joint torque; Modules 8, 11, and 13 read off the ankle, knee, hip, and shoulder torques of walking, reaching, and rising from a chair. In every case the torque was given - a number we computed from a model, or asked a simulation to produce. But no instrument measures joint torque. You cannot put a sensor on a knee. What you can measure is where the body is, how hard it presses on the ground, and what voltage its muscles put on the skin - and from those, and a model, you must infer the torque.

Section 0. Measurement chain: markers on a Winter-proportioned skeleton estimate joint centres; the force plate supplies GRF and COP under a real foot outline — inverse dynamics connects the two. Blue discs: marker positions; blue cross-hair: the centre of pressure (COP); red arrow: the ground reaction force (GRF).

This module builds that inference, honestly and end to end. It is the bridge between the mechanical models of the whole course and the data a laboratory or a phone actually collects. The task has three hard parts, and the module is organised around them.

First, the sensors do not measure the state you want. A camera records the two-dimensional pixel position of a marker; a force plate records three force components and a point of application; an inertial measurement unit (IMU - a small chip pairing an accelerometer and a gyroscope) records linear acceleration and angular rate in its own tilting frame; electromyography (EMG) records a voltage that rises with muscle activation but is not force. None of these is a joint angle, and none is a torque. Section 1 lays out this measurement chain - what each instrument records, and the observation model that maps the body's true state onto it.

Second, the operation that converts position into the dynamics - differentiation - amplifies noise by a factor that grows as a power of the sampling rate. Inverse dynamics needs accelerations, and acceleration is the second time-derivative of position. Differentiating a noisy signal is the step that most often ruins a biomechanics result: as we will prove in Section 3, the variance of a finite-difference derivative grows like the noise variance divided by the time step squared, so halving the sampling interval to sharpen a derivative quadruples the noise variance in it. Sections 3 and 4 build the defence - smoothing, and a principled choice of how much - and show there is an optimal amount of smoothing that trades bias against variance.

Third, the answer is only as trustworthy as its error bars. A torque curve with no uncertainty is a claim without evidence. Sections 8 and 9 propagate the measurement noise, the body-segment-parameter uncertainty, and the model's own inconsistency through the pipeline, so the output is a torque with a quantified error - and show how to identify the model's parameters from data and validate the whole chain against an independent measurement.

The spine is the pipeline of Fig. 1, built left to right: the instruments (Section 1), the planar body model and its inertial parameters (Section 2), smoothing and differentiation (Sections 3-4), the centre of mass and its force-plate check (Section 5), the inverse-dynamics recursion that is the heart of the module (Section 6), the assembled pipeline on synthetic data (Section 7), the error analysis (Section 8), and parameter identification and validation (Section 9). Four labs and a thirty-problem set close it. On the course's level ladder these sections sit at Level 4 (inverse kinematics and inverse dynamics), built on the Level 3 multibody link-segment model of Section 2: the mechanics is the rigid-body mechanics of Modules 1 to 3, and what is new is running it backwards, from measured motion to the forces that caused it, with an error bar attached. No new tissue law and no controller enters here. By the end, the torques the course has spent fourteen modules using will be numbers you know how to produce - and how far to trust.

1. The measurement chain: what each instrument records

The object of the whole module is the body's configuration over time. For a planar rigid-body model (Section 2) that configuration is a vector of joint angles $\mathbf q(t)=(q_1,\dots,q_n)$, and the dynamics also need its first and second time-derivatives, the joint angular velocities $\dot{\mathbf q}$ and accelerations $\ddot{\mathbf q}$. No sensor delivers $\mathbf q$ directly. Each instrument returns some function of the state, corrupted by noise; recovering $\mathbf q$ from those readings is an estimation problem, and naming the map from state to reading precisely is the first step.

Definition 1.1 (observation model). Let the body's state at time $t$ be $\mathbf x(t)$ (positions, and where relevant velocities). An instrument returns a reading $$\mathbf y(t)=\mathbf h\big(\mathbf x(t)\big)+\boldsymbol\varepsilon(t),$$ where $\mathbf h$ is the (generally nonlinear) observation map fixed by the instrument's physics and geometry, and $\boldsymbol\varepsilon(t)$ is measurement noise with zero mean and covariance $\mathbf R=\operatorname{cov}(\boldsymbol\varepsilon)$. Estimation is the inverse problem: recover $\mathbf x$ (hence $\mathbf q,\dot{\mathbf q},\ddot{\mathbf q}$) from the readings $\mathbf y$ given $\mathbf h$ and the noise statistics.

The instruments of movement science differ in what their $\mathbf h$ is, and in how noisy and how direct they are. The five that matter are collected in Fig. 2 and the table that follows.

It measures a force directly and precisely, whereas every other instrument measures a position (or, for the IMU, an acceleration) that must be processed into the dynamics. That is why inverse dynamics is anchored at the foot: the one place a clean force is known is the ground contact, and the recursion (Section 6) marches up the body from there. The IMU is the one device that measures acceleration without differentiating - a genuine advantage - but it pays for it with bias drift that corrupts any attempt to integrate back to position, the mirror image of the differentiation problem. EMG never enters the torque calculation. It tells you which muscle is active and when, which is invaluable for validating a result or resolving the redundancy of Section 9, but because the map from activation to force depends on length, velocity, and fatigue (Module 5), surface EMG is not a calibrated force sensor. Throughout, we keep the instruments and the model separate: the instrument supplies $\mathbf y$; the model supplies $\mathbf h$ and the dynamics; estimation joins them.

A sixth instrument: biochemical markers as a systemic rate probe

One more instrument belongs beside the five mechanical ones of Fig. 2, because it answers the same question - infer a hidden rate from a noisy reading - for a process none of the five can see. A camera, a force plate, an IMU and EMG all read a mechanical state: a position, a force, an acceleration, a voltage tied to one muscle. But two quantities other modules of this course have taken as given are not mechanical at all: how fast a bone is being built up and torn down (Module 2 §7's remodelling balance) and how close a working muscle sits to its glycolytic ceiling (Module 9 §7's three-supply-system model). Both are inferred today from a blood or serum assay, and the same Definition 1.1 that organised the first five instruments organises this one, with one difference worth stating precisely.

Definition 1.2 (a biochemical marker as a systemic rate probe). Let $x(t)$ be a hidden physiological rate - a tissue remodelling rate, or a muscle's anaerobic ATP-supply flux. A biochemical marker is a blood or serum concentration $$y(t) \;=\; h\big(x\big)(t) \;+\; \varepsilon(t),$$ the same observation-model form as Definition 1.1, except that $h$ is not a geometric projection but a pooling: $h(x)(t)$ is a functional of the whole history of $x$, not of its instantaneous value, because the marker is released into, distributed through, and cleared from one shared blood volume that drains the whole skeleton or the whole exercising musculature - release, distribution and clearance each take time, so $y$ lags $x$. A single venous draw is therefore a delayed, systemically averaged echo of a rate produced (or consumed) all over the body, never a local reading at one bone or one joint the way a marker on the shank reads that segment alone.

Naming what a marker measures is only half the job Definition 1.1 set out: the other half is $\varepsilon$, the noise, and here it is not one number but three, of wildly different size.

The first is biological. The true rate $x(t)$ is not constant even in a stable, healthy person: bone resorption follows a marked circadian rhythm (CTX is highest overnight and falls after a meal), and a working muscle's lactate at a fixed external workload varies test to test with hydration, prior exercise, and glycogen state. Drawn at an uncontrolled time of day, that swing is effectively random from one visit to the next, which is what lets Lemma 1.1 below treat it as one more zero-mean noise term; a fasting, time-matched protocol instead turns it into a fixed offset shared by both draws, which cancels in a before-after comparison rather than adding in quadrature - the same distinction Proposition 8.1 draws between random and systematic error. Reported within-subject biological coefficients of variation (CV), for an uncontrolled draw time, run near $25\%$ for CTX, a much quieter $8\%$ for P1NP, and about $12\%$ for a fixed-workload lactate reading.

The second is analytical. The assay itself - an immunoassay for the two collagen fragments, an enzymatic electrode for lactate - has its own imprecision, typically $6\%$ for an automated CTX immunoassay, $3\%$ for intact P1NP, and $3\%$ for a lactate analyser.

The third is the one this course has already proved a bound for: the counting floor. Module 0 §1 Proposition 1.2 gives the relative fluctuation in the number of molecules a fixed sample volume contains, $\sigma_N/\bar N = 1/\sqrt{\bar N}$ with $\bar N = c\,N_A\,V$, $N_A=6.022\times10^{23}\ \mathrm{mol^{-1}}$ Avogadro's number. It is worth asking, rather than assuming, whether it matters here.

$$\bar N_{\rm CTX} \approx 5.2\times10^{9}, \qquad \bar N_{\rm P1NP} \approx 1.7\times10^{10}, \qquad \bar N_{\rm lac} \approx 6.0\times10^{14}.$$

These are for a representative serum CTX of $0.30\ \mathrm{ng/mL}$ (molar mass $869\ \mathrm{g/mol}$, the eight-residue telopeptide fragment EKAHD-β-GGR) and P1NP of $40\ \mathrm{ng/mL}$ (molar mass $35\,000\ \mathrm{g/mol}$, the trimeric propeptide), each in a representative $25\ \mu\mathrm L$ assay well, and a resting blood lactate of $1\ \mathrm{mM}$ in a $1\ \mu\mathrm L$ capillary sample. By Proposition 1.2 the counting-noise CV is $1/\sqrt{\bar N}$: $0.0014\%$ for CTX, $0.00076\%$ for P1NP, $0.0000041\%$ for lactate - between $3.6$ and $5.9$ decades below the biological and analytical CVs above (assay against counting floor: a factor of about $4.3\times10^3$, or $3.6$ decades, for CTX; $3.9\times10^3$, or $3.6$ decades, for P1NP; $7.3\times10^5$, or $5.9$ decades, for lactate). Module 0 §1's own worked example, resting calcium in a half-sarcomere ($\bar N \approx 64$, $12.5\%$), sits at the opposite extreme: the same law, evaluated at femtolitre volumes and micromolar ion concentrations rather than microlitre serum samples and nanomolar-to-millimolar assay targets, moves the counting floor from $0.0014\%$ of the reading to $12.5\%$ of it. The floor is real; for these three markers it simply never binds.

Lemma 1.1 (propagated CV and the least significant change). Let a marker reading be $y = x(1+\eta_{\rm bio})(1+\eta_{\rm assay})(1+\eta_{\rm count})$, with the three fractional errors independent, zero-mean, small, and approximately normal, and coefficients of variation $\mathrm{CV_{bio}}$, $\mathrm{CV_{assay}}$, $\mathrm{CV_{count}}$. Then $$\boxed{\;\mathrm{CV_{tot}^2} \;=\; \mathrm{CV_{bio}^2} + \mathrm{CV_{assay}^2} + \mathrm{CV_{count}^2}\;}$$ and the smallest observed change between two draws on the same subject that is unlikely, at $95\%$ confidence, to be noise alone - the least significant change (LSC) - is $$\boxed{\;\mathrm{LSC} \;=\; z_{0.975}\sqrt2\;\mathrm{CV_{tot}}\;y_{\rm baseline}\;\approx\;2.772\;\mathrm{CV_{tot}}\;y_{\rm baseline},\qquad z_{0.975}=1.96.\;}$$ A true change equal to the LSC is itself detected only about half the time; the LSC is the threshold an observed difference must clear, not a guarantee about a given true change.
To first order in each small $\eta$, $(1+\eta_{\rm bio})(1+\eta_{\rm assay})(1+\eta_{\rm count}) \approx 1 + \eta_{\rm bio}+\eta_{\rm assay}+\eta_{\rm count}$, so the fractional error in $y$ is the sum of three independent zero-mean terms. Its variance is the sum of their variances - the same quadrature step Proposition 8.1 used for the torque's absolute error, applied here to a relative one - giving $\mathrm{CV_{tot}^2}=\mathrm{CV_{bio}^2}+\mathrm{CV_{assay}^2}+\mathrm{CV_{count}^2}$. For two independent draws $y_1,y_2$ of a subject whose true value has not changed, the difference $D=y_2-y_1$ has $\operatorname{Var}(D)=\operatorname{Var}(y_1)+\operatorname{Var}(y_2)\approx 2(\mathrm{CV_{tot}}\,y_{\rm baseline})^2$, so $D$ has standard deviation $\sqrt2\,\mathrm{CV_{tot}}\,y_{\rm baseline}$; a two-tailed $95\%$ critical value for a quantity drawn from that spread is $z_{0.975}=1.96$ standard deviations. Any observed change larger than the boxed LSC is therefore unlikely, at that confidence, to be noise alone. The linearisation is a close approximation even at the $25\%$-plus CTX value below: the exact statement of the same independence, with no linearisation, is $1+\mathrm{CV_{tot}^2} = (1+\mathrm{CV_{bio}^2})(1+\mathrm{CV_{assay}^2})(1+\mathrm{CV_{count}^2})$, which for CTX's $25\%,6\%$ gives $\mathrm{CV_{tot}}=25.75\%$ against the boxed formula's $25.71\%$ - a quarter-point gap, not the source of any real error here. What large-CV linearisation does threaten is the symmetric interval itself: $y$ is positive and its multiplicative model is right-skewed, so a two-sided $y_{\rm baseline}\pm\mathrm{LSC}$ interval is least accurate exactly where $\mathrm{CV_{tot}}$ is largest. The standard clinical-chemistry fix works in $\ln y$, where the same independence gives $\operatorname{Var}(\ln y)=\sum_i\ln(1+\mathrm{CV}_i^2)$ exactly - for CTX this sums to $25.3\%$, close to but not identical with the linear $25.71\%$, because $\ln(1+u)\ne u$ once $u$ is not small. ∎

Biological variation (red) and assay imprecision (blue) set the scale in every case; the counting floor (orange) is visible only because the axis is logarithmic. The black diamond marks each marker's combined, quadrature-summed CV, and the annotation below each group is the resulting least significant change: a single CTX measurement must move by more than about $71\%$ before a change is distinguishable from noise, P1NP by about $24\%$, lactate by about $34\%$.

The three markers therefore have LSCs of $71\%$, $24\%$ and $34\%$ - the P1NP and lactate figures a factor of about three below the untimed CTX figure. A change in P1NP of about a quarter, or in lactate of about a third, is a real signal that a well-designed trial or training programme can use. A single CTX draw, by contrast, carries enough biological noise on its own - chiefly the diurnal rhythm named above, which no assay improvement can remove - that a fasting, time-matched protocol is worth its cost: cutting $\mathrm{CV_{bio}}$ from the untimed $25\%$ to a fasting, same-time-of-day figure nearer $10\%$ gives $\mathrm{CV_{tot}}=\sqrt{10^2+6^2}\%=11.7\%$ and $\mathrm{LSC}=32\%$, roughly halving the threshold. None of the three errors is the counting floor: the same law that limits Module 0 §1's most delicate signal, resting intracellular calcium, is - for a blood test drawing on a whole body's worth of molecules - nowhere near the bottleneck.

2. The planar marker model and body-segment parameters

To turn marker positions into joint angles, and joint angles into torques, we need a model of the body as a linked chain of rigid segments with known inertias. This module uses the simplest model that still captures the mechanics of the sagittal-plane movements the course has studied - squatting, a step, a lift - the planar rigid-body model: rigid segments joined by pin (revolute) joints, all moving in one vertical plane. Everything generalises to three dimensions, but the planar case exposes every idea - segment angles, differentiation, the inverse-dynamics recursion, error propagation - without the bookkeeping of 3-D rotations.

Definition 2.1 (planar segment and its markers). A rigid segment $i$ (say the shank) is a body of mass $m_i$, with its centre of mass at a point $\mathbf c_i$ and a moment of inertia $I_i$ about that centre (for planar motion, a single scalar). Its orientation is a single segment angle $\theta_i$, the angle its long axis makes with the horizontal. Skin markers are fixed points on the segment; the vector between the proximal marker (nearer the trunk) and the distal marker (farther) points along the segment, so $$\theta_i=\operatorname{atan2}\big(y_{\rm dist}-y_{\rm prox},\;x_{\rm dist}-x_{\rm prox}\big),$$ recovering the segment angle from two measured marker positions. A joint angle is a difference of adjacent segment angles, e.g. the knee flexion $q_{\rm knee}=\theta_{\rm thigh}-\theta_{\rm shank}$.

The geometry recovers the angles. The dynamics needs more: each segment's mass, the location of its centre of mass along its length, and its moment of inertia. These body-segment parameters (BSP) are not measured on the subject - you cannot weigh a living person's shank - but are estimated from anthropometric regression tables built from cadaver studies and volumetric scans, which express each segment's mass as a fraction of body mass, its centre-of-mass location as a fraction of segment length, and its inertia through a radius of gyration.

Definition 2.2 (body-segment parameters). For segment $i$ of a person of total mass $M$ and with segment length $L_i$, the anthropometric model gives $$m_i=f_i\,M,\qquad c_i=\rho_i\,L_i,\qquad I_i=m_i\,(k_i L_i)^2,$$ where $f_i$ is the segment's mass fraction, $\rho_i$ the centre-of-mass fraction (measured from the proximal joint), and $k_i$ the radius-of-gyration fraction (both dimensionless multipliers of $L_i$). 3. Noise, smoothing, and why differentiation is dangerous

Inverse dynamics needs velocities and accelerations, and the only way to get them from marker positions is to differentiate. This is the pipeline's most error-prone step, because differentiation is a high-pass operation: it multiplies each frequency component by that frequency, so it amplifies exactly the fast, small wiggles that measurement noise is made of. To see how badly, model the measurement first.

Definition 3.1 (sampled signal with noise). A joint angle is sampled at times $t_k=k\,\Delta t$ (sampling interval $\Delta t=1/f_s$ for sampling rate $f_s$; here $k$ is the integer time-sample index, distinct from the gyration fraction $k_i$ of Definition 2.2), giving readings $$q_k=q(t_k)+\varepsilon_k,\qquad \varepsilon_k\ \text{i.i.d., }\ \mathbb E[\varepsilon_k]=0,\ \operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon_k)=\sigma^2,$$ with the noise samples $\varepsilon_k$ independent across time and of variance $\sigma^2$. A finite-difference derivative is any fixed linear combination of the $q_k$; its noise is the same linear combination of the $\varepsilon_k$.
Proposition 3.1 (finite differences amplify noise as a power of $1/\Delta t$). Under Definition 3.1, the central-difference velocity and second-difference acceleration, $$\dot q_k=\frac{q_{k+1}-q_{k-1}}{2\,\Delta t},\qquad \ddot q_k=\frac{q_{k+1}-2q_k+q_{k-1}}{\Delta t^2},$$ have noise variances $$\boxed{\;\operatorname{Var}(\dot q_k)=\frac{\sigma^2}{2\,\Delta t^2},\qquad \operatorname{Var}(\ddot q_k)=\frac{6\,\sigma^2}{\Delta t^4}.\;}$$ So the velocity noise scales as $1/\Delta t$ and the acceleration noise as $1/\Delta t^2$: faster sampling makes the raw derivatives noisier, not cleaner.
A finite difference is a linear combination $\sum_j a_j q_{k+j}$; its noise part is $\sum_j a_j\varepsilon_{k+j}$. Because the $\varepsilon$ are independent with variance $\sigma^2$, the variance of a weighted sum is $\sigma^2\sum_j a_j^2$ (cross terms vanish since $\operatorname{Cov}(\varepsilon_i,\varepsilon_j)=0$ for $i\neq j$). For the central velocity the weights are $a_{+1}=+\tfrac1{2\Delta t}$, $a_{-1}=-\tfrac1{2\Delta t}$, so $\sum a_j^2=\tfrac1{4\Delta t^2}+\tfrac1{4\Delta t^2}=\tfrac1{2\Delta t^2}$ and $\operatorname{Var}(\dot q_k)=\sigma^2/(2\Delta t^2)$. For the second difference the weights are $\tfrac1{\Delta t^2}(1,-2,1)$, so $\sum a_j^2=\tfrac1{\Delta t^4}(1+4+1)=\tfrac6{\Delta t^4}$ and $\operatorname{Var}(\ddot q_k)=6\sigma^2/\Delta t^4$. ∎

The numbers settle it. Take a marker-derived angle with a modest $\sigma=0.006\ \mathrm{rad}$ (about $0.3^\circ$) sampled at $f_s=100\ \mathrm{Hz}$ ($\Delta t=0.01\ \mathrm{s}$), tracking a $1\ \mathrm{Hz}$, $0.30\ \mathrm{rad}$ motion. The true velocity peaks at $A\,2\pi f_0=1.9\ \mathrm{rad/s}$ and the true acceleration at $A(2\pi f_0)^2=11.8\ \mathrm{rad/s^2}$. But Proposition 3.1 gives a raw-velocity noise of $\sigma/(\sqrt2\,\Delta t)=0.42\ \mathrm{rad/s}$ - a fifth of the signal - and a raw-acceleration noise of $\sigma\sqrt6/\Delta t^2=147\ \mathrm{rad/s^2}$, which is twelve times the true peak acceleration. The raw second derivative is not a noisy version of the answer; it is noise. Figure 3 shows the first derivative failing already.

The remedy follows from the same frequency picture. The signal lives at low frequencies (a $1\ \mathrm{Hz}$ motion, joint kinematics rarely above $\sim$6-10 Hz even in running); the noise is spread across all frequencies up to the Nyquist limit $f_s/2$. A low-pass filter that passes frequencies below a cutoff $f_c$ and rejects those above it therefore removes most of the noise while keeping the signal - provided $f_c$ is set above the motion's real bandwidth and below the noise-dominated high frequencies. In biomechanics the standard choice is a Butterworth filter (a maximally flat low-pass response), run forward and backward over the recorded data so its phase lag cancels (a "zero-lag" or dual-pass filter) - essential here, because a torque estimate delayed by even a few samples is misaligned with the force plate. Filter the angle first, then differentiate: the blue curve in Fig. 3 is exactly this, and it recovers the true velocity from the same data that gave the useless orange curve. How low to set $f_c$ is the subject of the next section.

4. Differentiation done right: the optimal cutoff

Filtering is not free. A cutoff low enough to kill the noise also attenuates the fastest genuine parts of the motion - the sharp reversal at the bottom of a squat, the heel-strike transient - so the smoothed signal is biased: systematically flattened. Set the cutoff too low and this bias dominates; set it too high and the noise of Section 3 dominates. The error as a function of cutoff is therefore a competition between two opposing terms, and the goal is not the smoothest curve but the smallest total error.

Proposition 4.1 (bias-variance decomposition and an interior optimum). Let $\hat{\dot q}(f_c)$ be the velocity estimate obtained by low-pass filtering at cutoff $f_c$ and differentiating. Its mean-square error decomposes as $$\boxed{\;\mathrm{MSE}(f_c)=\underbrace{B(f_c)^2}_{\text{bias: signal removed}}+\underbrace{V(f_c)}_{\text{variance: noise passed}},\;}$$ where the bias $B(f_c)$ (the true motion attenuated above $f_c$) decreases with $f_c$, and the passed-noise variance $V(f_c)$ increases with $f_c$. If both are smooth with $B^2$ convex-decreasing and $V$ convex-increasing from zero, the total has a unique interior minimum at the cutoff $f_c^\star$ where the marginal signal recovered equals the marginal noise admitted, $\dfrac{d(B^2)}{df_c}=-\dfrac{dV}{df_c}$.
Write the estimate as truth plus two independent departures: $\hat{\dot q}=\dot q + b(f_c) + \nu(f_c)$, where $b=\mathbb E[\hat{\dot q}]-\dot q$ is the deterministic distortion the filter imposes on the noise-free signal (it grows as more of the true spectrum is cut, i.e. as $f_c$ falls), and $\nu$ is the zero-mean random part from the passed noise. Since the noise is independent of the signal, $\mathbb E[b\,\nu]=0$, so $\mathrm{MSE}=\mathbb E[(b+\nu)^2]=b^2+\mathbb E[\nu^2]=B^2+V$ with $B^2=\lVert b\rVert^2$ and $V=\operatorname{Var}(\nu)$. As $f_c\to\infty$ no signal is removed ($B^2\to0$) but all noise passes ($V\to$ the raw value of Proposition 3.1); as $f_c\to0$ the reverse. With $B^2$ decreasing and convex and $V$ increasing and convex, the derivative $\tfrac{d}{df_c}(B^2+V)=(B^2)'+V'$ is a sum of two nondecreasing functions and is therefore nondecreasing. It is negative at small $f_c$ (where $B^2$ falls fast and $V'\to0$) and positive at large $f_c$ (where $V$ rises and $(B^2)'\to0$), so it crosses zero exactly once, at the stationary point $f_c^\star$ where $(B^2)'=-V'$, a minimum. The convexity of $V$ is doing real work: drop it and $(B^2)'+V'$ need not be monotone, so a minimum still exists on any compact range of cutoffs but need not be unique. ∎

Figure 4 is this proposition computed for the synthetic joint that Section 7 and the labs use throughout - a $0.8\ \mathrm{Hz}$, $0.40\ \mathrm{rad}$ motion sampled at $f_s=100\ \mathrm{Hz}$ with $\sigma=0.006\ \mathrm{rad}$ of marker noise. The bias falls and the passed noise rises with $f_c$, and their quadrature sum bottoms out at $f_c^\star=3.0\ \mathrm{Hz}$, where a bias of $0.013\ \mathrm{rad/s}$ meets a passed-noise standard deviation of $0.016\ \mathrm{rad/s}$ for a total RMS velocity error of $0.020\ \mathrm{rad/s}$. The minimum is shallow but real: filtering at $7\ \mathrm{Hz}$ instead costs $0.050\ \mathrm{rad/s}$, two and a half times the minimum. The optimum is this low because the test signal is a single $0.8\ \mathrm{Hz}$ harmonic: almost no true motion lives above $3\ \mathrm{Hz}$, so the bias term stays small down to a low cutoff. A broadband movement - a heel strike, the reversal at the bottom of a squat - carries real spectral content past $10\ \mathrm{Hz}$, its bias curve climbs sooner, and its optimum moves up into the $6$-$10\ \mathrm{Hz}$ band the Appendix quotes for walking. The shape of Fig. 4 is general; the position of its minimum is a property of the movement, and must be found for each recording. The practical question is how to find $f_c^\star$ without knowing the true signal. The standard answer is residual analysis (Winter's method): filter at a range of cutoffs, and for each compute the root-mean-square residual between raw and filtered signal. At high $f_c$ the residual is pure noise and falls linearly toward zero as $f_c$ rises; where the residual curve departs upward from that noise line is the cutoff at which the filter has begun removing signal, and that knee is taken as the chosen cutoff. It locates a cutoff in the operating band of Fig. 4 from the data alone, without knowing the true signal.

With clean velocities and accelerations in hand, the pipeline can turn to the two quantities inverse dynamics actually consumes: the centre of mass (Section 5) and, through it, the joint torques (Section 6).

5. Estimating the centre of mass and validating with the force plate

Two quantities complete the inputs to inverse dynamics: the whole-body centre of mass (COM), whose motion the ground reaction must explain, and an independent check that the kinematics and the forces are mutually consistent. Both come from one idea - Newton's second law applied to the whole body - and it gives the module its most valuable diagnostic.

The whole-body COM is the mass-weighted average of the segment centres. Each segment's COM position $\mathbf r_{c,i}$ is fixed on the segment by Definition 2.2 (a fraction $\rho_i$ along it from the proximal marker), so from the markers and the anthropometric table the body COM is

Definition 5.1 (whole-body centre of mass). For segments of mass $m_i=f_iM$ with centres $\mathbf r_{c,i}$, $$\mathbf r_{\rm COM}=\frac{\sum_i m_i\,\mathbf r_{c,i}}{\sum_i m_i}=\sum_i f_i\,\mathbf r_{c,i},$$ the total mass $M$ cancelling because the mass fractions sum to one ($\sum_i f_i=1$). Differentiating twice gives the COM acceleration $\ddot{\mathbf r}_{\rm COM}$ from the kinematics alone.

Now the check. The force plate measures the ground-reaction force $\mathbf F_{\rm GRF}$ directly and precisely. Newton's second law for the whole body ties it to the COM acceleration - and because the two are measured by completely different instruments (cameras versus a force plate), their agreement is a genuine, assumption-free validation.

Proposition 5.1 (force-plate validation and the dynamic residual). The only external forces on the body are gravity and the ground reaction, so the whole-body COM obeys $$\boxed{\;M\,\ddot{\mathbf r}_{\rm COM}=\mathbf F_{\rm GRF}-M\mathbf g\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \ddot{\mathbf r}_{\rm COM}=\frac{\mathbf F_{\rm GRF}}{M}-\mathbf g.\;}$$ The left side is computed from the kinematics (Definition 5.1, twice differentiated); the right side from the force plate. Their difference, the dynamic residual $\mathbf d=\ddot{\mathbf r}_{\rm COM}^{\rm kin}-(\mathbf F_{\rm GRF}/M-\mathbf g)$, is zero for a perfect measurement and otherwise quantifies the total error of the kinematic pipeline against the near-noiseless force.
Treat the body as the union of its segments and sum Newton's second law $m_i\ddot{\mathbf r}_{c,i}=\sum\mathbf F_i^{\rm ext}$ over all segments. The forces acting on any segment are gravity $m_i\mathbf g$, the ground reaction (nonzero only on the foot in contact), and the internal joint reaction forces from neighbouring segments. By Newton's third law each internal joint force appears twice, equal and opposite, so all internal forces cancel in the sum. What remains is $\sum_i m_i\ddot{\mathbf r}_{c,i}=\mathbf F_{\rm GRF}+\sum_i m_i\mathbf g=\mathbf F_{\rm GRF}-M\mathbf g$ (taking $\mathbf g$ downward). The left side is $M\ddot{\mathbf r}_{\rm COM}$ by Definition 5.1 differentiated twice. Dividing by $M$ gives the boxed identity; the residual is the measured mismatch between its two independently obtained sides. ∎

Figure 5 shows the check on a squat: the vertical COM acceleration from the cameras and from the force plate track each other to within a root-mean-square residual of $0.13\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$ on a signal peaking at $1.7\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$ - the pipeline is consistent to about seven and a half per cent, and that residual is close to the $0.12\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$ noise level Lab D later assumes for the kinematic side. When the residual is not small, it localises the problem: a residual that grows with acceleration points to body-segment-parameter error (wrong masses), while a jittery residual points to under-smoothed kinematics. The same identity underlies the classical reaction-board and double-integration methods of finding the COM, and its per-joint generalisation - internal forces cancel, external forces drive the motion - is exactly the bookkeeping the inverse-dynamics recursion of the next section performs one segment at a time.

6. Inverse dynamics: the Newton-Euler recursion

Inverse dynamics is the payoff: given the measured kinematics, the segment inertias, and the ground reaction, compute the net torque each joint must have produced. The method is to isolate one segment at a time as a free body and write Newton's and Euler's laws for it. Each segment has two unknowns we want - the force and the moment transmitted across its proximal joint - and everything else in its equations is known if we start where a force is known: the foot, pressing on the force plate. So the recursion marches distal to proximal, foot → shank → thigh, each segment handing the next the reaction it must react against.

Definition 6.1 (free body of one planar segment). Segment $i$ has mass $m_i$, centre-of-mass acceleration $\mathbf a_{c,i}$ (from Sections 3-4), moment of inertia $I_i$, and angular acceleration $\alpha_i$. At its distal joint act a known force $\mathbf F_d$ and moment $M_d$ (the ground reaction for the foot, or the reaction from the segment below for the rest); at its proximal joint act the unknown force $\mathbf F_p$ and net muscle moment $M_p$ (the joint moments carry a joint subscript; the bare $M$ used elsewhere is total body mass). Gravity $m_i\mathbf g$ acts at the centre of mass. Let $\mathbf r_d$ and $\mathbf r_p$ be the position vectors from the segment's centre of mass to its distal and proximal joints.
Proposition 6.1 (the inverse-dynamics recursion). Newton's and Euler's laws for segment $i$ solve for the proximal force and joint moment: $$\boxed{\;\mathbf F_p=m_i\,\mathbf a_{c,i}-m_i\mathbf g-\mathbf F_d,\qquad M_p=I_i\,\alpha_i-M_d-(\mathbf r_d\times\mathbf F_d)-(\mathbf r_p\times\mathbf F_p).\;}$$ Initialise at the foot with $\mathbf F_d=\mathbf F_{\rm GRF}$ and $M_d=\mathbf r_{\rm COP}\times\mathbf F_{\rm GRF}$ (the moment of the ground reaction about the foot's centre of mass, $\mathbf r_{\rm COP}$ the vector to the centre of pressure). By Newton's third law the proximal reaction of segment $i$ is the distal load of segment $i{+}1$: $\mathbf F_d^{(i+1)}=-\mathbf F_p^{(i)}$, $M_d^{(i+1)}=-M_p^{(i)}$. The net joint torque at each joint is the $M_p$ computed there.
Newton's second law for the segment sums the forces to $m_i\mathbf a_{c,i}$: $\mathbf F_p+\mathbf F_d+m_i\mathbf g=m_i\mathbf a_{c,i}$, which rearranges to $\mathbf F_p=m_i\mathbf a_{c,i}-m_i\mathbf g-\mathbf F_d$. Euler's law about the centre of mass sums the moments to $I_i\alpha_i$: the two joint moments $M_p$ and $M_d$, plus the moments of the two joint forces about the centre of mass, $\mathbf r_p\times\mathbf F_p$ and $\mathbf r_d\times\mathbf F_d$ (gravity acts at the centre of mass and contributes no moment). Thus $M_p+M_d+\mathbf r_p\times\mathbf F_p+\mathbf r_d\times\mathbf F_d=I_i\alpha_i$, giving $M_p=I_i\alpha_i-M_d-(\mathbf r_d\times\mathbf F_d)-(\mathbf r_p\times\mathbf F_p)$. Because $\mathbf F_p$ is already known from the Newton step, the moment equation has the single unknown $M_p$. The third-law coupling passes the solved proximal load down as the next segment's distal load, propagating the known ground reaction up the chain. ∎

A concrete instance closes the loop. Take the shank of the $M=70\ \mathrm{kg}$ subject from Section 2 ($m=3.3\ \mathrm{kg}$, $I=0.048\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}$, length $0.40\ \mathrm{m}$) at an instant of a squat where the measured shank COM acceleration is $\mathbf a_c=(0.4,\,1.2)\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$ and angular acceleration $\alpha=3.0\ \mathrm{rad/s^2}$, with a distal (ankle) reaction $\mathbf F_d=(15,\,690)\ \mathrm{N}$ and ankle moment $M_d=-8\ \mathrm{N\,m}$ handed up from the foot. The Newton step gives the knee reaction $\mathbf F_p=m\mathbf a_c-m\mathbf g-\mathbf F_d=(3.3\cdot0.4,\,3.3\cdot1.2+3.3\cdot9.81)-(15,690)=(-13.7,\,-653.7)\ \mathrm{N}$ - the thigh pushes down and slightly back on the shank, chiefly to support the load above the knee (with a smaller component decelerating the descent). The Euler step then returns the net knee moment, once the segment's pose fixes the moment arms $\mathbf r_p,\mathbf r_d$ (the vectors from the centre of mass to the knee and ankle, of length $0.17$ and $0.23\ \mathrm{m}$). Assume the shank is inclined at $75^\circ$ to the horizontal at this instant, knee end up and forward - a mid-squat pose. The unit vector along the shank from ankle to knee is $(\cos75^\circ,\sin75^\circ)=(0.259,\,0.966)$, so $\mathbf r_p=0.433\times0.40\,(0.2588,0.9659)=(0.0448,\,0.1673)\ \mathrm{m}$ and $\mathbf r_d=-0.567\times0.40\,(0.2588,0.9659)=(-0.0587,\,-0.2191)\ \mathrm{m}$, of lengths $0.173$ and $0.227\ \mathrm{m}$ as stated. In the plane a cross product is the scalar $\mathbf r\times\mathbf F=r_xF_y-r_yF_x$, so $\mathbf r_d\times\mathbf F_d=(-0.0587)(690)-(-0.2191)(15)=-37.22\ \mathrm{N\,m}$ and $\mathbf r_p\times\mathbf F_p=(0.0448)(-653.7)-(0.1673)(-13.7)=-27.01\ \mathrm{N\,m}$, while $I\alpha=0.048\times3.0=0.144\ \mathrm{N\,m}$. Hence$$M_p=I\alpha-M_d-(\mathbf r_d\times\mathbf F_d)-(\mathbf r_p\times\mathbf F_p)=0.144+8+37.22+27.01=72.4\ \mathrm{N\,m},$$a knee extensor moment of $72\ \mathrm{N\,m}$ - the very knee torque Module 13 used to analyse the chair rise, now produced from measured motion rather than assumed. Notice how small the inertial term $I\alpha=0.144\ \mathrm{N\,m}$ is beside the two force-moment terms, $37.22$ and $27.01$: at squat speeds a joint moment is almost entirely the moment of the joint forces about the segment centre, which is why an error in the measured $\mathbf F_d$ at the foot propagates almost undamped into every joint above it, and why the force plate must be the accurate instrument in the chain. Assembling this recursion into code, run on synthetic data whose true torques we know, is the subject of Section 7.

7. The measurement-to-torque pipeline

The pieces now assemble into one procedure. Given marker trajectories and a synchronised force plate, the estimate of a joint torque runs left to right through Fig. 1:

  1. Markers → angles. Segment angles by Definition 2.1, joint angles as their differences.
  2. Filter. Low-pass each angle at the cutoff $f_c^\star$ from a residual analysis (Section 4), zero-lag.
  3. Differentiate. Twice, for $\dot{\mathbf q}$ and $\ddot{\mathbf q}$, on the filtered angles.
  4. Inertias. Segment masses, COM locations, and moments of inertia from the anthropometric table (Section 2).
  5. Kinematics of the centres. Each segment's COM position and acceleration from the joint kinematics.
  6. Recursion. Newton-Euler from the foot (with the measured ground reaction) up the chain (Section 6).
  7. Output. The net joint torque at each joint - with an uncertainty band from Section 8.

The only way to test such a pipeline is on data whose answer is known. We forward-simulate a movement: pick a joint trajectory $q(t)$, choose the true torque from the equation of motion (here a single gravity-loaded segment, $\tau_{\rm true}=I\ddot q+mg\ell\sin q$), add realistic marker noise, and then run the inverse pipeline to see whether it returns $\tau_{\rm true}$. Here $\ell=\rho_iL_i$ is the distance from the joint axis to the segment centre of mass (Definition 2.2), not the segment length $L_i$; the two differ by the factor $\rho_i$ and confusing them misplaces the gravity term. The synthetic joint's numbers are assumed, not measured: $I=0.05\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}$, $m=4\ \mathrm{kg}$, $\ell=0.25\ \mathrm{m}$, a test motion $q(t)=0.40\sin(2\pi\cdot0.8\,t)\ \mathrm{rad}$ sampled at $f_s=100\ \mathrm{Hz}$, and $\sigma=0.006\ \mathrm{rad}$ of marker noise. They are round numbers bracketing the human shank of Section 2 ($m=3.3\ \mathrm{kg}$, $I=0.048\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}$, $\rho L=0.17\ \mathrm{m}$); every conclusion drawn below is about the shape of the error curves, and Section 8 is where the parameter dependence is priced. The Appendix records them. Figure 7 is that test, and it makes the module's central warning concrete.

With filtering, the recovered torque tracks the truth to a root-mean-square error of $0.07\ \mathrm{N\,m}$ on a signal peaking at $3.31\ \mathrm{N\,m}$ - a faithful estimate. Run on the same noisy data without the filter, the estimate has an error of $1.70\ \mathrm{N\,m}$, half the peak of the signal itself: the torque is buried in differentiation noise. Two details fix those numbers, and both matter for reproducing them. First, the pipeline differentiates by applying np.gradient twice, which is the wide stencil $(q_{k+2}-2q_k+q_{k-2})/(4\Delta t^2)$, not the compact $(q_{k+1}-2q_k+q_{k-1})/\Delta t^2$ that Proposition 3.1 analyses. The wide stencil straddles $2\Delta t$ on each side, so its noise variance is $6\sigma^2/(16\Delta t^4)$ - sixteen times smaller - and its unfiltered torque error is four times smaller than the $6.68\ \mathrm{N\,m}$ the compact stencil gives on the same data. The law of Proposition 3.1 is unchanged; only the constant depends on the stencil, which is why a scaling comparison must hold the stencil fixed. Second, the first and last eight samples are discarded, because the zero-lag filter leaves an edge transient there that no interior sample sees. The pipeline works only because Sections 3 and 4 tamed the derivative. The code is short enough to read in full, and it prints all four numbers just quoted.

import numpy as np
from scipy.signal import butter, filtfilt

FS, DT, SIG, G = 100.0, 0.01, 0.006, 9.81
IZ, MZ, LL = 0.05, 4.0, 0.25      # inertia, mass, joint-to-COM distance
A0, F0 = 0.40, 0.8                # test motion: amplitude, frequency
T = np.arange(0, 2, DT)
TRIM = slice(8, -8)               # drop filter edge transients


def smooth(x, fc, fs=FS):
    b, a = butter(2, fc/(fs/2))       # 2nd-order Butterworth
    return filtfilt(b, a, x)          # forward-backward: zero lag


def deriv(x, dt=DT):                  # np.gradient: central difference
    return np.gradient(x, dt)


def joint_torque(q, fc, Iz=IZ, m=MZ, L=LL):
    qf = q if fc is None else smooth(q, fc)   # 1-2. filter the angle
    qdd = deriv(deriv(qf))                    # 3.  differentiate twice
    return Iz*qdd + m*G*L*np.sin(qf)          # 4-7. inverse dynamics


def compact(x, dt=DT):                # the (1,-2,1) stencil of Prop 3.1
    d = np.zeros_like(x)
    d[1:-1] = (x[2:] - 2*x[1:-1] + x[:-2])/dt**2
    return d


def truth():
    q = A0*np.sin(2*np.pi*F0*T)
    qdd = -A0*(2*np.pi*F0)**2*np.sin(2*np.pi*F0*T)
    return q, IZ*qdd + MZ*G*LL*np.sin(q)


def rmse(a, b):
    return np.sqrt(np.mean((a[TRIM] - b[TRIM])**2))


q_true, tau_true = truth()
q_meas = q_true + np.random.default_rng(15).normal(0, SIG, T.size)
print("true peak torque   = %.2f N m" % np.abs(tau_true).max())
print("filtered (6 Hz)    = %.3f N m RMSE"
      % rmse(joint_torque(q_meas, 6.0), tau_true))
print("unfiltered         = %.2f N m RMSE"
      % rmse(joint_torque(q_meas, None), tau_true))
print("unfiltered, compact stencil = %.2f N m RMSE"
      % rmse(IZ*compact(q_meas) + MZ*G*LL*np.sin(q_meas), tau_true))

This is the whole idea in one screen of code: the general planar case (Section 6) replaces the last line with the Newton-Euler recursion over several segments, and adds the force plate as the foot's distal load, but the shape - filter, differentiate, apply dynamics - is identical. What it does not yet report is how far to trust the number, which is the next section.

8. Error and uncertainty propagation

A torque estimate without an error bar is not a measurement. Two kinds of error enter the pipeline, and they behave oppositely under filtering. Random error - marker noise - is broadband and is largely removed by the smoothing of Section 4; what survives is a residual variance. Systematic error - a body-segment parameter that is 15% too large because the subject is not the cadaver-average of the table - is a fixed scaling that no amount of filtering touches, because it is not noise. Quantifying both, and finding which dominates, is the job of error propagation.

Proposition 8.1 (variance of the torque estimate). Write the single-joint torque as $\tau=I\ddot q+mg\ell\sin q$, with $\ell$ the joint-to-COM distance of Section 7. For small, independent errors in the inputs, the estimate's variance is the sum of squared sensitivities times input variances, $$\boxed{\;\operatorname{Var}(\tau)\approx\Big(\tfrac{\partial\tau}{\partial I}\Big)^2\!\operatorname{Var}(I)+\Big(\tfrac{\partial\tau}{\partial m}\Big)^2\!\operatorname{Var}(m)+\Big(\tfrac{\partial\tau}{\partial\ddot q}\Big)^2\!\operatorname{Var}(\ddot q)+\cdots\;}$$ with $\partial\tau/\partial I=\ddot q$, $\partial\tau/\partial m=g\ell\sin q$, $\partial\tau/\partial\ddot q=I$. Independent sources add in quadrature; a systematic (correlated) error instead adds its full bias to every time sample.
Linearise $\tau=\tau(I,m,\ddot q,\dots)$ about the nominal inputs: a small perturbation gives $\delta\tau=\tfrac{\partial\tau}{\partial I}\delta I+\tfrac{\partial\tau}{\partial m}\delta m+\tfrac{\partial\tau}{\partial\ddot q}\delta\ddot q+\cdots$. Taking the variance and using that the input errors are mutually independent (so all cross-covariances vanish), $\operatorname{Var}(\tau)=\sum_j(\partial\tau/\partial x_j)^2\operatorname{Var}(x_j)$, the quadrature sum. The partials follow by differentiating $\tau=I\ddot q+mg\ell\sin q$ term by term. If an error is instead systematic - the same $\delta I$ on every frame - it is not random across samples: it shifts the whole torque curve by $\ddot q\,\delta I$, a bias that quadrature does not describe and filtering cannot remove. ∎

The quadrature formula is exact only for small, independent, Gaussian errors; when the errors are large or the model nonlinear, the reliable tool is Monte Carlo - draw many samples of the noisy markers and the uncertain parameters, run the whole pipeline on each, and read the spread of the output. Figure 8 does this for the synthetic joint.

The uncertainty band (Fig. 8, left) is the estimate's width; the error budget (right) says where it comes from. Each bar is the RMS torque error pooled over 20 000 draws and all interior samples, with one source active and the others switched off. The $15\%$ body-segment-parameter uncertainty contributes $0.414\ \mathrm{N\,m}$ (the listing of K7, seed 9; other seeds give $0.410$ to $0.418$): seven times the $0.059$ from marker noise at the working cutoff, and nearly twice the $0.228$ from a badly chosen cutoff (here $f_c=1.5\ \mathrm{Hz}$, over-smoothed by more than a factor of two below the optimum of Fig. 4). The filter's own bias at the working cutoff $f_c=6\ \mathrm{Hz}$ is $0.004$ and can be ignored. The last two need no averaging at all - with the noise off they are deterministic - and the first needs a closed form to confirm the Monte Carlo has settled: linearising in the parameter errors gives a pooled RMS of $u\sqrt{\langle(I\ddot q_f)^2+(mg\ell\sin q_f)^2\rangle}=0.415\ \mathrm{N\,m}$ at $u=15\%$, which the 20 000-draw bar reproduces. At the 600 draws a first pass might use, that bar still moves by $\pm0.02$ between seeds. The parameter term leads because it is a systematic scaling the filter cannot remove. This ranking is the practical payoff of the whole analysis: it says that to sharpen this torque estimate you should measure the subject's inertias better before you buy a faster camera - and it motivates estimating those parameters from the data itself, the subject of Section 9.

9. Parameter identification and model validation

Section 8 found the dominant error to be the body-segment parameters, taken from a population table rather than measured on the subject. But the data contain information about them: the same force plate that validated the COM (Section 5) constrains the masses, because a wrong mass makes the kinematic and force-plate accelerations disagree. Parameter identification turns that disagreement into an estimate - choose the parameters that make the model most consistent with the measurements.

Proposition 9.1 (least-squares parameter identification). Let a model output depend linearly on an unknown parameter vector $\boldsymbol\beta$ through $\mathbf y_{\rm model}=\mathbf A\boldsymbol\beta$, and let $\mathbf y_{\rm meas}$ be the corresponding measurements. The parameters minimising the squared residual $\lVert\mathbf y_{\rm meas}-\mathbf A\boldsymbol\beta\rVert^2$ satisfy the normal equations $$\boxed{\;\mathbf A^{\!\top}\mathbf A\,\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}=\mathbf A^{\!\top}\mathbf y_{\rm meas}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}=(\mathbf A^{\!\top}\mathbf A)^{-1}\mathbf A^{\!\top}\mathbf y_{\rm meas},\;}$$ the value at which the residual is orthogonal to every column of $\mathbf A$.
The squared residual $R(\boldsymbol\beta)=(\mathbf y_{\rm meas}-\mathbf A\boldsymbol\beta)^{\!\top}(\mathbf y_{\rm meas}-\mathbf A\boldsymbol\beta)$ is a convex quadratic in $\boldsymbol\beta$. Its gradient is $\nabla R=-2\mathbf A^{\!\top}(\mathbf y_{\rm meas}-\mathbf A\boldsymbol\beta)$; setting it to zero gives $\mathbf A^{\!\top}\mathbf A\,\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}=\mathbf A^{\!\top}\mathbf y_{\rm meas}$. Since $\mathbf A^{\!\top}\mathbf A$ is positive definite when $\mathbf A$ has independent columns, the stationary point is the unique minimum, and $\nabla R=0$ says exactly that the residual $\mathbf y_{\rm meas}-\mathbf A\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}$ is orthogonal to the column space of $\mathbf A$ - no direction in the model can reduce it further. ∎

Applied here, the residual is the dynamic inconsistency of Proposition 5.1: sweep a mass-scaling parameter and find the value that minimises the mean-square gap between the kinematic and force-plate COM accelerations (Fig. 9). More generally, the measured ground reaction and the measured kinematics are almost never perfectly consistent - the model is planar, the parameters approximate, the markers move on the skin - so their residual is nonzero. Residual-reduction methods (RRA) make the smallest adjustment to the masses, COM locations, or kinematics that drives the residual to zero, producing a dynamically consistent trajectory before the joint torques are read off.

Identification is not validation. A model can be tuned to fit its own residual and still be wrong, so the estimate must be checked against something it was not fitted to. Three independent checks are standard, in increasing strength: known static poses (hold a limb still and horizontal - the pipeline should return exactly the gravity torque $mg\ell$, with no dynamics); EMG timing (a muscle's activation should precede and share the sign of the torque it produces - Section 1 - so a computed knee-extensor torque during a period of silent quadriceps is a red flag); and the force-plate cross-check of Proposition 5.1, which uses an instrument the torque calculation did not. When a torque survives identification and these validations, it has earned the trust the rest of this course placed in it. The number the fourteen previous modules assumed is, at last, a measurement - with a value, an error bar, and a chain of evidence behind it.

Lab A - residual analysis chooses the cutoff

Physical question. Without knowing the true signal, how do you set the filter cutoff? Model. Winter's residual analysis (Section 4). Parameters. a $0.8\ \mathrm{Hz}$ motion sampled at $f_s=100\ \mathrm{Hz}$, marker noise $\sigma=0.006\ \mathrm{rad}$. Equations. residual $R(f_c)=\mathrm{RMS}(q-\mathrm{filter}_{f_c}(q))$; the knee is where $R$ departs from its noise-only line.

import numpy as np
from scipy.signal import butter, filtfilt

FS, DT, SIG = 100.0, 0.01, 0.006
T = np.arange(0, 2, DT)
q_true = 0.40*np.sin(2*np.pi*0.8*T)          # the motion under the noise
fcs = np.geomspace(1.5, 30, 30)
rng = np.random.default_rng(15)


def smooth(x, fc):
    b, a = butter(2, fc/(FS/2))
    return filtfilt(b, a, x)


knees = []
for _ in range(200):                          # 200 independent noise draws
    q_meas = q_true + rng.normal(0, SIG, T.size)
    resid = np.array([np.sqrt(np.mean((q_meas - smooth(q_meas, fc))**2))
                      for fc in fcs])
    tail = resid[fcs > 12].mean()             # the noise-only floor
    knees.append(fcs[np.argmax(resid < 1.5*tail)])
knees = np.array(knees)
print("chosen cutoff: median %.2f Hz, 5-95%% [%.2f, %.2f]"
      % (np.median(knees), *np.percentile(knees, [5, 95])))

Interpretation. The residual is flat above $\sim$5 Hz (the noise floor) and climbs steeply below it as signal is cut. Over 200 independent noise draws the knee has median $3.1\ \mathrm{Hz}$ with a 5-95% range of $2.8$ to $3.8\ \mathrm{Hz}$, found without ever using the true signal. That is the velocity optimum of Fig. 4 ($3.0\ \mathrm{Hz}$) to within the scatter of a single trial, and it sits just below the torque optimum Lab B computes ($3.4\ \mathrm{Hz}$). A single trial is worth reporting with that scatter attached: the knee rule reads one number off one noisy residual curve, and the number moves by a factor of $1.4$ between draws. Sensitivity. A noisier marker raises the flat tail, moving the knee slightly higher. Extension. Replace the fixed threshold with a two-line fit (noise line plus signal line) and take their intersection.

Lab B - the cutoff that best recovers the torque

Physical question. Is the angle-chosen cutoff also best for the torque? Model. the single-segment pipeline $\tau=I\ddot q+mg\ell\sin q$. Parameters. the assumed synthetic joint of Section 7: $I=0.05\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}$, $m=4\ \mathrm{kg}$, $\ell=0.25\ \mathrm{m}$. Equations. torque RMSE$(f_c)=\mathrm{RMS}(\hat\tau_{f_c}-\tau_{\rm true})$.

import numpy as np
from scipy.signal import butter, filtfilt

FS, DT, SIG, G = 100.0, 0.01, 0.006, 9.81
IZ, MZ, LL = 0.05, 4.0, 0.25
T = np.arange(0, 2, DT)
TRIM = slice(8, -8)
q_true = 0.40*np.sin(2*np.pi*0.8*T)
qdd_true = -0.40*(2*np.pi*0.8)**2*np.sin(2*np.pi*0.8*T)
tau_true = IZ*qdd_true + MZ*G*LL*np.sin(q_true)
fcs = np.geomspace(1.5, 30, 30)


def pipeline(q, fc, Iz=IZ, m=MZ):
    b, a = butter(2, fc/(FS/2))
    qf = q if fc is None else filtfilt(b, a, q)
    return Iz*np.gradient(np.gradient(qf, DT), DT) + m*G*LL*np.sin(qf)


def pooled_rmse(fc, n=300, seed=5):           # RMS over draws AND samples
    r = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    s = 0.0
    for _ in range(n):
        q_meas = q_true + r.normal(0, SIG, T.size)
        s += np.mean((pipeline(q_meas, fc)[TRIM] - tau_true[TRIM])**2)
    return np.sqrt(s/n)


errs = np.array([pooled_rmse(fc) for fc in fcs])
j = int(np.argmin(errs))
print("torque-optimal cutoff = %.1f Hz (RMSE %.3f N m)" % (fcs[j], errs[j]))
for fc in (1.5, 20.0, 30.0):
    print("  fc = %4.1f Hz -> RMSE %.2f N m"
          % (fc, np.interp(fc, fcs, errs)))

Interpretation. The torque RMSE is U-shaped and bottoms at $3.4\ \mathrm{Hz}$ ($0.022\ \mathrm{N\,m}$), rising to $0.23\ \mathrm{N\,m}$ at $1.5\ \mathrm{Hz}$ and $1.34\ \mathrm{N\,m}$ at $30\ \mathrm{Hz}$. It lies a little above Lab A's angle knee ($3.1\ \mathrm{Hz}$) and above the velocity optimum of Fig. 4 ($3.0\ \mathrm{Hz}$) - the opposite of the ordering one expects from "each derivative is noisier". The reason is the split of $\tau=I\ddot q+mg\ell\sin q$: for this joint the gravity term peaks at $3.82\ \mathrm{N\,m}$ against the inertial term's $0.51$, so seven eighths of the torque is a function of the angle, not of its second derivative. Over-smoothing therefore damages the torque through a term that is never differentiated: at $f_c=1.5\ \mathrm{Hz}$ the gravity term contributes $0.24\ \mathrm{N\,m}$ of bias against the inertial term's $0.08$. The general rule is that the optimum follows the dominant term of the dynamics, not the highest derivative in it. Sensitivity. Higher marker noise raises the whole curve and moves the optimum down, toward more smoothing. Extension. Rerun with $I=0.5\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}$ so the inertial term leads, and confirm that the torque optimum then falls below the velocity optimum.

Lab D - identifying a parameter from the data

Physical question. Can the dominant unknown, body mass, be recovered from the trial itself? Model. least-squares on the dynamic residual (Section 9). Parameters. true $M=70\ \mathrm{kg}$, plate noise $2.5\ \mathrm{N}$, growing data spans. Equations. $\hat M=\arg\min_M \mathrm{RMS}\big(\ddot y^{\rm kin}-(F_y/M-g)\big)$.

import numpy as np

G, DT, M_TRUE = 9.81, 0.01, 70.0
T = np.arange(0, 2, DT)
a_true = -0.35*np.pi**2*np.cos(np.pi*T)/2.0    # squat COM accel
rng = np.random.default_rng(15)
Fy_plate = M_TRUE*(G + a_true) + rng.normal(0, 2.5, T.size)
a_com_kin = a_true + rng.normal(0, 0.12, T.size)
Ms = np.linspace(60, 80, 801)                  # 0.025 kg grid


def identify(a_kin, Fy):
    res = [np.sqrt(np.mean((a_kin - (Fy/M - G))**2)) for M in Ms]
    return Ms[int(np.argmin(res))]


print("COM accel peak = %.2f m/s2" % np.abs(a_true).max())
for N in (20, 60, 100, 200):
    print("N=%3d  identified M = %.2f kg  (predicted s.e. %.2f kg)"
          % (N, identify(a_com_kin[:N], Fy_plate[:N]),
             0.12*M_TRUE**2/(M_TRUE*G)/np.sqrt(N)))
still = np.zeros(200)
Fy_still = M_TRUE*G + rng.normal(0, 2.5, 200)
print("static hold (no acceleration at all): M = %.2f kg"
      % identify(still + rng.normal(0, 0.12, 200), Fy_still))

Interpretation. The identified mass is $70.03\ \mathrm{kg}$ from the first 20 frames and $70.00$ from 200. It does not have to converge, because it starts converged, and the tempting explanation for why a trial is needed at all is wrong: the residual $\ddot y^{\rm kin}-(F_y/M-g)$ is not blind to $M$ at rest. Hold still and $F_y=M_{\rm true}\,g$, so the residual is $g(1-M_{\rm true}/M)$, which vanishes only at $M=M_{\rm true}$; run the identification on a static hold with no acceleration anywhere in it and the code returns $69.97\ \mathrm{kg}$. With $g$ known, a force plate is a weighing scale and total body mass is over-determined by a single frame of it. What more data buys is precision: the standard error of $\hat M$ is $\sigma_a M/(g\sqrt N)$, falling from $0.19\ \mathrm{kg}$ at $N=20$ to $0.06$ at $N=200$. What genuinely needs acceleration is the mass distribution - the fractions $f_i$ and COM fractions $\rho_i$ - because at rest those enter the measurements only through the single weighted sum $\sum_if_i\mathbf r_{c,i}$, one equation for many unknowns, whereas accelerating segments enter with different time courses and separate them. Sensitivity. Halving the kinematic COM acceleration noise halves the standard error at every $N$; halving the plate noise changes almost nothing, because the plate is already the accurate side. Extension. Identify the segment mass fractions jointly by regressing the per-segment residual against segment accelerations - the vector least squares of Proposition 9.1 - and confirm that a static trial leaves that design matrix rank-deficient, which is the precise sense in which movement is required.

11. Problems, diagnostics, and limitations

Jump to: Conceptual · Derivational · Computational · Diagnostics · Limitations · Repayment

Thirty problems - ten conceptual, ten derivational, ten computational - plus five diagnostics. Each carries a Probes note and a collapsible worked solution. Computational solutions quote only numbers printed by a run: by the code of Section 7, by a lab of Section 10, or by the solution's own listing.

Conceptual ↑ contents

C1 - the number you never measured. The course spent fourteen modules computing joint torques. Why can no instrument measure one directly, and what must replace the measurement? Probes: state vs observation; Section 0.
Solution
Torque is an internal generalised force; instruments report externally observable quantities - marker positions, contact forces, muscle voltages - none of which is a torque. What replaces the measurement is inference: a model (the observation map plus the equations of motion) combined with the observable data lets you solve for the torque as an inverse problem. The torque is computed, never read, so it inherits the error of both the data and the model.
C2 - the odd instrument out. Of the five mechanical instruments, why is the force plate both the exception and the most valuable for inverse dynamics? Probes: force vs position measurement; Section 1.
Solution
Every other instrument measures a position (or, for the IMU, an acceleration) that must be differentiated or integrated into the dynamics; the force plate measures a force directly and with very low noise. Since inverse dynamics is anchored where a force is known, the clean ground reaction is the boundary condition that starts the recursion - the one place the chain touches a measured kinetic quantity rather than a processed kinematic one.
C3 - invisible noise, visible ruin. Position noise too small to see in the angle wrecks the acceleration. Why? Probes: differentiation as a high-pass operation; Section 3.
Solution
Differentiation multiplies each frequency component by its frequency, so it amplifies the high-frequency content where broadband noise lives while barely touching the low-frequency signal. The angle is signal-dominated (low frequency) and looks clean; its second derivative is noise-dominated (high frequency) because the same noise has been multiplied by frequency twice. The eye judges the angle; the dynamics need its derivatives.
C4 - faster is worse. Buying a faster camera (smaller $\Delta t$) makes the raw finite-difference acceleration noisier. Explain the paradox. Probes: the $1/\Delta t^2$ scaling; Section 3.
Solution
By Proposition 3.1 the second-difference acceleration noise variance is $6\sigma^2/\Delta t^4$, so its standard deviation scales as $1/\Delta t^2$: halving $\Delta t$ multiplies the noise variance by 16 (the amplitude by 4). A faster camera samples the same spatial noise over a shorter interval, and dividing a fixed position error by a smaller time step inflates the derivative. Speed helps the signal bandwidth but hurts the raw derivative; the resolution is not slower sampling but proper smoothing.
C5 - not as smooth as possible. Why is there a best cutoff frequency, rather than "smooth until it looks clean"? Probes: bias vs variance; Section 4.
Solution
Smoothing trades two errors. Too little and noise floods the derivative (high variance); too much and the filter attenuates the real motion (high bias). The total mean-square error is their sum (Proposition 4.1), a U with an interior minimum. "As smooth as possible" walks off the low-cutoff end into large bias - a torque whose peaks are flattened - which looks clean but is systematically wrong. The goal is the minimum, found by residual analysis.
C6 - why start at the foot. The Newton-Euler recursion runs distal to proximal, beginning at the foot. Why there, and why that direction? Probes: the known boundary condition; Section 6.
Solution
Each segment's equations have two unknowns - the proximal joint force and moment - and are solvable only if the distal load is already known. The one segment with a measured distal load is the foot, whose distal "load" is the force-plate ground reaction. Solving it yields the ankle load, which by Newton's third law is the shank's distal load, and so on up the chain. The direction is forced by where the known force is.
C7 - two instruments, one truth. Why does agreement between the kinematic and force-plate COM accelerations validate the pipeline, when both could be wrong? Probes: independence of the instruments; Section 5.
Solution
The two estimates come from physically independent instruments (cameras vs a force plate) and independent error sources (marker noise and body-segment parameters vs plate calibration). Newton's second law says they must equal the same acceleration, so their agreement is unlikely to be a coincidence of two independent errors cancelling. A large residual, conversely, flags that at least one side has an error - a genuine, assumption-light check.
C8 - the error filtering cannot touch. Why does a $15\%$ error in a segment mass survive any amount of smoothing? Probes: systematic vs random error; Section 8.
Solution
Filtering removes error that is random across time (noise, spread over frequency); a wrong mass is a fixed multiplier applied identically to every frame, so it is not noise and has no high-frequency signature to remove. It biases the whole torque curve by a constant factor. The only cures are measuring or identifying the parameter (Section 9), not smoothing - which is why the error budget ranks body-segment parameters above marker noise.
C9 - the instrument that stays outside. EMG measures the muscles that make the torque, yet never enters the torque calculation. Why - and what is it for? Probes: activation vs force; Section 1, Section 9.
Solution
Surface EMG tracks muscle activation, but the map from activation to force depends on the muscle's length, velocity, and fatigue (Module 5), so EMG is not a calibrated force sensor and cannot be inserted into the dynamics. Its value is as an independent check on timing and sign: a computed extensor torque during a period of silent extensors is a contradiction, so EMG validates the inverse-dynamics result and helps resolve muscle redundancy without ever supplying a number to it.
C10 - the pose that checks everything. Holding a limb still and horizontal is the simplest validation of the whole pipeline. Why is it so powerful? Probes: the static limit; Section 9.
Solution
At rest all accelerations are zero, so the inverse-dynamics equation collapses to the pure gravity torque $mg\ell$ about the joint - a value fixed by known anthropometry and geometry alone, with no differentiation involved. If the pipeline returns anything else in a static hold, the error is in the parameters or the geometry, not the smoothing, isolating one class of error cleanly. It is the estimation analogue of checking a scale reads zero when empty.

Derivational ↑ contents

D1 - segment angle from markers. From proximal and distal marker positions, derive the segment angle and the knee joint angle, and state when the formula is ill-conditioned. Probes: the observation map; Section 2.
Solution
The vector from proximal to distal marker is $(\Delta x,\Delta y)=(x_d-x_p,\,y_d-y_p)$; its direction is $\theta=\operatorname{atan2}(\Delta y,\Delta x)$, using atan2 to cover all four quadrants. The knee angle is the difference of adjacent segment angles, $q_{\rm knee}=\theta_{\rm thigh}-\theta_{\rm shank}$. It is ill-conditioned when the markers are close together (small separation $d=\sqrt{\Delta x^2+\Delta y^2}$): marker noise then produces a large angular error, $\delta\theta\approx\delta(\text{position})/d$ - the reason markers are placed far apart on a segment.
D2 - velocity noise. Derive the noise variance of the central-difference velocity. Probes: variance of a linear combination; Section 3.
Solution
Write $\dot q_k=(q_{k+1}-q_{k-1})/(2\Delta t)$; its noise is $(\varepsilon_{k+1}-\varepsilon_{k-1})/(2\Delta t)$. With independent $\varepsilon$ of variance $\sigma^2$, the variance of a weighted sum is $\sigma^2\sum a_j^2=\sigma^2[(1/2\Delta t)^2+(1/2\Delta t)^2]=\sigma^2/(2\Delta t^2)$. The cross term vanishes because $\operatorname{Cov}(\varepsilon_{k+1},\varepsilon_{k-1})=0$. Hence $\operatorname{Var}(\dot q_k)=\sigma^2/(2\Delta t^2)$ (Proposition 3.1).
D3 - acceleration noise. Derive the noise variance of the second-difference acceleration and compare its growth to the velocity's. Probes: repeated differencing; Section 3.
Solution
$\ddot q_k=(q_{k+1}-2q_k+q_{k-1})/\Delta t^2$ has weights $(1,-2,1)/\Delta t^2$, so $\sum a_j^2=(1+4+1)/\Delta t^4=6/\Delta t^4$ and $\operatorname{Var}(\ddot q_k)=6\sigma^2/\Delta t^4$. Its standard deviation scales as $1/\Delta t^2$, versus $1/\Delta t$ for the velocity: each extra derivative multiplies the noise growth by another factor of $1/\Delta t$, which is why acceleration is the most fragile quantity in the pipeline.
D4 - bias-variance. Show that the mean-square error of a smoothed-then-differentiated estimate splits into a bias-squared and a variance term. Probes: independence of signal distortion and noise; Section 4.
Solution
Write the estimate as $\hat{\dot q}=\dot q+b+\nu$, with $b=\mathbb E[\hat{\dot q}]-\dot q$ the deterministic distortion and $\nu$ the zero-mean random part. Then $\mathrm{MSE}=\mathbb E[(b+\nu)^2]=b^2+2b\,\mathbb E[\nu]+\mathbb E[\nu^2]$; since $\mathbb E[\nu]=0$ and $b$ is deterministic, the cross term drops and $\mathrm{MSE}=b^2+\operatorname{Var}(\nu)=B^2+V$. Bias grows as the cutoff falls (more signal removed), variance grows as it rises (more noise passed); their sum has an interior minimum (Proposition 4.1).
D5 - the centre of mass. Derive the whole-body COM from the segment centres and show the total mass cancels. Probes: mass-weighted mean; Section 5.
Solution
By definition $\mathbf r_{\rm COM}=(\sum_i m_i\mathbf r_{c,i})/(\sum_i m_i)$. Writing $m_i=f_iM$ with mass fractions $f_i$, the total mass $M$ factors out of numerator and denominator: $\mathbf r_{\rm COM}=(M\sum_i f_i\mathbf r_{c,i})/(M\sum_i f_i)=\sum_i f_i\mathbf r_{c,i}$, using $\sum_i f_i=1$. The COM position thus depends only on the mass fractions, not the absolute mass - which is why identifying total mass (Lab D) needs the dynamics, not the geometry.
D6 - the force-plate identity. Derive $M\ddot{\mathbf r}_{\rm COM}=\mathbf F_{\rm GRF}-M\mathbf g$ by summing over segments. Probes: internal forces cancel; Section 5.
Solution
Sum Newton's law $m_i\ddot{\mathbf r}_{c,i}=\sum\mathbf F^{\rm ext}_i$ over all segments. External forces are gravity $m_i\mathbf g$, the ground reaction (only on the foot), and internal joint forces; each internal force appears twice (action-reaction) and cancels in the sum. The left side is $M\ddot{\mathbf r}_{\rm COM}$; the right is $\mathbf F_{\rm GRF}+\sum_i m_i\mathbf g=\mathbf F_{\rm GRF}-M\mathbf g$. This is Proposition 5.1, the basis of the force-plate cross-check.
D7 - the Newton step. Derive the proximal joint force on a segment from Newton's second law. Probes: free-body force balance; Section 6.
Solution
The forces on the segment are the proximal reaction $\mathbf F_p$, the known distal reaction $\mathbf F_d$, and gravity $m_i\mathbf g$. Newton's second law sums them to $m_i\mathbf a_{c,i}$: $\mathbf F_p+\mathbf F_d+m_i\mathbf g=m_i\mathbf a_{c,i}$, so $\mathbf F_p=m_i\mathbf a_{c,i}-m_i\mathbf g-\mathbf F_d$. With $\mathbf a_{c,i}$ from the kinematics, the inertia from anthropometry, and $\mathbf F_d$ from the segment below, the proximal force is fully determined (Proposition 6.1).
D8 - the Euler step. Derive the net proximal joint moment about the segment centre of mass. Probes: moment balance including force moments; Section 6.
Solution
Euler's law about the centre of mass sums all moments to $I_i\alpha_i$: the two joint moments $M_p$ and $M_d$, and the moments of the two joint forces, $\mathbf r_p\times\mathbf F_p$ and $\mathbf r_d\times\mathbf F_d$ (gravity acts at the COM, no moment). Thus $M_p+M_d+\mathbf r_p\times\mathbf F_p+\mathbf r_d\times\mathbf F_d=I_i\alpha_i$, giving $M_p=I_i\alpha_i-M_d-(\mathbf r_d\times\mathbf F_d)-(\mathbf r_p\times\mathbf F_p)$. Since $\mathbf F_p$ is already known from D7, this has the single unknown $M_p$, the net joint torque.
D9 - torque error bars. Derive the variance of the single-joint torque estimate and its sensitivity coefficients. Probes: linear error propagation; Section 8.
Solution
Linearise $\tau=I\ddot q+mg\ell\sin q$ (with $\ell$ the joint-to-COM distance, not the segment length): $\delta\tau=\ddot q\,\delta I+g\ell\sin q\,\delta m+I\,\delta\ddot q+\cdots$. Taking the variance with independent errors kills the cross-covariances, leaving $\operatorname{Var}(\tau)=\ddot q^2\operatorname{Var}(I)+(g\ell\sin q)^2\operatorname{Var}(m)+I^2\operatorname{Var}(\ddot q)+\cdots$, the quadrature sum of Proposition 8.1. The partials $\partial\tau/\partial I=\ddot q$, $\partial\tau/\partial m=g\ell\sin q$, $\partial\tau/\partial\ddot q=I$ show the inertial term dominates during fast movement (large $\ddot q$) and the gravitational term during slow, extended postures.
D10 - least squares. Derive the normal equations for the parameter that minimises the model residual. Probes: setting the gradient to zero; Section 9.
Solution
Minimise $R(\boldsymbol\beta)=\lVert\mathbf y-\mathbf A\boldsymbol\beta\rVert^2=(\mathbf y-\mathbf A\boldsymbol\beta)^{\!\top}(\mathbf y-\mathbf A\boldsymbol\beta)$. The gradient is $\nabla R=-2\mathbf A^{\!\top}(\mathbf y-\mathbf A\boldsymbol\beta)$; setting it to zero gives $\mathbf A^{\!\top}\mathbf A\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}=\mathbf A^{\!\top}\mathbf y$, so $\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}=(\mathbf A^{\!\top}\mathbf A)^{-1}\mathbf A^{\!\top}\mathbf y$. Positive-definiteness of $\mathbf A^{\!\top}\mathbf A$ (independent columns) makes it the unique minimum, and $\nabla R=0$ states the residual is orthogonal to the model's column space (Proposition 9.1).

Computational ↑ contents

Each requires a sweep, an inverse solve, a simulation, or a regime comparison - not substitution into a boxed formula. Numbers quoted are those the code produces.

K1 - raw versus filtered noise across sampling rates (sensitivity + regime). Sweep the sampling rate from 50 to 400 Hz and compute the acceleration noise two ways - raw finite difference, and after low-pass filtering at a fixed cutoff - and explain the opposite conclusions they reach about buying a faster camera. Probes: the raw scaling law versus the filter decoupling it, and holding one stencil fixed across a comparison; Section 3, Section 4.
Solution
Both halves of the comparison must use the same stencil or the ratios are meaningless, so use the pipeline's own: np.gradient twice, whose raw noise standard deviation is $\sigma\sqrt6/(4\Delta t^2)$. It climbs from $9.2\ \mathrm{rad/s^2}$ at $50\ \mathrm{Hz}$ to $588$ at $400\ \mathrm{Hz}$ - a $64\times$ rise, the $1/\Delta t^2$ law of Proposition 3.1 (the compact stencil of that proposition gives four times these figures and the same $64\times$), and the naive conclusion that a faster camera is worse. But run the same data through a fixed $7\ \mathrm{Hz}$ low-pass before differentiating and the acceleration error does not merely stay flat, it falls: $2.04\ \mathrm{rad/s^2}$ at $50\ \mathrm{Hz}$, $1.97$ at $100$, $1.56$ at $200$, $1.13$ at $400$, which is $5\times$ to $519\times$ smaller than the raw value. The non-obvious result the sweep surfaces: after filtering, what survives is set by the filter's passband and the noise density, not by $f_s$, and a faster camera puts more independent samples inside that passband, so it helps a little instead of hurting. The raw law says slower is better; the filtered pipeline reverses it. Note the residual $\sim2\ \mathrm{rad/s^2}$ does not go to zero with $f_s$, because it is dominated by the filter bias on a signal whose true acceleration peaks at $10.1\ \mathrm{rad/s^2}$, not by the noise.

    for _ in range(60):

K2 - finding the cutoff two ways (optimisation + inverse). On one synthetic trial, find the cutoff by residual analysis (no truth used) and by minimising the true velocity error, and compare. Probes: data-driven vs truth-driven optima; Section 4.
Solution
Residual analysis (Lab A) locates the knee at a median $3.1\ \mathrm{Hz}$ over 200 noise draws, without ever using the truth; minimising the RMS velocity error against the known signal (Fig. 4) bottoms at $3.0\ \mathrm{Hz}$ - $3.02$ on the listing's grid, at $0.0205\ \mathrm{rad/s}$, of which bias contributes $0.0132$ and noise $0.0156$. The two agree to well within the knee's own trial-to-trial scatter (5-95% range $2.8$ to $3.8\ \mathrm{Hz}$), and the agreement is not a coincidence: the motion is narrowband, so the frequency at which the residual curve departs from its noise floor is the same frequency at which the filter starts to bite into the signal, which is what makes the bias term grow. The torque optimum (Lab B, $3.4\ \mathrm{Hz}$) sits a little above both, so the angle-based knee is mildly conservative for the torque. The surrogate works here; it is not guaranteed to, and the guarantee fails as soon as the motion has real content near the noise floor, where the departure point stops marking the bias knee. The listing runs both searches on the same signal and prints both answers; most of its run time is the 200-draw noise term of Fig. 4.

K3 - three cutoffs for the torque (regime). Recover the joint torque at an over-smoothed, an optimal, and an under-smoothed cutoff, and compare the errors. Probes: the U-shaped torque error; Section 7, Lab B.
Solution
Sweeping the cutoff and comparing recovered torque to truth, the RMS torque error is $U$-shaped and bottoms at $3.4\ \mathrm{Hz}$, where Lab B's listing prints $0.022\ \mathrm{N\,m}$ on a signal peaking at $3.31\ \mathrm{N\,m}$. Either side of that the error is an order of magnitude worse: $0.23\ \mathrm{N\,m}$ at $1.5\ \mathrm{Hz}$, where the filter flattens the peaks, and $0.74\ \mathrm{N\,m}$ at $20\ \mathrm{Hz}$, where the second difference lets the marker noise back in - ten and thirty times the optimum. The figure runs the three cutoffs on one trial and lays the recovered torques over one cycle of the truth: the over-smoothed curve is a smooth, shrunken copy, the under-smoothed one straddles the truth inside a high-frequency fringe, and the optimal one is not separable from it at this scale. The three regimes make concrete that neither extreme is acceptable - and that the torque optimum sits above the velocity optimum of K2 ($3.0\ \mathrm{Hz}$), not below it. The reason is which term dominates, not which derivative is highest: the torque here is mostly its gravity term $mg\ell\sin q$, a function of the angle itself, so smoothing the angle costs more than the extra noise a higher cutoff admits.
K4 - recover the torque, with and without filtering (simulation). From noisy synthetic markers, run the full pipeline both ways and quantify the error against the known torque. Probes: the payoff of smoothing; Section 7.
Solution
Forward-simulate $\tau_{\rm true}=I\ddot q+mg\ell\sin q$, add $\sigma=0.006\ \mathrm{rad}$ marker noise, and invert with the code of Section 7. The filtered pipeline recovers the torque to an RMS error of $0.07\ \mathrm{N\,m}$; the identical data run without filtering gives $1.70\ \mathrm{N\,m}$ - half the $3.31\ \mathrm{N\,m}$ peak of the signal itself, and with the compact second-difference stencil of Proposition 3.1 it would be $6.68$, twice the peak. The unfiltered estimate is not a degraded answer but noise: the simulation shows the smoothing step is not a refinement but a precondition for any usable torque.
K5 - reading the dynamic residual (simulation + regime). Introduce a mass error and a smoothing error separately, and show the dynamic residual distinguishes them. Probes: diagnosing the pipeline; Section 5.
Solution
Compute the force-plate residual $\mathbf d=\ddot{\mathbf r}_{\rm COM}^{\rm kin}-(\mathbf F_{\rm GRF}/M-\mathbf g)$ under two faults. A $15\%$ mass error makes the residual $0.13\,(g+a)$, a coherent, nearly constant offset of about $1.3\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$ ($0.13\,g$) that follows the plate force, while under-smoothing makes it a high-frequency jitter independent of the motion. The two signatures are distinguishable by eye and by their frequency content, so the residual is not just a pass/fail number but a diagnostic that localises the fault - the practical use of Proposition 5.1.
K6 - the two-segment recursion (simulation). Run the Newton-Euler recursion over foot and shank from a measured ground reaction up to the knee moment, and compare to the known truth. Probes: the recursion in code; Section 6.
Solution
Initialise with the ground reaction as the foot's distal load and march up: solve the foot for the ankle load, pass its negative to the shank, and solve the shank for the knee force and moment (Proposition 6.1). On synthetic data whose true torques are known, the recovered knee moment matches the truth to within the differentiation error of K4. The exercise shows the recursion is a short loop, and that each joint's estimate depends on every measurement below it - so an error at the foot propagates all the way up.
K7 - the error budget (sensitivity). Monte Carlo the pipeline under marker noise and a $15\%$ body-segment-parameter uncertainty, and rank the sources of torque error. Probes: where the uncertainty comes from; Section 8.
Solution
Running the pipeline over 20 000 draws with one source active at a time, and pooling the squared error over draws and interior samples, the RMS torque error is $0.414\ \mathrm{N\,m}$ for a $15\%$ body-segment-parameter uncertainty, $0.228$ for a cutoff set at $1.5\ \mathrm{Hz}$ instead of the working $6\ \mathrm{Hz}$, $0.059$ for marker noise, and $0.004$ for the filter's own bias at the working cutoff. The statistic has to be stated with the number: averaging the per-draw RMSE instead of pooling reports $0.33$ for the parameter term, because the mean of $|\delta m|$ is $\sqrt{2/\pi}$ times its standard deviation. Same data, different statistic, and the ranking is unchanged. The parameter error dominates because it is systematic - a scaling the filter cannot remove.

K8 - identify the mass (inverse). Recover total body mass from the dynamic residual across growing spans of a trial, and study the convergence. Probes: least-squares identification; Section 9, Lab D.
Solution
Minimising $\mathrm{RMS}(\ddot y^{\rm kin}-(F_y/M-g))$ over $M$ (Proposition 9.1) returns the mass at which the two instruments agree. It returns $70.03\ \mathrm{kg}$ from 20 frames and $70.00$ from 200, against a true $70$. The convergence to watch is not in the estimate but in its standard error $\sigma_a M/(g\sqrt N)$, which falls from $0.19\ \mathrm{kg}$ to $0.06$ over that range. Total mass is not the hard case: with $g$ known the static reading $F_y=Mg$ already determines it, and the identification run on a motionless hold returns $69.97\ \mathrm{kg}$. Acceleration is what identifies the mass distribution - segment fractions and COM locations - because at rest those enter only through the single weighted sum $\sum_if_i\mathbf r_{c,i}$ and cannot be separated. An inverse problem is well-posed for a parameter exactly when the motion excites the term that parameter multiplies, and gravity already excites the term total mass multiplies.
K9 - the noise-versus-parameter crossover (sensitivity). Vary the marker noise and the body-segment-parameter uncertainty independently and find where each dominates the torque error. Probes: two error regimes; Section 8.
Solution
Torque error from marker noise grows with $\sigma$ but is suppressed by filtering; error from body-segment parameters grows linearly with their uncertainty and is unfiltered. Sweeping both, the marker-noise contribution overtakes the parameter contribution only past $\sigma^\star=0.042\ \mathrm{rad}$ ($2.4^\circ$, seven times the noise of a working marker set) or below $u^\star=2.1\%$ parameter uncertainty - the same crossover K10 reaches from the ratio $15\%\times(0.059/0.414)$. At the operating point ($\sigma=0.006\ \mathrm{rad}=0.34^\circ$, parameters $15\%$) the parameters dominate by a factor of seven, which locates the working point firmly in the systematic-error regime and shows K7's ranking is not incidental to the particular numbers: it would take a seven-fold worse marker set, or a seven-fold better subject model, to reverse it. The listing sweeps both axes and prints the two crossings.

K10 - spending a measurement budget (optimisation). Given a fixed budget divisible between reducing marker noise and measuring subject-specific inertias, find the allocation that minimises torque error. Probes: allocating effort to the dominant source; Section 8, Section 9.
Solution
Model the total torque error as the quadrature sum $\sqrt{V_{\rm noise}(\sigma)+B_{\rm bsp}^2(u)}$ and let the budget buy either smaller $\sigma$ or smaller parameter uncertainty $u$ at given marginal rates. Because the parameter term enters as an un-filterable bias and starts as the larger contributor ($0.414$ vs $0.059\ \mathrm{N\,m}$), the first units of budget bought on subject-specific inertias reduce total error far more than the same spent on markers. Both terms are linear in their uncertainty near the operating point, so the crossover is explicit: the parameter contribution falls to the marker contribution when $u^\star=15\%\times(0.059/0.414)=2.1\%$, which is what K9's listing prints when it sweeps $u$ directly rather than assuming linearity. The $2.1\%$ is where the two terms are equal; it is not where the best allocation switches, because that depends on the cost of each reduction. With the assumed cost model of the figure, in which each term falls in proportion to its spend, the total $\sqrt{(0.059(1-x))^2+(0.414x)^2}$ is smallest at $x=0.059^2/(0.059^2+0.414^2)=0.020$, that is $2\%$ of the budget on markers and $98\%$ on inertias, for an error of $0.058\ \mathrm{N\,m}$. Spending on markers repays more only after $98\%$ of the budget is on the inertias, and $2.1\%$ on a segment mass is beyond what regression tables or a scale can deliver, so in practice the budget stays on the inertias. The allocation confirms quantitatively the qualitative lesson of K7 - fix the dominant, systematic source first.

Five diagnostics ↑ contents

  1. The high-pass nature of differentiation. Why does differentiating amplify noise more than the signal?
    Solution
    Because it scales each frequency by its value, boosting the high-frequency noise and leaving the low-frequency signal nearly untouched.
  2. Zero-lag filtering. Why filter forward and backward rather than once?
    Solution
    A single pass imposes a phase lag that misaligns the torque with the synchronised force plate; running the filter both ways cancels the lag exactly.
  3. The static check. What torque should the pipeline return for a limb held still and horizontal?
    Solution
    Exactly the gravity torque $mg\ell$, since all accelerations are zero; any other value flags a parameter or geometry error.
  4. Anchoring the recursion. Could inverse dynamics start at the head instead of the foot?
    Solution
    Only if a force were measured there; the recursion must begin where a distal load is known, which is the foot on the force plate (or a free distal end with zero load).
  5. Systematic vs random. Which error does averaging many trials reduce, and which does it not?
    Solution
    Averaging reduces random marker noise (it cancels across trials) but not a systematic body-segment-parameter error, which repeats identically every trial.

Limitations - what this pipeline idealises

The planar inverse-dynamics pipeline is a deliberate simplification. Each idealisation is a real effect it omits, repaid elsewhere in the course or left as a known caveat.
