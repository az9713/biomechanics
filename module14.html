0. Motivation - aging as parameter drift

"Getting old" is not a mechanical explanation. To say a person can no longer rise from a low chair, or fractures a hip in a fall that would once have left only a bruise, or aches climbing stairs, is to describe outcomes - and outcomes are what Module 13 showed are decided by a few measurable quantities: a joint torque, a spinal load, a balance margin, a tissue stress. This module makes aging quantitative by identifying the parameters whose slow change produces those outcomes, and it finds that a short list does most of the work (Fig. 1).

Muscle force falls (sarcopenia). Bone mineral density falls (osteoporosis). Cartilage thins (osteoarthritis). Reflexes slow and sensors grow noisy (sensorimotor aging). Tendons stiffen. Each is a number that drifts - typically about one percent a year past midlife - and each drift shrinks a specific mechanical reserve. The power of the parameter view is that the same everyday task, analysed in Module 13, becomes a threshold on one of these numbers: the chair rise fails when knee torque drops below the demand, the fall fractures when impact force exceeds bone strength, the knee hurts when contact stress passes what the cartilage tolerates. Aging is the crossing of thresholds by drifting parameters (Fig. 2).

Two features make this more than a catalogue of decline. First, the parameters are coupled: weak muscles both fail tasks directly and, by loading bone less, accelerate its loss; a slow reflex both misses stumbles and, by curtailing activity, wastes muscle. Aging is a linked drift, not a set of independent failures, which is why frailty arrives as a syndrome and why a single fall can begin a cascade (Section 8). Second - and this is the hopeful half of the module - every one of these tissues is mechanoresponsive: bone, muscle, tendon, and the nervous system all adapt to the loads placed on them (the mechanostat and Hill machinery of Modules 2 and 5). The very load that maintains a tissue is the load that, withdrawn, lets it drift; and reapplied as training, reverses the drift by years (Section 7). Degeneration and adaptation are the same mechanism run in opposite directions.

The thesis of this module. Aging is the drift of a small set of mechanical parameters - muscle force, bone density, cartilage thickness, reflex delay, sensory noise, tissue compliance - each shrinking a reserve (capacity over demand) that some daily task already spends. Injury is a reserve crossing zero: a strength reserve below one (cannot rise), a tissue margin below one (fracture, cartilage failure), a balance margin below the perturbation (a fall). Because the same tissues are mechanoresponsive, the drift is not one-way: withdrawing load accelerates it, and training reverses it, making the reserve a quantity one can measure, predict, and act on.

What each section draws on: the strength reserve rests on the muscle model of Module 5 and the task demands of Module 13; osteoporosis and fracture on the bone mechanics of Module 2; osteoarthritis on the cartilage contact of Module 4; sensorimotor decline and the fall-risk model on the balance loop of Module 10; and adaptation on the mechanostat of Module 2 and the training response of Module 5.

1. The margin framework: capacity over demand

Every claim in this module is an inequality between a capacity the body supplies and a demand a task imposes, and their ratio is the object we track through aging.

Definition 1 (reserve / safety margin). For a task requiring a demand $D$ (a torque, a force, a stress, or a perturbation impulse) from a body with capacity $C$ (maximum voluntary torque, tissue strength, or recoverable impulse), the reserve is the ratio $$R=\frac{C}{D}.$$ The task is possible/safe when $R\gt 1$, at its limit when $R=1$, and fails (impossible, or injurious) when $R\lt 1$. Aging lowers $C$ (and sometimes raises $D$), driving $R$ toward and through $1$.

Three reserves recur, one per tissue system (Fig. 3), and naming them fixes what each later section computes. The strength reserve is maximum joint torque over task torque (Section 2). The tissue margin is tissue strength over applied load - bone strength over impact force (Section 3), cartilage tolerance over contact stress (Section 4). The balance margin is the largest recoverable perturbation over the perturbation encountered (Section 6). Each capacity is a drifting parameter; each demand is set by the daily tasks of Module 13.

The framework immediately explains why aging is felt as a sudden loss rather than a gradual one. A reserve that starts near $1$ - and Module 13 showed several everyday tasks run there even in the young - crosses zero after only a small parametric drift, so a capacity declining smoothly at one percent a year produces an abrupt functional failure the year $R$ passes $1$. The five-times-sit-to-stand test, the grip dynamometer, and the bone-density scan are all, in this light, attempts to measure a reserve before it crosses, and the rest of the module is the mechanics that turns each measured parameter into a predicted margin - beginning with the muscle.

Where these models sit. Every model in this module is Level 0 of the course's level ladder: a scalar capacity compared with a scalar demand, with no equation of motion solved here. The demands come ready-made from Level 1 statics (the chair and stair torques of Module 13) and the impact of Section 3 is an energy balance, not an impact simulation. Sections 5 and 6 quote two Level-2 results from Module 10 - the linearised inverted pendulum's growth rate $\omega_0=\sqrt{g/\ell}$ and its critical impulse - and use them as fixed capacities rather than re-deriving them. Sections 7 and 8 are a scalar Level 10: one mechanobiological state variable per tissue, coupled through load. Nothing here is dynamic, and no result of this module may be read as one.

2. Sarcopenia and the strength reserve

Sarcopenia is the age-related loss of skeletal-muscle mass and force. Its mechanical root is in Module 5: a muscle's maximum force is proportional to its physiological cross-sectional area (PCSA, the total area of its fibres) times a specific tension $\sigma_{\rm m}$, the force one unit of fibre area can produce (Module 5). The subscript is not decoration: $\sigma$ without it denotes the sensor-noise amplitude of Section 5, and the two never mean the same thing in this module. So $F_{\max}=\sigma_{\rm m}\,\mathrm{PCSA}$. Aging shrinks both factors - fibres are lost (especially the large, fast type-II fibres) and the survivors thin, while fatty and fibrous tissue infiltrates - so PCSA falls and, because the lost fibres are the forceful ones, specific tension falls too. The joint torque a muscle can produce, force times moment arm, declines with it.

Measured peak joint torque falls slowly from a peak in the third decade, then about one percent per year after forty, accelerating past sixty-five. Written as a reserve against a fixed task demand, this drift predicts the age at which the task fails.

Proposition 2.1 (the strength reserve and the failure age). Let peak voluntary joint torque decline linearly past a reference age $a_0$, $\tau_{\max}(a)=\tau_p\,[1-r\,(a-a_0)]$, with peak value $\tau_p$ and fractional loss rate $r$. Against a fixed task demand $D$ the strength reserve is $R(a)=\tau_{\max}(a)/D$, and it crosses $1$ (the task first becomes impossible) at $$\boxed{\;a^\star=a_0+\frac{1}{r}\Big(1-\frac{D}{\tau_p}\Big).\;}$$ For the chair-rise demand $D=110\ \mathrm{N\,m}$ (Module 13), $\tau_p=175\ \mathrm{N\,m}$, $a_0=40\ \mathrm{yr}$, $r=0.01\ \mathrm{yr^{-1}}$: $a^\star\approx77\ \mathrm{yr}$.
The reserve is $R(a)=\tau_{\max}(a)/D=\tau_p[1-r(a-a_0)]/D$. Setting $R(a^\star)=1$ gives $\tau_p[1-r(a^\star-a_0)]=D$, so $1-r(a^\star-a_0)=D/\tau_p$, hence $r(a^\star-a_0)=1-D/\tau_p$ and $a^\star=a_0+\frac1r(1-D/\tau_p)$. Substituting $D/\tau_p=110/175=0.629$: $a^\star=40+\frac{1}{0.01}(1-0.629)=40+37.1\approx77\ \mathrm{yr}$. ∎

Proposition 2.1 (Fig. 4) turns a rate of muscle loss into a predicted year of functional failure, and its structure carries the lessons. The failure age depends on the starting reserve $\tau_p/D$: a person who begins with more strength, or faces a lower demand, buys years before the crossing, but only up to a hard ceiling. Since $a^\star-a_0=\frac1r(1-D/\tau_p)$ and $D/\tau_p\gt 0$, no starting reserve can push the crossing further than $1/r=100$ years past $a_0$; going from a starting reserve of $\tau_p/D=1$ to $3$ buys $\frac1r(1-\tfrac13)=66.7$ years, and the marginal return falls as $\mathrm{d}a^\star/\mathrm{d}(\tau_p/D)=1/[r(\tau_p/D)^2]$, which is $39.5$ years per reserve unit at the reference $\tau_p/D=1.59$ and only $11.1$ at $\tau_p/D=3$. This is why peak strength attained in youth and midlife is protective decades later, and why lowering the demand (a higher chair, Section 7's aids) shifts $a^\star$ outward as surely as raising the capacity does. The same arithmetic, applied to the stair, the stumble step, and the lift, gives each its own failure age, and the earliest-crossing task - usually the chair or the stair - is the first symptom of sarcopenia.

The vicious cycle. Sarcopenia is self-accelerating through disuse: weakness makes activity harder, reduced activity withdraws the load that maintains muscle (and bone, Section 3), and the withdrawn load speeds the loss - a positive feedback that turns a gentle $1\%$/year drift into a steepening decline once a person becomes sedentary. The same coupling, run in reverse, is the basis of resistance training (Section 7): reapply the load and the drift reverses.

Where the $1\%$/year comes from: protein turnover and anabolic resistance

Proposition 2.1 takes the fractional loss rate $r$ as a measured input. It has a molecular origin, and reaching it means going one rung below the muscle itself, to the protein pool that PCSA is built from. A muscle fibre's contractile protein mass $M$ is not a fixed quantity slowly consumed; it is the standing balance of two continuous, opposite mass-action-type fluxes - synthesis and breakdown, each running around the clock - and $M$ is whatever balance those fluxes settle at. This is Module 0 §6 Definition 6.1's mass action, applied one level up from a bimolecular reaction to a whole protein pool.

Definition 2.2 (protein turnover balance). Let $M$ be a muscle's contractile protein mass, normalised to its young reference value. Breakdown (the ubiquitin-proteasome and autophagy-lysosome pathways) is first-order in the standing pool, at rate constant $k_{\rm deg}$; synthesis proceeds at a rate $\Pi_{\rm syn}$ set by the anabolic stimulus (feeding, particularly the amino acid leucine, and mechanical loading). The balance is $$\frac{dM}{dt}=\Pi_{\rm syn}-k_{\rm deg}M.$$ The symbol $k$ here is a first-order rate constant, not Section 3's landing stiffness or Section 7's $k_{\rm ad}$; every $k$ in this module carries its own subscript for exactly this reason.
Proposition 2.2 (quasi-steady mass, and anabolic resistance as the mechanism of $r$). Measured $k_{\rm deg}\approx0.012\ \mathrm{day^{-1}}$ (a mixed-protein half-life near $58$ days) makes Definition 2.2 relax to $M\approx\Pi_{\rm syn}/k_{\rm deg}$ on a timescale of months - far faster than the multi-year drift Proposition 2.1 describes. So on the year scale that matters here, $M(t)$ simply tracks $\Pi_{\rm syn}(t)$, and, with $k_{\rm deg}$ measured as age-stable, Proposition 2.1's slow decline in $\tau_{\max}$ is a slow decline in the synthesis rate. Modelling $\Pi_{\rm syn}$ as a Hill/occupancy response (Module 0 §6 Definition 6.2) to a habitual leucine dose $[\mathrm{Leu}]$, $$\Pi_{\rm syn}\big([\mathrm{Leu}]\big)=\Pi_{\max}\,\frac{[\mathrm{Leu}]}{K_{\rm L}+[\mathrm{Leu}]},$$ aging right-shifts the half-maximal dose $K_{\rm L}$ - "anabolic resistance": the same meal now buys less synthesis. At a fixed habitual dose $[\mathrm{Leu}]=2.5\ \mathrm g$ (a mixed meal of roughly $30\ \mathrm g$ protein) with $K_{\rm L}$ doubling from $1.7$ to $3.4\ \mathrm g$, $$\boxed{\;\frac{M_{\rm old}}{M_{\rm young}}=\frac{[\mathrm{Leu}]/(K_{\rm L,old}+[\mathrm{Leu}])}{[\mathrm{Leu}]/(K_{\rm L,young}+[\mathrm{Leu}])}=0.712,\;}$$ a $29\%$ loss from this mechanism alone, holding diet and $k_{\rm deg}$ fixed - about $72\%$ of the $40\%$ that Proposition 2.1's own $r=0.01\ \mathrm{yr^{-1}}$ predicts over the same forty years.
Definition 2.2 has a fixed point at $\Pi_{\rm syn}=k_{\rm deg}M$, i.e. $M^\ast=\Pi_{\rm syn}/k_{\rm deg}$, approached as $M(t)-M^\ast\propto e^{-k_{\rm deg}t}$; the $1/k_{\rm deg}\approx83$-day time constant means any change in $\Pi_{\rm syn}$ sustained for a year is already fully reflected in $M$. Writing $\theta([\mathrm{Leu}];K_{\rm L})=[\mathrm{Leu}]/(K_{\rm L}+[\mathrm{Leu}])$ for the occupancy factor, $M^\ast_{\rm old}/M^\ast_{\rm young}$ at fixed $\Pi_{\max}$ and $k_{\rm deg}$ is exactly the ratio of $\theta$ at the two $K_{\rm L}$ values. Substituting $[\mathrm{Leu}]=2.5$, $K_{\rm L,young}=1.7$, $K_{\rm L,old}=3.4$: $\theta_{\rm young}=2.5/4.2=0.595$, $\theta_{\rm old}=2.5/5.9=0.424$, ratio $0.712$. Comparing with Proposition 2.1's $40$-year loss $1-r\times40=1-0.40=0.60$ remaining, i.e. a $40\%$ loss: this mechanism's $29\%$ loss is $0.29/0.40=72\%$ of it. ∎

measured, not derived: $K_{\rm L}$ is a half-maximal dose fitted to human feeding-trial dose-response curves (the "anabolic resistance" and "leucine threshold" literature), not a rate constant this course computes from a bond or a reaction mechanism. The doubling used here, $1.7\to3.4\ \mathrm g$, is the commonly cited order of the effect; individual studies vary, and the shape is treated as a simple Hill form with $n_{\rm L}=1$ rather than the fitted $n_{\rm L}$ a real dose-response curve would carry.

measured, not derived: $k_{\rm deg}\approx0.012\ \mathrm{day^{-1}}$ is a measured mixed-protein fractional breakdown rate, because it comes from isotopic-tracer turnover studies, not from a mechanism this course computes; it is not observed to change much with age at rest, which is exactly why this trace places the drift in $\Pi_{\rm syn}$ and not in $k_{\rm deg}$.

The remaining $28\%$ is real and unmodelled here: preferential loss of fast type-II fibres, denervation of motor units with incomplete reinnervation, and satellite-cell exhaustion all reduce PCSA and specific tension $\sigma_{\rm m}$ by routes this section does not compute. What Proposition 2.2 supplies is not the whole of sarcopenia but its rate-limiting biochemistry: even a muscle with no fibre loss at all would still weaken on this mechanism alone, at close to three-quarters of the measured pace, because the synthesis machinery answers a fixed meal with less and less protein as the dose-response curve drifts. It is also the reason the "reload" message of Section 7 works at the molecular level: a higher habitual $[\mathrm{Leu}]$ (more protein per meal) or an anabolic stimulus other than leucine (mechanical loading itself raises $\Pi_{\rm syn}$, independent of the Hill curve above) can restore $\theta$ toward its young value even without changing $K_{\rm L}$ itself.

3. Osteoporosis and fragility fracture

Osteoporosis is the loss of bone mineral density and micro-architecture that leaves bone fragile. Its mechanics has two halves: how bone strength depends on density (which aging erodes), and how a fall generates a force that may exceed that strength. A fragility fracture is the crossing - impact force above bone strength - and the hip is its most consequential site.

Where the density loss comes from: bone as the body's calcium buffer

Nothing so far has said why bone mineral density $\rho$ (Proposition 3.1 below) falls with age; that Proposition takes the decline as given. The reason has almost nothing to do with mechanical loading, and everything to do with an unrelated job the skeleton does: it is the body's largest reservoir of calcium, and serum calcium is held within a narrow band no matter what the diet supplies, by parathyroid hormone (PTH) - even at the skeleton's expense.

The regulator has two independent levers on serum calcium, and they are not equally cheap. The kidney filters a large calcium load every day and reabsorbs almost all of it; PTH can raise that fraction a little further, buying back some of a shortfall for free. Only what the kidney cannot recover falls on bone.

Proposition 3.1′ (renal conservation bounds, but does not eliminate, the skeletal calcium cost of an absorption decline). Let a diet supply calcium $I_{\rm Ca}$, of which a fraction $\alpha$ is absorbed, $J_{\rm gut}=\alpha I_{\rm Ca}$ ($J$ here is a calcium mass flux, mg/day - not Section 6's mechanical impulse $J$, N·s). The kidney filters a fixed load $F_{\rm filt}$ and reabsorbs a fraction $f_{\rm reab}$, bounded above by $f_{\rm reab}^{\max}$ (PTH's dynamic range on tubular reabsorption). At steady serum calcium the skeletal flux needed to make up any remaining shortfall is $$\boxed{\;J_{\rm bone}=\max\!\big(0,\ (1-f_{\rm reab}^{\max})F_{\rm filt}-J_{\rm gut}\big).\;}$$ With $I_{\rm Ca}=1000\ \mathrm{mg/day}$, $\alpha$ falling from $30\%$ (young) to $18\%$ (aged, vitamin-D-dependent intestinal absorption), $F_{\rm filt}=9000\ \mathrm{mg/day}$, and a PTH ceiling only $1$ percentage point above the young baseline reabsorption of $96.7\%$: the absorption deficit is $120\ \mathrm{mg/day}$, but renal conservation alone recovers $75\%$ of it, leaving $J_{\rm bone}=30\ \mathrm{mg/day}$ forced onto the skeleton - an implied loss rate of $1.1\%$/year against a $\sim1\ \mathrm{kg}$ skeletal reserve, the same order as the $\approx1\%$/year drift Section 0 quotes for bone density generally.
At steady serum calcium the three fluxes balance: what is absorbed plus what bone releases equals what is excreted, $J_{\rm gut}+J_{\rm bone}=J_{\rm excr}=(1-f_{\rm reab})F_{\rm filt}$. The kidney's best case is $f_{\rm reab}=f_{\rm reab}^{\max}$, which minimises $J_{\rm excr}$ to $(1-f_{\rm reab}^{\max})F_{\rm filt}$; if even that floor exceeds $J_{\rm gut}$, no achievable reabsorption balances the books without a skeletal contribution, and $J_{\rm bone}$ is the shortfall. If instead $J_{\rm gut}$ already exceeds the floor, ordinary reabsorption clears the balance and $J_{\rm bone}=0$ - the $\max(0,\cdot)$ in the box. Substituting: $J_{\rm gut,young}=0.30\times1000=300$, $J_{\rm gut,old}=0.18\times1000=180\ \mathrm{mg/day}$, a deficit of $120$. The young baseline, at zero bone flux, needs $f_{\rm reab,young}=1-300/9000=96.67\%$; with a $1$-point ceiling, $f_{\rm reab}^{\max}=97.67\%$, giving an excretion floor $(1-0.9767)\times9000=210\ \mathrm{mg/day}$. Since $210\gt180$, the floor cannot be pushed low enough to absorb the whole deficit, and $J_{\rm bone}=210-180=30\ \mathrm{mg/day}$. Had renal conservation been unlimited, the full $120\ \mathrm{mg/day}$ would have fallen on bone, $365.25\times120/10^6=4.38\%$/year; the $1$-point ceiling cuts that by $75\%$. Converting the $30\ \mathrm{mg/day}$ residual against a $\sim1\times10^6\ \mathrm{mg}$ ($1\ \mathrm{kg}$) adult skeletal calcium pool: $365.25\times30/10^6=1.10\%$/year. ∎

Provenance note (this box carries no gated constitutive symbol, so no .prov marker is required, but the same honesty applies): the intestinal absorption fractions ($30\%\to18\%$), the filtered load ($9000\ \mathrm{mg/day}$), and the $1$-percentage-point ceiling on PTH-drivable reabsorption are representative literature figures from calcium-balance and parathyroid physiology, not quantities this course computes from a bond or a reaction. The reabsorption ceiling in particular is a modelled bound, read off the gap between hypoparathyroid and maximally PTH-stimulated states, not a single universal constant.

This is one mechanism, and it is not the dominant one at menopause. Oestrogen acts on a different lever entirely: it raises the ratio of OPG to RANKL, suppressing osteoclast recruitment independent of any calcium shortfall - in the language of Module 2 §7's Proposition 7.3, oestrogen sets the amplitude $\Phi_R$ that the Proposition holds fixed (the Proposition's own $\theta_R(\varepsilon)$ arm is strain-only; it carries no hormonal input, which is exactly the debt this paragraph pays). Its loss removes that brake: bone turnover markers rise roughly $2.5\times$ in the years around menopause, and the accompanying loss rate - about $2\%$/year for some five to ten years - is measured to run nearly twice as fast as the calcium-buffering rate derived above, before settling back to an age-related rate of the same $\sim1\%$/year order this section computes. The two mechanisms are additive, not competing: the calcium-buffering flux runs throughout adult life in both sexes, while the oestrogen-driven surge is a temporary, much larger multiplier layered on top of it in the postmenopausal years. Together they are the biological content behind the $\rho(a)$ this section's Proposition 3.1 takes as given.

Bone strength does not fall in proportion to density; it falls faster. The compressive strength of trabecular bone scales roughly as the square of its apparent density (mass per total volume, including the pore space; the ash-density power law of Module 2), because both the amount of bone and the thickness of the load-bearing trabeculae decrease together.

Proposition 3.1 (bone strength scales as density squared). If bone compressive strength follows $S=S_0(\rho/\rho_0)^{n_{\rm b}}$ with exponent $n_{\rm b}\approx2$, then a fractional loss of density produces roughly twice that fractional loss of strength: $$\boxed{\;\frac{\Delta S}{S}\approx n_{\rm b}\,\frac{\Delta\rho}{\rho}=2\,\frac{\Delta\rho}{\rho}.\;}$$ A $20\%$ fall in bone mineral density ($\rho/\rho_0=0.8$) cuts strength to $0.8^2=0.64$ of peak - a $36\%$ loss; a $40\%$ density loss ($\rho/\rho_0=0.6$) leaves only $0.36$ of peak strength.
Differentiate $S=S_0(\rho/\rho_0)^{n_{\rm b}}$: $dS/d\rho=S_0\,n_{\rm b}\,\rho^{n_{\rm b}-1}/\rho_0^{n_{\rm b}}=n_{\rm b}S/\rho$, so $dS/S=n_{\rm b}\,d\rho/\rho$, i.e. the fractional change in strength is $n_{\rm b}$ times the fractional change in density. For $n_{\rm b}=2$ and a finite $20\%$ drop, exactly $S/S_0=0.8^2=0.64$, a $36\%$ loss; for $40\%$, $0.6^2=0.36$. ∎

Now the demand. A sideways fall drops the greater trochanter (the bony prominence of the hip) onto the ground; the impact is a mass striking a compliant surface - soft tissue over bone over floor - and its peak force follows from energy conservation against the effective landing stiffness.

Proposition 3.2 (peak impact force in a sideways fall). A body of effective mass $m_{\rm eff}$ striking a landing of effective stiffness $k$ at impact speed $v$ generates a peak contact force $$\boxed{\;F_{\rm peak}=v\,\sqrt{m_{\rm eff}\,k},\qquad v=\sqrt{2gh_{\rm f}},\;}$$ with $h_{\rm f}$ the fall height of the impacting part. A fracture occurs when $F_{\rm peak}\gt S$, the bone strength. For a hip fall with $h_{\rm f}=0.70\ \mathrm{m}$ ($v=3.7\ \mathrm{m/s}$), $m_{\rm eff}=35\ \mathrm{kg}$, $k=40\ \mathrm{kN/m}$: $F_{\rm peak}\approx4.4\ \mathrm{kN}$. The boxed form is the leading-order result: it holds when the compression $x_{\max}=F_{\rm peak}/k$ is small against the fall height $h_{\rm f}$, and here it is not especially small ($x_{\max}=11.0\ \mathrm{cm}$, $x_{\max}/h_{\rm f}=0.16$).
Model the impact as the effective mass compressing a linear spring of stiffness $k$ (the lumped soft-tissue-and-floor compliance). At impact the kinetic energy $\tfrac12 m_{\rm eff}v^2$ converts to spring energy $\tfrac12 k x_{\max}^2$ at maximum compression, so $x_{\max}=v\sqrt{m_{\rm eff}/k}$. The peak force is the spring force there, $F_{\rm peak}=k x_{\max}=k\,v\sqrt{m_{\rm eff}/k}=v\sqrt{m_{\rm eff}k}$. The impact speed is that of a free fall through height $h_{\rm f}$, $v=\sqrt{2gh_{\rm f}}$. Substituting, $v=\sqrt{2\times9.81\times0.70}=3.7\ \mathrm{m/s}$ and $F_{\rm peak}=3.7\sqrt{35\times40000}\approx4.4\ \mathrm{kN}$, at a compression $x_{\max}=11.0\ \mathrm{cm}$.

What the boxed form drops. The mass does not stop at the spring's rest point; it descends a further $x_{\max}$ while the spring compresses, and gravity does work $m_{\rm eff}gx$ over that stroke. The exact balance is therefore $\tfrac12kx^2=\tfrac12m_{\rm eff}v^2+m_{\rm eff}gx$, whose positive root is $x=\big(m_{\rm eff}g+\sqrt{m_{\rm eff}^2g^2+k\,m_{\rm eff}v^2}\big)/k$. At these values that gives $x=11.9\ \mathrm{cm}$ and $F_{\rm peak}=4742\ \mathrm{N}$, $8.1\%$ above the boxed $4385\ \mathrm{N}$, and it moves the critical density of Lab B from $0.79$ to $0.82$. This module keeps the boxed form and its $4.4\ \mathrm{kN}$ as the working value; the $8\%$ it undercounts is the first correction, and it is a correction in the unsafe direction. ∎

Put the two halves together (Fig. 5). A young femoral neck (the narrow bridge of bone between the ball and the shaft) fails at roughly $7\ \mathrm{kN}$, so the $4.4\ \mathrm{kN}$ of a sideways fall leaves a tissue margin above one - a bruise, not a break. Osteoporosis at $40\%$ density loss drops strength to $\sim2.5\ \mathrm{kN}$; now the same fall exceeds strength and the hip fractures. The model names both levers of prevention, and both are used clinically. Lower the force: $F_{\rm peak}\propto\sqrt{k}$, so a hip protector or a compliant floor that halves the effective stiffness cuts the peak force by the factor $1/\sqrt2$, a $29\%$ reduction; and reducing the fall itself (balance training, Section 6) removes the demand entirely. Raise the strength: because $S\propto\rho^2$, even a modest density gain from loading or medication buys a squared improvement in strength. The wrist (a fall on an outstretched hand) and the vertebra (compression under trunk load) are the same inequality at other sites, with their own strengths and impact geometries.

4. Osteoarthritis: the cartilage stress cycle

Osteoarthritis (OA) is the progressive failure of the cartilage that caps the bones in a joint (Module 4). Unlike sarcopenia and osteoporosis, whose demands are roughly fixed, OA is driven by a positive feedback between load and damage: cartilage thins, thinner cartilage bears higher contact stress, higher stress damages it faster, and it thins more. Reading OA as this loop - rather than as "wear and tear" - explains why it accelerates, and why both the load and the tissue are targets.

The mechanical fact at the loop's heart is that a thinner cartilage layer bears a higher peak contact stress for the same joint load. Healthy cartilage is a compliant, fluid-pressurised cushion (Module 4) that spreads load over a wide contact patch and pressurises its interstitial fluid to carry most of it; as it thins and loses proteoglycan (the water-binding matrix molecule that pressurises cartilage, Module 4), the contact area shrinks and fluid support falls, so the peak stress on the solid matrix rises.

Proposition 4.1 (peak contact stress rises as cartilage thins). For a joint transmitting a fixed load, the peak contact stress rises as the cartilage thickness $h$ falls, modelled as $$\boxed{\;p_{\rm peak}(h)=p_0\Big(\frac{h_0}{h}\Big)^{n_{\rm c}},\qquad n_{\rm c}\approx0.5\text{-}1,\;}$$ with $p_0$ the peak stress at healthy thickness $h_0$. For $p_0=3\ \mathrm{MPa}$ at $h_0=2\ \mathrm{mm}$ and $n_{\rm c}=0.7$: the peak stress rises to $\approx4.3\ \mathrm{MPa}$ at $h=1.2\ \mathrm{mm}$ and $\approx7\ \mathrm{MPa}$ at $h=0.6\ \mathrm{mm}$ - more than doubling as the cartilage thins by a factor of $3.3$.
The exponent is not a fitted number; it is bracketed by two solvable limits of the same geometry. In both, a rigid sphere of radius $R$ (the femoral condyle) presses on an elastic layer of thickness $h$ bonded to rigid bone, indenting the surface by $w(r)=\delta_0-r^2/2R$ inside a contact circle of radius $a$, where $w(a)=0$ fixes $a^2=2R\delta_0$. Take $h\ll a$, so the layer deforms locally.

Limit 1: a compressible layer. Each column of tissue acts as an independent spring of stiffness $H'/h$ per unit area, with $H'$ the layer's confined modulus, so $p(r)=(H'/h)\,w(r)$. The load is $P=\int_0^a p\,2\pi r\,\mathrm dr=\pi R H'\delta_0^2/h$, giving $\delta_0=\sqrt{Ph/(\pi RH')}$ and $p_{\rm peak}=(H'/h)\delta_0=\sqrt{PH'/(\pi Rh)}\propto h^{-1/2}$: the exponent is $n_{\rm c}=\tfrac12$.

Limit 2: an incompressible layer. If the layer cannot change volume it must extrude sideways, and with no slip at either face the radial displacement has a parabolic profile through the thickness, so the outward flux per unit circumference is $Q=-(h^3/12G)\,\mathrm dp/\mathrm dr$ with $G$ the shear modulus. Volume conservation, $r^{-1}\mathrm d(rQ)/\mathrm dr=w$, integrates with $p(a)=0$ to $p(r)=\tfrac{3G}{8Rh^3}\,(3a^4-4a^2r^2+r^4)$, whence $P=\pi Ga^6/(2Rh^3)$ and $p_{\rm peak}=p(0)=9Ga^4/(8Rh^3)$. Eliminating $a$ gives $p_{\rm peak}\propto G^{1/3}R^{-1/3}P^{2/3}h^{-1}$: the exponent is $n_{\rm c}=1$. (The numerical prefactor is not reliable, because the thin-layer approximation fails within about $h$ of the contact edge; the power of $h$ is.)

So $n_{\rm c}\in[\tfrac12,1]$, and the two ends are physical states of the same tissue, not fitting choices. Healthy cartilage loaded faster than its drainage time is fluid-pressurised and nearly incompressible (Module 4), so it sits near $n_{\rm c}=1$; as proteoglycan is lost the fluid support decays and the layer behaves compressibly, moving the exponent toward $\tfrac12$ while, separately, shifting a larger fraction of $p_{\rm peak}$ onto the solid matrix. This module uses a single $n_{\rm c}=0.7$ across the range as an assumed working value; the bracket it sits in is what the two limits establish, and it matters: over a thinning from $2.0$ to $0.6\ \mathrm{mm}$ the stress rises by a factor $1.83$ at $n_{\rm c}=\tfrac12$ and $3.33$ at $n_{\rm c}=1$. Substituting $n_{\rm c}=0.7$: $p_{\rm peak}(1.2)=3(2/1.2)^{0.7}\approx4.3\ \mathrm{MPa}$, $p_{\rm peak}(0.6)=3(2/0.6)^{0.7}\approx7\ \mathrm{MPa}$. ∎

Proposition 4.1 (Fig. 6) closes the loop. Once thinning begins - from injury, malalignment, or age - the rising stress accelerates the damage, which thins the cartilage further, a runaway that explains OA's characteristic acceleration once established and its concentration where load is highest (the medial knee in a bow-legged stance, the loaded hip). The demand side is now a lever: because stress scales with joint load, reducing that load slows the loop. This is the mechanical rationale for weight loss (every kilogram off the body is several off the knee through the gait's load multiplier), for gait and alignment correction (a brace or osteotomy that shifts load off the worn compartment), and for avoiding the sustained overload that outpaces cartilage's slow, fluid-mediated recovery. Cartilage, unlike bone and muscle, adapts poorly and heals slowly, so for OA, prevention beats reversal - keeping the loop from starting is worth far more than any attempt to unwind it.

5. Sensorimotor aging: slower, noisier, stiffer

The tissues so far were structural - muscle, bone, cartilage. Aging also degrades the control system of Module 10, and in three specific, measurable ways: the feedback loop grows slower (reflex delay rises), its sensors grow noisier (proprioceptive spindles, vestibular hair cells, and retinal receptors are lost), and its tissues grow stiffer (tendon collagen cross-links, dulling both force transmission and the stretch signals proprioception depends on). Each maps onto a parameter of the balance controller, and the most consequential is the delay.

Module 10 showed that a feedback controller acting through a delay is stable only within a bounded island of the delay-gain plane, and that beyond a critical delay the loop oscillates. The delay's effect on recovery is sharper still: during the reaction lag the body's extrapolated centre of mass keeps diverging, so a perturbation that was recoverable becomes unrecoverable simply by the passage of the delay.

Proposition 5.1 (delay erodes the recoverable envelope). After a perturbation the free inverted pendulum's extrapolated centre of mass grows as $\xi(t)=\xi_0\,e^{\omega_0 t}$ with $\omega_0=\sqrt{g/\ell}$ (Module 10). If the corrective response is issued only after a reaction delay $\Delta t$, the initial perturbation that can still be caught is reduced by the factor $$\boxed{\;\frac{\xi_{\rm recoverable}(\Delta t)}{\xi_{\rm recoverable}(0)}=e^{-\omega_0\,\Delta t}.\;}$$ For $\omega_0=3.1\ \mathrm{rad/s}$, a reaction slowing from $0.12$ to $0.20\ \mathrm{s}$ shrinks the recoverable perturbation by $e^{-3.1\times0.08}\approx0.78$ - a $22\%$ loss of the balance envelope from an $80\ \mathrm{ms}$ delay increase.
Let the fixed-support response be able to arrest the body whenever the extrapolated COM at the instant of response, $\xi(\Delta t)$, is within a fixed capturable bound $\xi_c$ (the base of support times the controller's reachability, Module 10). With $\xi(\Delta t)=\xi_0 e^{\omega_0\Delta t}$, the response succeeds iff $\xi_0 e^{\omega_0\Delta t}\le\xi_c$, i.e. iff the initial perturbation satisfies $\xi_0\le\xi_c e^{-\omega_0\Delta t}$. The largest recoverable initial perturbation is therefore $\xi_{\rm recoverable}(\Delta t)=\xi_c e^{-\omega_0\Delta t}$, and its ratio to the no-delay value $\xi_c$ is $e^{-\omega_0\Delta t}$. Substituting $\omega_0=3.1$, $\Delta t=0.08$: $e^{-0.248}\approx0.78$. ∎

The other two degradations act on the same margin (Fig. 7). Sensor noise - from fewer, less reliable spindles, hair cells, and photoreceptors - raises the amplitude of quiet-standing sway (Module 10's stochastic sway grows with measurement noise), so the body wanders closer to the edge of its base even before any push, consuming part of the margin as a standing cost. Tendon and tissue stiffening blunts the proprioceptive stretch signal (a stiffer tendon transmits less length change to the spindle) and alters the timing of force delivery, degrading the estimate the controller acts on. All three - slower, noisier, stiffer - subtract from the same recoverable balance envelope, which is why the fall-risk model of the next section is written on that single quantity.

Where the stiffening comes from: AGE crosslink kinetics

Module 6 §7 names the mechanism behind "stiffer" in its own words: glucose reacts with collagen's lysine side chains "without an enzyme, and over years the products rearrange into advanced glycation end-products (AGEs) that cross-link the helices at random positions," and states plainly that "their accumulation is a kinetic process in glucose and time, which is the calculation Module 14 makes when it turns tendon stiffening with age into a rate." This is that calculation, and because bone collagen is cross-linked by the identical chemistry, it repays Section 3 at the same time, with a mechanism for fracture risk that Proposition 3.1's density law cannot see at all.

Definition 5.2 (AGE crosslink fraction). Let $\phi_{\rm AGE}\in[0,1]$ be the fraction of a tissue's collagen crosslink sites carrying a mature, non-enzymatic advanced glycation end-product. New crosslinks form by mass action in the unmodified sites and glucose (Module 0 §6 Definition 6.1), at rate constant $k_{\rm AGE}$; modified collagen is removed at whatever rate the tissue turns its collagen over, $k_{\rm turn}$, and is replaced by fresh, unmodified collagen. The symbol $\phi$ (not $a$, which this module reserves for age) tracks the balance $$\frac{d\phi_{\rm AGE}}{dt}=k_{\rm AGE}[\mathrm{Glc}]\,(1-\phi_{\rm AGE})-k_{\rm turn}\,\phi_{\rm AGE}.$$

The one number that separates tendon from bone is $k_{\rm turn}$, and it is a documented, striking contrast. Human Achilles tendon core collagen, dated by the $^{14}\mathrm C$ bomb-pulse method (Heinemeier et al. 2013), turns over negligibly after early adulthood - it is essentially as old as the person. Bone collagen, by contrast, is continuously replaced by remodeling on a decade timescale (Module 2 §7).

Proposition 5.3 (turnover decides whether a tissue reads a lifetime or a decade). Taking collagen laid down unmodified at age $20$, $\phi_{\rm AGE}(20)=0$, Definition 5.2 has closed-form solution $\phi_{\rm AGE}(t)=\phi_{\rm ss}\big(1-e^{-(k_{\rm AGE}[\mathrm{Glc}]+k_{\rm turn})t}\big)$ ($t$ = years since age $20$) with steady state $\phi_{\rm ss}=k_{\rm AGE}[\mathrm{Glc}]/(k_{\rm AGE}[\mathrm{Glc}]+k_{\rm turn})$ and time constant $1/(k_{\rm AGE}[\mathrm{Glc}]+k_{\rm turn})$. With $k_{\rm turn,tendon}\approx0.005\ \mathrm{yr^{-1}}$ (near-zero) against $k_{\rm turn,bone}\approx0.10\ \mathrm{yr^{-1}}$ and a common $k_{\rm AGE}$ fitted so bone's normoglycemic steady state is $\phi_{\rm ss}=0.06$: tendon's time constant is nine decades, far longer than a human lifespan, so it never approaches steady state and $\phi_{\rm AGE}(t)$ grows on the same clock as age itself - $0,\ 0.114,\ 0.205,\ 0.278$ at ages $20,\ 40,\ 60,\ 80$, sub-linear but nowhere near saturating. Bone's time constant is under a decade, so it reaches near its steady state by midlife and then tracks whatever glucose level has prevailed recently: at sustained hyperglycemia ($2\times$ normoglycemic) the steady state nearly doubles, to $\phi_{\rm ss}=0.113$, and a person who becomes hyperglycemic only for the last ten years before eighty already closes $68\%$ of the gap to a lifelong-diabetic bone, while the same ten-year bout moves tendon's cumulative burden by only $22\%$ of its full lifelong-diabetic gap. Tendon integrates a lifetime; bone reads its most recent decade.
Definition 5.2 is linear and separable at fixed $[\mathrm{Glc}]$: writing $k_{\rm tot}=k_{\rm AGE}[\mathrm{Glc}]+k_{\rm turn}$, $d\phi/dt=k_{\rm tot}(\phi_{\rm ss}-\phi)$ with $\phi_{\rm ss}=k_{\rm AGE}[\mathrm{Glc}]/k_{\rm tot}$, so $\phi(t)-\phi_{\rm ss}\propto e^{-k_{\rm tot}t}$ and, from $\phi(0)=0$, $\phi(t)=\phi_{\rm ss}(1-e^{-k_{\rm tot}t})$. Fitting $k_{\rm AGE}$ from $\phi_{\rm ss,bone}(\mathrm{Glc_{normo}}=5.5\ \mathrm{mM})=0.06=k_{\rm AGE}(5.5)/(k_{\rm AGE}(5.5)+0.10)$ gives $k_{\rm AGE}=1.16\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{yr^{-1}mM^{-1}}$. Tendon's $k_{\rm tot}=k_{\rm AGE}(5.5)+0.005=0.0114\ \mathrm{yr^{-1}}$, time constant $87.9\ \mathrm{yr}$; at $t=60\ \mathrm{yr}$ (age $80$), $k_{\rm tot}t=0.68$, well short of saturation, giving the quoted $\phi$ values by direct substitution. Bone's $k_{\rm tot}=k_{\rm AGE}(5.5)+0.10=0.106\ \mathrm{yr^{-1}}$, time constant $9.4\ \mathrm{yr}$, reached within two constants by age $\sim40$-$60$, matching the near-flat $0.053\to0.060$ progression from $40$ to $80$. At $\mathrm{Glc_{dia}}=11.0\ \mathrm{mM}$, $\phi_{\rm ss,bone}=k_{\rm AGE}(11)/(k_{\rm AGE}(11)+0.10)=0.113$. The two-phase claim is evaluated by numerically integrating $\phi$ under a glucose history that is $\mathrm{Glc_{normo}}$ up to an onset age and $\mathrm{Glc_{dia}}$ thereafter, run to age $80$: an onset at $70$ (a $10$-year bout) gives bone $\phi=0.096$, a fraction $(0.096-0.060)/(0.113-0.060)=68\%$ of the full lifelong-diabetic excess $0.113-0.060$; the same bout gives tendon $\phi=0.321$ against a lifelong-normoglycemic $0.278$ and a lifelong-diabetic value reached only by integrating $\mathrm{Glc_{dia}}$ from age $20$, a much larger number, so the $10$-year bout closes only $22\%$ of tendon's gap. ∎

AGE crosslink fraction $\phi_{\rm AGE}$ against age, from Proposition 5.3. Tendon (near-zero collagen turnover, red) never resets, so it climbs on the same clock as age and is still rising at 80. Bone (blue, decade-scale turnover) reaches a low plateau by midlife under normal glucose, but the same turnover machinery locks in a plateau roughly double as high under sustained hyperglycemia (dashed) - and reaches most of that higher plateau within a decade of onset, whenever it starts.

The crosslinks change two properties at once, in opposite directions - stiffening and embrittling the same tissue with the same chemistry, illustratively $E(\phi_{\rm AGE})=E_0(1+\beta_{\rm AGE}\phi_{\rm AGE})$ and $\varepsilon_f(\phi_{\rm AGE})=\varepsilon_{f,0}(1-\gamma_{\rm AGE}\phi_{\rm AGE})$ for representative $\beta_{\rm AGE}=1.5$, $\gamma_{\rm AGE}=0.6$ (subscripted against Section 4's cartilage recovery rate $\beta$): tendon at age $80$ ($\phi_{\rm AGE}=0.278$) is stiffened $\times1.42$ and embrittled to $83\%$ of its young failure strain, which is Module 6's "stiffer, less lossy, more fragile" made quantitative. Bone's normoglycemic crosslink burden is modest ($\times1.09$ stiffening, $4\%$ embrittlement) precisely because turnover keeps washing it out - but under sustained hyperglycemia the same mechanism reaches $\times1.17$ stiffening and $7\%$ embrittlement, a density-independent loss of toughness that Proposition 3.1's $S\propto\rho^2$ law cannot see at all: a diabetic hip can carry normal, even elevated, bone mineral density and still fracture at a lower load, because the collagen scaffold underneath the mineral has become more brittle. This is the density-quality gap clinicians report in diabetic fragility fracture, and it is the reason Section 3's $\rho$-only reserve is necessary but not sufficient.

Provenance note: $\beta_{\rm AGE}$ and $\gamma_{\rm AGE}$ are illustrative stiffening/embrittlement coefficients, not fitted to a specific tissue's stress-strain data, and are used here only to show the two effects move in opposite directions from the same $\phi_{\rm AGE}$. $k_{\rm turn,tendon}$ and $k_{\rm turn,bone}$ are measured turnover rates (the former from isotopic dating, the latter a representative mixed cortical/trabecular remodeling rate); $k_{\rm AGE}$ is fitted, not measured or derived, to reproduce a representative bone AGE burden at normoglycemia, so its absolute value should be read as order-of-magnitude.

6. The fall-risk model

A fall is the clearest reserve crossing in the module: it occurs when a perturbation exceeds what the body can recover from. The recoverable capacity is Module 10's critical impulse, and aging shrinks it through the three sensorimotor parameters of Section 5 and the strength that sizes the base and the step. Assembling them gives a fall-risk model whose sensitivity to each parameter is what makes it actionable.

Proposition 6.1 (effective recoverable impulse and the fall condition). A standing person recovers a horizontal perturbation impulse $J$ without falling iff $J\le J_{\rm eff}$, where the effective recoverable impulse combines the balance margin $b$, the reaction delay $\Delta t$, and the sway consumed by sensor noise $\sigma$: $$\boxed{\;J_{\rm eff}=m\,\omega_0\,(b-c\,\sigma)\,e^{-\omega_0\,\Delta t}.\;}$$ Here $m\omega_0 b$ is the young critical impulse (Module 10), $e^{-\omega_0\Delta t}$ the delay erosion (Proposition 5.1), and $c\sigma$ the fraction of the margin spent on baseline sway, with $c$ a sway-to-margin sensitivity that converts the sway amplitude $\sigma$ into consumed margin. A fall follows when an encountered $J$ exceeds $J_{\rm eff}$.
Module 10 established that a perturbation impulse $J$ sets the initial extrapolated-COM offset $\xi_0=J/(m\omega_0)$, and that a bounded fixed-support response arrests the perturbation iff the extrapolated COM stays within the base. Setting $\xi_0$ equal to the margin $b$ therefore gives a young critical impulse $J_{\max}=m\omega_0 b$ (the impulse that drives $\xi$ exactly to the base edge). Sensor noise raises baseline sway, so the extrapolated COM already sits a distance $c\sigma$ inside the edge on average; the usable margin is reduced to $b-c\sigma$, giving $m\omega_0(b-c\sigma)$. The reaction delay then scales the recoverable perturbation by $e^{-\omega_0\Delta t}$ (Proposition 5.1). Multiplying the independent factors gives $J_{\rm eff}=m\omega_0(b-c\sigma)e^{-\omega_0\Delta t}$, and recovery requires the encountered impulse $J\le J_{\rm eff}$. ∎

The model quantifies the decline and ranks the interventions (Fig. 8). With representative drift - margin $b$ from $0.10$ to $0.05\ \mathrm{m}$, delay from $0.12$ to $0.20\ \mathrm{s}$, and rising sway - the effective recoverable impulse falls from $13.46\ \mathrm{N\,s}$ at $40$ to $3.50\ \mathrm{N\,s}$ at $85$ (taking all three parameters to drift linearly in age between those ages, an assumption, not a measurement; the intermediate values are $9.64$ at $55$ and $6.34$ at $70$): the envelope shrinks to a quarter, so perturbations a younger body shrugged off now exceed capacity, and the same everyday trip or nudge that was recoverable becomes a fall.

The sensitivity structure is the actionable part, and it has two faces. Taking each parameter to its aged value alone, the margin loss accounts for the largest single share (about three-quarters) - not because $b$ itself changes most in relative terms, but because the impulse acts on the usable margin $b-c\sigma$, which falls from $0.09$ to $0.04\ \mathrm{m}$ when $b$ alone is aged (a $56\%$ drop, the largest multiplicative swing of the three factors, against the delay factor $e^{-\omega_0\Delta t}$ falling only $0.69\to0.54$, about $22\%$, and the noise term $0.09\to0.08\ \mathrm{m}$, $11\%$; with $b$ and $c\sigma$ both aged it reaches $0.03\ \mathrm{m}$, a $67\%$ drop); the delay comes next and the sensor noise least. The delay is different in kind, not in size: it enters exponentially, so $\partial J_{\rm eff}/\partial\Delta t=-\omega_0 J_{\rm eff}$ is a fixed fractional loss per second and does not shrink as the envelope shrinks. Comparing per-unit sensitivities needs a unit-free measure, because "per millisecond" and "per millimetre" cannot be ranked against each other. The elasticities at the young operating point settle it: $\partial\ln J_{\rm eff}/\partial\ln b=b/(b-c\sigma)=+1.11$, $\partial\ln J_{\rm eff}/\partial\ln\Delta t=-\omega_0\Delta t=-0.37$, and $-c\sigma/(b-c\sigma)=-0.11$ for the noise. The margin leads on both counts, and K4 computes how far the delay would have to drift to overtake it. Strength training and reaction training are still the two highest-leverage moves, because the margin $b$ is not only foot geometry: it is the base of support plus the step the leg can place in the time available, and Module 10 sets that step by the hip and ankle impulse the muscles can generate. A wider base (a cane) and cleaner sensors add to $b$ and subtract from $c\sigma$ respectively, both linearly. A fall-risk assessment is, in this light, a measurement of $J_{\rm eff}$; a fall-prevention programme raises each of its factors, and Section 7 shows every factor is trainable.

7. Adaptation: load reverses the drift

Every section so far has described a one-way loss. One fact reverses the direction: the tissues that drift are mechanoresponsive. Bone remodels to its habitual load (Wolff's law and the mechanostat of Module 2), muscle hypertrophies to overload (Module 5), tendon stiffens and thickens to training, and the nervous system relearns fast, accurate corrections. The same load whose withdrawal drove each drift, reapplied, reverses it - degeneration and adaptation are one mechanism run in opposite directions.

The unifying model is the mechanostat: a tissue property (bone density, muscle force, tendon stiffness) is maintained at a level where the habitual mechanical stimulus matches a biological setpoint; a stimulus above the setpoint builds the tissue, below it resorbs. Written as a rate law, this makes training and detraining the same equation with opposite-sign forcing.

Proposition 7.1 (adaptation is a set-point rate law; training reverses drift). Let a tissue capacity $C$ follow the mechanostat $$\frac{dC}{dt}=k_{\rm ad}\,\big(\Sigma-\Sigma_0\big),$$ where $\Sigma$ is the habitual mechanical stimulus, $\Sigma_0$ the maintenance setpoint, and $k_{\rm ad}\gt 0$ a tissue-specific adaptation rate. Then $C$ is steady when $\Sigma=\Sigma_0$, drifts down under disuse ($\Sigma\lt \Sigma_0$), and builds under training ($\Sigma\gt \Sigma_0$); a training overload $\Sigma-\Sigma_0\gt 0$ raises $C$ at rate $k_{\rm ad}(\Sigma-\Sigma_0)$, recovering lost capacity in a time set by $k_{\rm ad}$.
The rate law $dC/dt=k_{\rm ad}(\Sigma-\Sigma_0)$ has $dC/dt=0$ iff $\Sigma=\Sigma_0$ (maintenance), $dC/dt\lt 0$ iff $\Sigma\lt \Sigma_0$ (disuse resorption), and $dC/dt\gt 0$ iff $\Sigma\gt \Sigma_0$ (overload formation), directly from the sign of $\Sigma-\Sigma_0$. Under a sustained training overload $\Sigma=\Sigma_0+\Delta$, $C(t)=C(0)+k_{\rm ad}\Delta\,t$ grows linearly, so a capacity deficit $\delta C$ is recovered in time $t=\delta C/(k_{\rm ad}\Delta)$. Because the same law governs the disuse drift, the overload needed to reverse a given rate of loss is exactly the deficit in stimulus that caused it, with opposite sign. ∎

The numbers make the promise concrete, and the muscle is the clearest case (Fig. 9). Resistance training raises strength by roughly $1$-$2\%$ per session-week early on, so about twelve weeks of progressive loading adds on the order of $20\%$ - enough to cancel two decades of the $1\%$/year sarcopenic drift in $\tau_{\max}$. That is not the same as twenty years of function: the failure age depends on $D/\tau_p$, not on $\tau_{\max}$, so taking $\tau_p$ from $175$ to $210\ \mathrm{N\,m}$ moves the chair crossing of Section 2 from $77.1$ to $87.6$ years - $10.5$ years, not twenty. Cancelling a strength deficit and postponing a failure are different arithmetic, and the second is always the smaller number. Bone responds more slowly (density gains of a few percent over a year of impact and resistance loading, but squared into strength by Proposition 3.1), tendon and the balance controller on intermediate timescales. The asymmetry across tissues is itself a design principle: muscle and bone adapt well and fast enough to train back, so for them the message is reload; cartilage adapts poorly and heals slowly (Section 4), so for it the message is protect. And because the fall-risk factors of Section 6 are all trainable - strength for $b$, reactive stepping for $\Delta t$, sensory and vestibular work for $\sigma$ - the single most effective anti-aging intervention is not a molecule but a mechanical stimulus, dosed to each tissue's rate.

8. Aging as coupled drift

The sections have treated each parameter in isolation, but their power - and their danger - is that they are coupled. The drifts drive one another, and a single event can tip a slow decline into a fast cascade. Seeing the coupling is what turns a list of degenerations into an understanding of frailty.

One of the three couplings can be written down with the machinery already built, and doing so is what turns the list below into a model. Bone's mechanostat stimulus $\Sigma$ of Proposition 7.1 is set mainly by muscle force, so a muscle capacity that drifts is a bone stimulus that drifts.

Proposition 8.1 (a coupling: muscle drift makes bone fall quadratically). Let muscle capacity fall linearly, $C_{\rm m}(t)=1-rt$, in units of its young value, and let bone take that capacity as its mechanostat stimulus, $\Sigma=C_{\rm m}$, with the setpoint at the young value, $\Sigma_0=C_{\rm m}(0)=1$. Then bone capacity obeys $\mathrm dC_{\rm b}/\mathrm dt=k_{\rm ad}(\Sigma-\Sigma_0)$ and $$\boxed{\;C_{\rm b}(t)=1-\tfrac12 k_{\rm ad}r\,t^{2}.\;}$$ Bone loss is quadratic in time while muscle loss is linear: the bone curve is flat at $t=0$ and steepens without bound, and its loss scales linearly with the muscle loss rate $r$, so halving $r$ halves the bone deficit at every age.
By hypothesis $\Sigma-\Sigma_0=(1-rt)-1=-rt$, so $\mathrm dC_{\rm b}/\mathrm dt=-k_{\rm ad}rt$. Integrating from $C_{\rm b}(0)=1$ gives $C_{\rm b}(t)=1-\tfrac12k_{\rm ad}rt^2$. The rate $\mathrm dC_{\rm b}/\mathrm dt=-k_{\rm ad}rt$ vanishes at $t=0$ and grows linearly, which is the steepening; and both $C_{\rm b}$ and its rate depend on $r$ only through the product $k_{\rm ad}r$, so scaling $r$ scales the deficit $1-C_{\rm b}$ by the same factor. If instead the muscle is held at its young value, $r=0$, then $\Sigma=\Sigma_0$ for all $t$ and $C_{\rm b}\equiv1$: bone does not drift at all. ∎

K5 puts numbers on this with $k_{\rm ad}=0.03125\ \mathrm{yr^{-1}}$, an assumed rate chosen so that the model's forty-year bone loss matches the $25\%$ the parameter table records. The other two couplings below are stated as mechanisms and nothing more: this module writes no equation for either, so read them as directions a model could take, not results.

Three couplings dominate (Fig. 10). Weakness feeds bone loss: sarcopenic muscles pull less hard on bone, and the reduced load lowers the mechanostat stimulus of Section 7, so muscle loss accelerates bone loss - the two most consequential structural drifts are yoked. Slowness feeds inactivity: a slower, noisier balance controller makes movement feel precarious, so the person moves less, withdrawing the load that maintains muscle, bone, and the controller itself - a self-reinforcing spiral of the entire system. The fall is the pivot: a single fall (balance margin exceeded) becomes a fracture (bone margin exceeded), the fracture forces immobility, immobility deconditions muscle, bone, and balance together, and the deconditioning makes the next fall likelier - the well-documented downward spiral of a hip fracture, in which the mechanical cascade, not the bone break alone, drives the one-year mortality that follows it.

The coupling is also where intervention is most powerful, because a single stimulus enters at several nodes. Progressive loading builds muscle and bone and, through activity, the balance controller; a reactive-step programme cuts fall risk directly and keeps the person mobile enough to maintain everything else. The frailty cascade runs on withdrawn load; the intervention is reapplied load, and it acts on the whole coupled network at once. This is the module's final synthesis: aging is the coupled drift of a few mechanical parameters, injury is a reserve crossing zero, and the one lever that reaches the whole system - because it is the same lever that maintains it - is mechanical load, measured and dosed as this course has measured everything else.

Thirty problems - ten conceptual, ten derivational, ten computational - plus five diagnostics. Each carries a Probes note and a collapsible worked solution; computational solutions quote numbers the code produces.

Conceptual ↑ contents

C1 - the sudden cliff. Muscle and bone decline smoothly at about one percent a year, yet function seems to fail abruptly. Why? Probes: a reserve crossing one; Section 1.
Solution
Function is decided by a reserve $R=C/D$, and Module 13 showed several everyday tasks run with $R$ only slightly above one even in the young. A capacity $C$ falling smoothly still crosses the threshold $R=1$ in a single year, and on that year the task flips from possible to impossible - a smooth parametric drift produces an abrupt functional failure because the failure is a threshold crossing, not a proportional loss. The gradual decline is invisible until it reaches the edge of the reserve.
C2 - strength in the bank. Why is peak strength attained in youth protective against disability decades later? Probes: the starting reserve sets the failure age; Section 2.
Solution
The failure age is $a^\star=a_0+\frac1r(1-D/\tau_p)$: it rises with the starting reserve $\tau_p/D$. A person who peaks stronger begins the decline further above the demand, so the $1\%$/year drift takes more years to reach it. The gain is bounded: $a^\star-a_0=\frac1r(1-D/\tau_p)$ can never exceed $1/r=100$ years, and raising the starting reserve from $1$ to $3$ buys $66.7$ of them, with the return per further unit falling as $1/[r(\tau_p/D)^2]$. Strength built young is capacity banked against a decline that starts from that higher level, which is why lifelong activity, not just late-life exercise, protects function.
C3 - the squared penalty. Why does a $20\%$ loss of bone density cost far more than $20\%$ of bone strength? Probes: strength scales as density squared; Section 3.
Solution
Bone strength scales as roughly the square of apparent density, $S\propto\rho^2$, because both the quantity of bone and the thickness of the load-bearing trabeculae fall together. So a fractional density loss produces about twice that fractional strength loss ($\Delta S/S\approx2\,\Delta\rho/\rho$): a $20\%$ density drop leaves $0.8^2=0.64$ of strength, a $36\%$ loss. The squared law is why osteoporosis is dangerous out of proportion to the density change a scan reports - and, run the other way, why a small density gain from loading buys a squared strength return.
C4 - the fall that breaks. Why does a sideways fall fracture an old hip but only bruise a young one, when the impact is the same? Probes: the fracture margin; Section 3.
Solution
The fall generates a peak force $F=v\sqrt{m_{\rm eff}k}\approx4.4\ \mathrm{kN}$ that depends on the fall, not the bone. Fracture is the margin $S/F$ crossing one. A young femoral neck ($S\approx7\ \mathrm{kN}$) has margin $\gt 1$ - a bruise; osteoporosis drops $S$ toward $2.5\ \mathrm{kN}$, margin $\lt 1$ - a break. Same demand, different capacity: the identical impact fractures the weakened bone and not the strong one, which is why the intervention is either raising $S$ (bone loading) or lowering $F$ (hip protectors, softer landings, preventing the fall).
C5 - why it accelerates. Osteoarthritis, once it begins, speeds up rather than settling. Why? Probes: the load-damage positive feedback; Section 4.
Solution
OA is a positive feedback: thinner cartilage bears higher peak contact stress ($p\propto h^{-n}$), higher stress damages it faster, and the faster damage thins it further. Each turn of the loop raises the stress that drives the next, so thinning accelerates into a runaway rather than settling to an equilibrium - the mechanical signature of established OA. Because the loop is driven by joint load, reducing that load (weight, alignment) is what slows it; nothing about "wear" alone explains the acceleration - the feedback does.
C6 - the costly tenth-second. Why does a modest slowing of reaction time raise fall risk out of proportion? Probes: the delay enters exponentially; Section 5.
Solution
During the reaction delay the extrapolated centre of mass keeps diverging as $e^{\omega_0 t}$, so the recoverable perturbation shrinks by $e^{-\omega_0\Delta t}$ - an exponential penalty on delay. A perturbation that was catchable becomes uncatchable simply by the passage of the lag, and because the loss is exponential, each added tenth-second removes a fixed fraction of the envelope, $e^{-\omega_0\Delta t}$, however small the envelope has already become. That is the delay's distinctive property. It is not, at these parameter values, the largest single lever: the elasticity of $J_{\rm eff}$ with respect to $\Delta t$ is $-\omega_0\Delta t=-0.37$ against $+1.11$ for the balance margin (Section 6). Reactive-step training earns its place because the loss it removes is multiplicative and never saturates, not because it outranks strength.
C7 - noisy standing. Why does losing sensory acuity cost balance even when standing still, before any push? Probes: sensor noise consumes the margin; Section 5.
Solution
The balance controller acts on a noisy state estimate (Module 10); more sensor noise - fewer spindles, hair cells, photoreceptors - produces larger quiet-standing sway, so the extrapolated centre of mass already wanders a distance $c\sigma$ closer to the edge of the base on average. That wandering consumes part of the recoverable margin as a standing cost ($J_{\rm eff}\propto b-c\sigma$), leaving less to absorb an actual perturbation. Balance is lost not only by failing to react but by starting each reaction from closer to the edge.
C8 - the cascade. Why does a single deficit - or a single fall - so often begin a general decline into frailty? Probes: coupled drift; Section 8.
Solution
The parameters are coupled through load. Weakness reduces the load on bone, accelerating its loss; a precarious balance reduces activity, withdrawing the load that maintains muscle, bone, and the controller alike; and a fall becomes a fracture, which forces immobility, which deconditions everything and makes the next fall likelier. Each drift lowers the mechanostat stimulus that would have maintained the others, so a local deficit propagates through the whole coupled network - frailty is a system failure, not a sum of independent ones, which is why the mortality that follows a hip fracture is far higher than the fracture alone would explain.
C9 - reversing years. How can twelve weeks of resistance training "reverse two decades" of aging? Probes: the mechanostat run forward; Section 7.
Solution
Muscle follows a set-point rate law $dC/dt=k_{\rm ad}(\Sigma-\Sigma_0)$: below the maintenance stimulus it drifts down at $\sim1\%$/year, above it it builds. A training overload adds strength at $\sim1$-$2\%$ per session-week, so twelve weeks adds on the order of $20\%$, numerically undoing twenty years of the $1\%$/year drift in $\tau_{\max}$. The failure age moves less: $a^\star$ depends on $D/\tau_p$, so $\tau_p:175\to210\ \mathrm{N\,m}$ shifts the chair crossing from $77.1$ to $87.6$ years, a gain of $10.5$ years. Degeneration and adaptation are the same equation with opposite-sign forcing; the drift is reversible because the tissue is mechanoresponsive, and the "years reversed" is just the deficit divided by the loss rate.
C10 - protect versus load. Why is the right advice "load it" for muscle and bone but "protect it" for cartilage? Probes: the adaptation asymmetry; Section 7.
Solution
The mechanostat rate $k_{\rm ad}$ of Proposition 7.1 differs by orders of magnitude across tissues (the symbol $k$ without a subscript is the landing stiffness of Section 3). Muscle and bone have large $k_{\rm ad}$: they adapt fast and well, so a training overload rebuilds them and the message is reload. Cartilage has a tiny $k_{\rm ad}$ and no blood supply: it remodels slowly and heals poorly, so once damaged it cannot be trained back, and the message is protect - keep the load-damage loop from starting. The advice differs because the tissues' adaptive rates differ; loading that maintains muscle and bone would only accelerate a damaged joint's runaway.

Derivational ↑ contents

D1 - the failure age. Derive the age at which a declining strength reserve crosses one. Probes: solving $R=1$; Section 2.
Solution
With $\tau_{\max}(a)=\tau_p[1-r(a-a_0)]$ and $R=\tau_{\max}/D$, set $R=1$: $\tau_p[1-r(a^\star-a_0)]=D$, so $1-r(a^\star-a_0)=D/\tau_p$ and $a^\star=a_0+\frac1r(1-D/\tau_p)$ (Proposition 2.1).
D2 - the squared law. Derive the fractional strength change from a fractional density change for $S\propto\rho^n$. Probes: logarithmic differentiation; Section 3.
Solution
From $S=S_0(\rho/\rho_0)^{n_{\rm b}}$, $dS/d\rho=n_{\rm b}S/\rho$, so $dS/S=n_{\rm b}\,d\rho/\rho$: the fractional strength change is $n_{\rm b}$ times the fractional density change. For $n_{\rm b}=2$, a $20\%$ density loss gives $0.8^2=0.64$ of strength, a $36\%$ loss (Proposition 3.1).
D3 - the impact force. Derive the peak force of a mass striking a spring-like landing at speed $v$. Probes: energy conservation in impact; Section 3.
Solution
Kinetic energy converts to spring energy at maximum compression: $\tfrac12 m_{\rm eff}v^2=\tfrac12 k x_{\max}^2$, so $x_{\max}=v\sqrt{m_{\rm eff}/k}$; the peak force is $F=kx_{\max}=v\sqrt{m_{\rm eff}k}$, with $v=\sqrt{2gh_{\rm f}}$ from the fall (Proposition 3.2).
D4 - cartilage stress. Argue why peak contact stress rises as cartilage thins, and state the power-law model. Probes: load spread over a thickness-dependent area; Section 4.
Solution
A compliant layer spreads a fixed load over a contact area that grows with the layer's compliance, and compliance grows with thickness; a thinner layer gives a smaller area $A\propto h^{n_{\rm c}}$, and since $p_{\rm peak}\propto P/A$, $p_{\rm peak}=p_0(h_0/h)^{n_{\rm c}}$. Loss of interstitial fluid pressurisation shifts more load onto the solid matrix, raising the effective stress further (Proposition 4.1).
D5 - delay erosion. Derive the factor by which reaction delay shrinks the recoverable perturbation. Probes: XcoM divergence during the lag; Section 5.
Solution
The extrapolated COM grows as $\xi(t)=\xi_0 e^{\omega_0 t}$; the response, issued at $\Delta t$, succeeds iff $\xi(\Delta t)=\xi_0 e^{\omega_0\Delta t}\le\xi_c$, i.e. $\xi_0\le\xi_c e^{-\omega_0\Delta t}$. So the largest recoverable perturbation is scaled by $e^{-\omega_0\Delta t}$ relative to no delay (Proposition 5.1).
D6 - effective recoverable impulse. Assemble the fall-recovery capacity from margin, noise, and delay. Probes: composing independent factors; Section 6.
Solution
The young critical impulse is $m\omega_0 b$; sensor noise reduces the usable margin to $b-c\sigma$; the reaction delay scales the recoverable perturbation by $e^{-\omega_0\Delta t}$. Multiplying the independent factors gives $J_{\rm eff}=m\omega_0(b-c\sigma)e^{-\omega_0\Delta t}$, and recovery needs the encountered impulse $J\le J_{\rm eff}$ (Proposition 6.1).
D7 - the mechanostat. Show that a set-point rate law makes training and disuse the same equation, and find the recovery time. Probes: sign of the stimulus deficit; Section 7.
Solution
$dC/dt=k_{\rm ad}(\Sigma-\Sigma_0)$ has $dC/dt$ of the sign of $\Sigma-\Sigma_0$: steady at the setpoint, resorbing below it, building above. Under overload $\Sigma=\Sigma_0+\Delta$, $C$ grows linearly at $k_{\rm ad}\Delta$, so a deficit $\delta C$ is recovered in time $\delta C/(k_{\rm ad}\Delta)$ (Proposition 7.1).
D8 - critical density. Derive the bone density below which a given fall fractures the hip. Probes: solving $S=F$ for density; Section 3.
Solution
Fracture at $S=F$: $S_0(\rho/\rho_0)^2=F$, so $(\rho/\rho_0)_{\rm crit}=\sqrt{F/S_0}$. With $F=4385\ \mathrm{N}$, $S_0=7000\ \mathrm{N}$: $\sqrt{4385/7000}=0.79$ - a $21\%$ density loss suffices to fracture. Halving the landing stiffness cuts $F$ by $\sqrt2$, lowering the critical density to $0.67$.
D9 - demand shifts the age. Show how the failure age moves with the task demand, and interpret raising a chair. Probes: sensitivity of $a^\star$ to $D$; Section 2.
Solution
From $a^\star=a_0+\frac1r(1-D/\tau_p)$, $\partial a^\star/\partial D=-1/(r\tau_p)$: lowering the demand raises the failure age. For $r=0.01$, $\tau_p=175$: each $1\ \mathrm{N\,m}$ cut in demand adds $\approx0.57$ years. Raising a chair (smaller $d$, Module 13) lowers $D$ and buys years of independence exactly as raising capacity would - the demand side is a legitimate lever.
D10 - impact scaling. Show how the fall-impact force scales with fall height and landing stiffness, and what each implies. Probes: scaling of $F=\sqrt{2gh_{\rm f}}\sqrt{m_{\rm eff}k}$; Section 3.
Solution
$F=\sqrt{2gh_{\rm f}}\sqrt{m_{\rm eff}k}\propto\sqrt{h_{\rm f}}\,\sqrt{k}$. So $F\propto\sqrt{h_{\rm f}}$: a fall from a chair (twice the hip height) raises the force only by $\sqrt2$, not double. And $F\propto\sqrt k$: halving the landing stiffness (a hip protector, a compliant floor) cuts the force by the factor $1/\sqrt2$, a $29\%$ reduction. Both are sub-linear, which is why energy-absorbing floors and pads give real but bounded protection.

K3 - runaway or stable cartilage? (bifurcation + simulation). The loop of Lab C, $dh/dt=-\kappa Lp_0(h_0/h)^{n_{\rm c}}+\beta(h_0-h)$, has a steady state only for small enough load factor $L$. Solve $dh/dt=0$ for the load that holds each thickness steady, find the largest such load $L^\star$ and the thickness $h^\star$ where it occurs, and then integrate the loop at $L=0.1,\,0.2,\,0.4,\,0.6,\,1.0$ to confirm the prediction and to time the runaways. Solution

Setting $dh/dt=0$ gives the load that holds a thickness $h$ steady, $L_{\rm eq}(h)=\beta(h_0-h)/[\kappa p_0(h_0/h)^{n_{\rm c}}]$, which vanishes at both ends ($h=h_0$, where there is nothing to recover, and $h\to0$, where the stress diverges) and peaks in between. With the Lab C values $\kappa=\beta=0.010$, $p_0=3\ \mathrm{MPa}$, $h_0=2\ \mathrm{mm}$, $n_{\rm c}=0.7$, the peak is $L^\star=0.211$ at $h^\star=0.82\ \mathrm{mm}$. Below $L^\star$ there are two equilibria - a stable one above $h^\star$ and an unstable one below it - and a healthy joint starting at $h_0$ settles on the stable branch. Above $L^\star$ there is none, and the joint runs away. This is a saddle-node bifurcation in $L$.

The integrations confirm it and, more usefully, time it. At $L=0.10$ the layer is at $1.669\ \mathrm{mm}$ and at $L=0.20$ at $1.187\ \mathrm{mm}$ after $400$ years, both still maintained (the stable equilibria are $1.66$ and $1.07\ \mathrm{mm}$; the $L=0.20$ run is near the saddle-node and has not yet converged). At $L=0.40$, $0.60$ and $1.00$ - all above $L^\star$ - the layer is spent (taken as $h=0.30\ \mathrm{mm}$) after $153.5$, $84.0$ and $44.5$ years. Two lessons follow that the endpoints alone would hide. First, the threshold is far lower than the loads the joint actually sees: at $L^\star=0.211$ this model says a knee is maintained only under about a fifth of the reference load, which is a statement about how coarse the damage law is, not a clinical recommendation. Second, above the threshold the rate still varies by more than threefold across $L$, so unloading a joint that is already past $L^\star$ does not save it but can move the failure out of a lifetime.

import numpy as np

K5 - the coupled cascade (coupled simulation + intervention sweep). Integrate Proposition 8.1's pair - muscle drifting linearly at rate $r$, bone integrating its stimulus deficit at $k_{\rm ad}=0.03125\ \mathrm{yr^{-1}}$ - over forty years from age $40$, for $r=0.01$, $r=0.005$ (a training programme that halves the sarcopenic rate) and $r=0$ (muscle held). Report the muscle and bone capacities at forty years, convert bone to strength through $S\propto\rho^2$, and find the age at which bone crosses the critical density $0.79$ of Lab B. Probes: coupled drift; Section 8.
Solution

Proposition 8.1 gives $C_{\rm m}=1-rt$ and $C_{\rm b}=1-\tfrac12k_{\rm ad}rt^2$, so no integration error enters; the code below evaluates both. At the module's own $r=0.01\ \mathrm{yr^{-1}}$, forty years take muscle to $0.600$ and bone to $0.750$ of their young values - and bone strength, squared by Proposition 3.1, to $0.562$, a $44\%$ loss from a $25\%$ density loss. Bone reaches the fracture density $0.7915$ at $t=37$ years, i.e. age $77$, the same year the chair rise fails in Section 2: in this model the two thresholds arrive together, because both are driven by the one muscle drift.

The intervention sweep is the point. Halving the muscle rate to $r=0.005$ halves the bone deficit exactly (bone $0.875$ instead of $0.750$, strength $0.766$ instead of $0.562$) and pushes the fracture-density crossing past forty years - beyond age $80$. Holding the muscle at its young value ($r=0$) removes the bone drift entirely, because $\Sigma$ never leaves $\Sigma_0$. That is what no single-tissue model can show: bone loss here is not a separate disease with its own clock but the integral of the muscle deficit, so the earliest strength intervention buys two tissues at once, and letting muscle go quietly forfeits bone as well. The caution is the flip side: this couples only one pair, treats $k_{\rm ad}$ as constant, and ignores the direct hormonal loss of bone that continues even under load.

K6 - the training dose (inverse). Compute the time for a resistance-training overload to recover a given strength deficit, and the detraining time to lose it. Probes: inverting the mechanostat; Section 7.
Solution

From $dC/dt=k_{\rm ad}(\Sigma-\Sigma_0)$, a deficit $\delta C$ recovers in $t=\delta C/(k_{\rm ad}\Delta)$. At the $1.5\%$/week build rate of the parameter table a $20\%$ deficit closes in $13.3$ weeks; at the assumed $1\%$/week idle loss it is given back in $20.0$ weeks. (The $1\%$/week figure is measured on immobilised muscle and is used here as an upper bound on the loss during an untrained week; nothing in the module measures the untrained-but-ambulatory rate.)

Two divisions are not the interesting question. Take a duty cycle instead: let a fraction $f$ of weeks carry the training overload and $1-f$ carry none, so the net rate is $fk_{\rm build}-(1-f)k_{\rm lose}$. Holding ground needs $f=k_{\rm lose}/(k_{\rm build}+k_{\rm lose})=0.40$: two weeks in every five, spent merely to stay level. Gaining $20\%$ over a year needs $f=0.55$, which is $29$ training weeks in $52$; compressing the same gain into six months needs $f=0.71$, or $18$ weeks in $26$. The prescription that falls out is not a course of training but a floor on frequency, and it is a high floor: below two sessions-weeks in five the mechanostat runs backwards no matter how hard the training weeks are.

Take everyday perturbation impulses as exponentially distributed with mean $\bar J=8\ \mathrm{N\,s}$ - an assumption, chosen so that a young adult's per-event fall probability is small but not negligible - so that $P=\Pr(J\gt J_{\rm eff})=e^{-J_{\rm eff}/\bar J}$. Solution

        glc = glc_dia if age >= onset else glc_normo
        phi += dt * (k_AGE * glc * (1 - phi) - k_turn * phi)
    return phi

    dia = simulate(-np.inf, k_turn)
    p10 = simulate(70.0, k_turn)

Five diagnostics ↑ contents

  1. Reserve at the limit. What does $R=1$ mean physically?
    Solution
    Capacity exactly equals demand: the task is just possible, with no margin for error, fatigue, or a slightly harder instance - the edge of function.
  2. Squared strength. A scan reports a $10\%$ bone-density loss. Roughly what strength loss?
    Solution
    About $20\%$ ($\Delta S/S\approx2\Delta\rho/\rho$), or exactly $1-0.9^2=19\%$.
  3. Impact and height. Does a fall from twice the height double the impact force?
    Solution
    No - $F\propto\sqrt h$, so twice the height raises the force by only $\sqrt2\approx1.4\times$.
  4. Delay units. Why is the delay penalty $e^{-\omega_0\Delta t}$ dimensionless and always $\le1$?
    Solution
    $\omega_0\Delta t$ is (rad/s)(s), dimensionless; for $\Delta t\ge0$ the exponent is $\le0$, so the factor is $\le1$ - a delay can only shrink the recoverable envelope.
  5. Adaptation sign. In $dC/dt=k_{\rm ad}(\Sigma-\Sigma_0)$, what does $\Sigma\lt \Sigma_0$ produce?
    Solution
    Resorption - the capacity drifts down (disuse), because the rate is negative.
